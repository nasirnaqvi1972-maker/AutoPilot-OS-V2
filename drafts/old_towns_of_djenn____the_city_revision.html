    Chronology & Settlement: The Old Towns of Djenné feature a continuous human presence dating back to approximately 250 B.C. (3rd century B.C.), confirmed by archaeological excavations conducted in 1977, 1981, 1996, and 1997. Serial Property Composition: The UNESCO cultural property consists of four distinct archaeological mounds—Djenné-Djeno, Hambarkétolo, Kaniana, and Tonomba—alongside the historic fabric of the modern town of Djenné, covering 48.5 hectares across ten districts. Economic & Global Significance: Historically, Djenné operated as a premier market hub and a critical node in the trans-Saharan gold trade network, connecting sub-Saharan resources with broader continental trade routes. Religious Evolution: The site represents a profound transition from a pre-Islamic urban civilization (evidenced by early dwellings, funerary jars, and artifacts in the surrounding mounds) to a major center for the propagation of Islam during the 15th and 16th centuries. Architectural Tradition: Traditional domestic architecture utilizes earthen construction, characterized by verticality, exterior buttresses, pilasters, decorative entry points ("potige"), and placement on hillocks (*toguere*) to withstand seasonal Niger inland delta floods. Construction techniques are passed down through the hereditary *Barey* mason corporation. Monumental Landmarks: The Great Mosque serves as the definitive masterpiece of local Sudanese-Sahelian religious earthen architecture, reflecting historical layers including Moroccan influences (post-1591), Toucouleur dominion (1862), and its major structural reconstruction in 1906–1907.

4. Draft Article

Few landscapes on the African continent encapsulate the intricate dialogue between human ingenuity and environmental adaptation as profoundly as the Old Towns of Djenné. Situated in the inland delta of the Niger River in Mali, this serial property is far more than a collection of picturesque mud buildings; it represents over two millennia of continuous urban development, trans-Saharan commercial enterprise, and architectural mastery. For students of global history, Djenné stands as a vital pillar demonstrating how ancient sub-Saharan societies engineered complex urban frameworks long before external colonial or continental interventions reshaped the region.

Archaeological investigations—most notably campaigns in 1977, 1981, 1996, and 1997—pushed the historical horizon of the site back to 250 B.C. The UNESCO-designated property comprises not only the historic fabric of the living city, which spans 48.5 hectares divided into ten districts, but also four foundational archaeological mounds: Djenné-Djeno, Hambarkétolo, Kaniana, and Tonomba. These mounds preserve the material culture of a thriving pre-Islamic civilization. Excavations have yielded circular and rectangular structures built with traditional round mud bricks known as djénné ferey, alongside funerary jars, rich terra-cotta statuary, millstones, grinders, and metal scoria. These findings reveal an advanced, self-sustaining society characterized by sophisticated metallurgy, craft specialization, and dense settlement patterns designed around the ecological rhythms of the Niger Delta floodplains.

As centuries progressed, Djenné transformed from a localized riverine and agrarian network into a premier market hub. Its geographical position made it an indispensable link in the trans-Saharan gold trade, channeling West African gold northward toward Mediterranean and Eurasian markets. This economic vitality paved the way for profound cultural and religious shifts. During the 15th and 16th centuries, Djenné evolved into one of West Africa’s most illustrious centers for the propagation of Islam, functioning alongside Timbuktu as a beacon of Islamic scholarship, piety, and trade. The city's identity as the pious "town of saints" took root during this era, fortified by the establishment of Koranic schools and sacred tombs.

Architecturally, Djenné is globally renowned for its intensive and monumental use of earth. To combat the severe seasonal floods of the Niger inland delta, early and medieval inhabitants deliberately constructed their dwellings upon elevated hillocks, or toguere. The historic domestic architecture of the town—represented by nearly 2,000 surviving traditional houses—is celebrated for its rigorous composition, distinct verticality, and exterior buttress systems. Façades are frequently punctuated by ornamental pilasters and intricate entry porches, highlighted by the central decorative motif known as the potige, which marks the position of the front door. This living architectural tradition is sustained through generations by the Barey corporation, a hereditary guild of masons who safeguard the specialized techniques required to shape and maintain earthen structures.

At the center of this architectural tradition stands the Great Mosque, a masterpiece of Sudanese-Sahelian religious architecture. While its stylistic lineage bears historical scars and influences from Moroccan incursions in 1591 and Toucouleur imperial rule in 1862, the current monumental structure—rebuilt between 1906 and 1907—remains an enduring symbol of local religious and artistic expression. The mosque, together with the surrounding historic fabric, continues to embody the Outstanding Universal Value recognized by its World Heritage listing.

Despite its rich heritage, Djenné faces contemporary pressures that threaten its physical and cultural authenticity. The archaeological mounds and urban fabric remain vulnerable to environmental degradation, including rainwater leaching, erosion, and gullying. Furthermore, rapid population growth, building speculation, and the introduction of modern, non-traditional construction materials—such as cement, fired bricks, and metal doors—pose severe risks to the aesthetic integrity of the earthen landscape. Safeguarding this irreplaceable chapter of human history relies heavily on the active management of local cultural missions, community-led conservation plans, and the enduring dedication of the Barey masons to protect a heritage carved directly from the earth.
