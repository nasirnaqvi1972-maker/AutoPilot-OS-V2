1. Research Findings

Based on the UNESCO documentation for Sangay National Park, the site spans a vast altitudinal range from 900 to 5,319 meters above sea level in the Ecuadorian Andes. Established as a National Park in 1979 and designated a UNESCO World Heritage site in 1983, the park encompasses approximately 270,000 to 502,105 hectares depending on historical administrative extensions and core inscribed boundaries. The park is characterized by three major stratovolcanoes—Tungurahua (5,016m), Sangay (5,230m), and Altar (5,319m)—with Sangay noted globally for its continuous volcanic activity. The region supports a vertical spectrum of ecosystems ranging from tropical Amazonian rainforests and cloud forests to high-altitude moorlands (páramos) and glacial fields, housing threatened endemic species such as the mountain tapir (Tapirus pinchaque) and the spectacled bear (Tremarctos ornatus).

4. Draft Article

Introduction and Geographical Context

Situated in the heart of the Ecuadorian Andes, Sangay National Park represents one of the most ecologically diverse and topographically dramatic protected areas in South America. Encompassing an altitudinal gradient that surges from 900 meters to 5,319 meters above sea level, the park bridges tropical Amazonian lowlands and high-altitude glacial domains. According to UNESCO evaluations, this vertical compression of ecosystems creates rare environmental transitions, embedding cloud forests, extensive wetland systems containing 327 lakes, and vast expanses of montane grasslands known as páramos within a contiguous natural corridor.

Geological Significance and Volcanism

The landscape of Sangay is profoundly shaped by tectonic activity and continuous volcanism. The park features three prominent stratovolcanoes: Tungurahua, Altar, and Sangay. Among these, the symmetrically cone-shaped Sangay volcano is globally distinguished by its prolonged and relentless period of activity. Historical and geological evidence indicates that continuous volcanic ash deposition acts as a primary driver of ecological succession. Volcanic emissions enrich high-altitude soils, generating fertile micro-habitats that facilitate rapid plant colonization, particularly among specialized bunch grasses and cushion plants adapted to extreme weather and high-efficiency water capture.

Historiography of Conservation and Institutional Milestones

The formal protection of the region evolved through distinct administrative phases during the late 20th century. The Ecuadorian government designated the territory as a National Park in 1979 to curb unregulated resource extraction and preserve its unique biotic assemblages. Shortly thereafter, the international significance of the region was codified when it was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1983 under criteria recognizing its superlative natural phenomena and complex ecological habitats. To accommodate ecological corridors, the park boundaries were expanded southward in 1992, enlarging the total administrative zone to over 500,000 hectares, although the formally recognized World Heritage property boundary remained at 271,925 hectares.

Infrastructure Conflicts and the "In Danger" Designation

The historical trajectory of Sangay National Park includes significant ecological crises, most notably associated with infrastructure development. In 1992, the park was inscribed on the List of World Heritage in Danger. This administrative action was triggered primarily by the construction of the Guamote-Macas road, which bisected the protected zone, physically separating the core World Heritage site from its southern extension. The construction introduced severe anthropogenic stressors, including chemical contamination of the Upano River and adjacent lakes, the destructive use of dynamite, localized microclimate alterations, and indirect consequences such as illegal deforestation, cattle ranching, and poaching.

Responding to these compounding threats, conservation authorities implemented rigorous management interventions. A comprehensive executive management plan was approved in 2005, introducing strategic guidelines for ecological restoration, participatory community management, and heightened regulatory monitoring by the Ecuadorian Ministry of Environment. These corrective measures successfully mitigated infrastructural impacts and land-use conflicts, leading to the removal of Sangay from the List of World Heritage in Danger in 2005.

Biodiversity and Modern Preservation Challenges

Today, natural vegetation covers approximately 84.5 percent of the park, maintaining the least disturbed assemblage of native species in the region. The park serves as a critical biological sanctuary for high-profile endangered and vulnerable species, functioning as one of the last remaining refuges for the mountain tapir (Tapirus pinchaque) and supporting substantial populations of the spectacled bear (Tremarctos ornatus), the Andean condor, and the giant anteater. Despite robust constitutional frameworks—such as Ecuador's 2008 constitutional recognition of state responsibility for biodiversity—ongoing pressures from agricultural encroachment, livestock grazing along lower subtropical boundaries, and illegal hunting require constant administrative vigilance.
