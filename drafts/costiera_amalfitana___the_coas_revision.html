1. Research Findings

The Costiera Amalfitana (Amalfi Coast) encompasses 11,231 hectares across 15 municipalities on the southern slopes of the Sorrentine Peninsula in Salerno province, Campania, Italy. Archaeological and historical findings establish the following chronological framework and material evidence:

  • Prehistoric Period: Human presence dates back to the Palaeolithic and Mesolithic eras, confirmed by artifact remains discovered at Positano.
  • Roman Era: The region integrated into the Roman administrative apparatus as a colony during the 4th century CE.
  • Early Middle Ages to Maritime Republic Era (9th–11th Centuries): Intensive settlement expanded significantly following the early medieval period. The region achieved its zenith as the Amalfi Sea Republic, an autonomous maritime power whose geopolitical reach and Mediterranean trade networks facilitated an intense cultural synthesis. Architectural and artistic outputs from this era frequently display an "Arabic-Norman" stylistic fusion, exhibiting morphological similarities to eastern urban forms, such as narrow streets and stepped alleyways reminiscent of Levantine souks.
  • Agricultural and Topographical Adaptation: The Lattari Mountains form a natural barrier separating the Gulf of Naples from the Gulf of Salerno. Human adaptation to this vertical, high-gradient topography resulted in extensive landscape engineering, notably drystone-walled terracing (*macere*) utilized for viticulture, olive groves, and citrus orchards on lower slopes, paired with pastoralism and sheep-farming in upland plateaus.

2. Evidence Gaps

While the UNESCO documentation and national legislative inventories (such as the *Codice dei beni culturali e del paesaggio*, Legislative Decree No. 42/2004) provide robust administrative and architectural data for the medieval and modern periods, specific archaeological gaps remain:

  • The precise socio-economic mechanisms and material culture of the transition phase from late Roman villa economies to early medieval monastic and communal land-holding patterns are under-represented in the surface-level architectural surveys.
  • Detailed chronological sequences regarding the genesis and phasing of early terracing systems (*fondi*) lack absolute dating (such as high-resolution OSL or radiocarbon assays on buried soils), leaving the exact medieval versus post-medieval introduction of specific drystone agricultural terracing open to localized debate.

4. Draft Article

Geographies of Verticality: Archaeology, Trade, and Landscape Evolution in the Costiera Amalfitana

Stretching along the southern flank of the Sorrentine Peninsula between the Gulf of Naples and the Gulf of Salerno, the Costiera Amalfitana represents one of the Mediterranean's most intensely negotiated human landscapes. Far from being a pristine natural amenity, this 11,231-hectare tract of sheer limestone cliffs, deep ravines, and marine terraces is an anthropogenic archive. For readers examining global and comparative maritime history—particularly scholars familiar with the littoral economies of South and West Asia—the Amalfi Coast offers a striking European parallel to societies that successfully converted extreme, high-gradient topographies into flourishing maritime and agricultural hubs.

Archaeological investigations across the 15 municipalities comprising the UNESCO World Heritage property demonstrate a deep chronology of human occupation. Excavations at Positano have yielded pivotal Palaeolithic and Mesolithic material remains, proving that hunter-gatherer and foraging groups exploited the coastal rock shelters long before the advent of complex Mediterranean state systems. By the 4th century CE, this rugged terrain was formally incorporated into the Roman administrative orbit as a colonial territory, setting the stage for the infrastructure of roads and maritime outposts that would later characterize the region's connectivity.

The defining historical trajectory of the Costiera, however, crystallized during the early Middle Ages. Between the 9th and 11th centuries, Amalfi emerged as a formidable autonomous Maritime Republic (*Repubblica Marinara*). Positioned at the crossroads of Byzantine, Lombard, and Islamic Mediterranean trade networks, the merchants of Amalfi projected commercial power far beyond the Italian peninsula. This geopolitical integration left an indelible material imprint on the urban topography of settlements such as Amalfi, Atrani, Ravello, and Scala. Architectural historians identify this era through the emergence of the "Arabic-Norman" style—a distinctive structural and decorative synthesis that fused western masonry traditions with eastern motifs. Furthermore, the claustrophobic, organic urban layouts of these coastal towns, characterized by arterial stairways and subterranean vaulted passages (*ogival or vaulted corridors*), bear functional and morphological resemblances to the urban fabric of eastern commercial centres and souks.

Equally significant is the vernacular archaeology of the rural zones, which illustrates an extraordinary paradigm of human adaptation to environmental constraints. The Lattari Mountains, extending from the Picentini chain to the Tyrrhenian Sea, create an abrupt climatic and physical barrier. To render this precipitous topography productive, generations of inhabitants engineered a terraced agricultural system. Supported by monumental drystone walls (*macere*), these terraces transformed steep, erosion-prone slopes into productive plots for viticulture, olive arboriculture, and citrus cultivation. This vertical land-use model divided the landscape into distinct ecological tiers: intensive horticultural terraces hugging the sea level, mid-slope woodlands and maquis, and wide upland pastures dedicated to traditional sheep-farming.

Today, the safeguarding of the Costiera Amalfitana requires navigating complex administrative and ecological challenges. Modern legal protections—primarily anchored by the Italian Code of Cultural Heritage and Landscape (*Decreto Legislativo 42/2004*), regional Urban Territorial Plans, and the management structures of the Parco Regionale dei Monti Lattari—strive to reconcile heritage conservation with intense anthropogenic pressures. Vulnerable to hydrogeological hazards such as landslides and earthquakes, alongside the strains of vehicular congestion and mass tourism, the Amalfi Coast remains a living laboratory where ancient infrastructural resilience meets contemporary heritage management.
