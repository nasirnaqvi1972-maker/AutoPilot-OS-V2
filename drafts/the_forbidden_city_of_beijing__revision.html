1. Research Findings

The Forbidden City in Beijing, functioning alongside the imperial complex in Shenyang, constitutes the primary architectural and material archive of supreme state power in late feudal China under the Ming and Qing dynasties (UNESCO/CPE). Constructed between 1406 and 1420 under the direction of the Ming emperor Zhu Di, the Beijing complex served as the seat of governance and the residential core for 14 Ming emperors and 10 Qing emperors across a span of 505 years (UNESCO/CPE). Archaeological and structural evidence reveals a master-planned spatial layout characterized by a strict central axis, bilateral symmetry, the functional division of an outer court (for state affairs) at the front and an inner court (for domestic life) at the rear, and integrated landscaped courtyards inherited from antecedent Yuan dynasty urban planning traditions (UNESCO/CPE). Furthermore, the spatial organization of the Shenyang Palace—built between 1625 and 1637 by Nurgaci—reflects the distinct socio-military organization of the Manchu people, specifically the “eight-banner” system, alongside architectural syntheses incorporating Han, Mongolian, and Tibetan cultural elements (UNESCO/CPE).

2. Evidence Gaps

While official architectural surveys and archival inventories are exceptionally comprehensive regarding elite court culture, ritual structures, and official engineering techniques, the source material and site data present distinct gaps concerning the daily lived experiences, labor contributions, and subaltern histories of the non-elite artisans, builders, and lower-ranking service staff who constructed and maintained these vast complexes. Additionally, while the physical integration of Manchu shamanistic practices (such as the sacrificial spaces within the Qingning Palace) is well-documented, the exact diachronic evolution of these northern ritual forms under the pressure of prolonged Sinicization within the palace precincts remains a point requiring deeper archaeological and archival reconciliation.

4. Draft Article

Architectural Legacies of Supreme Power: The Forbidden City and the Material Culture of the Ming and Qing Dynasties

For more than five centuries, the Forbidden City in the heart of Beijing stood as the undisputed epicenter of political, ritual, and cosmological power in late imperial China. Functioning as the royal residence and seat of government from the completion of its primary construction phase under the Ming emperor Zhu Di in 1420 until the abdication of the Qing dynasty, the complex witnessed the reigns of 14 Ming and 10 Qing emperors (UNESCO/CPE). Far more than a mere collection of palaces, this sprawling architectural ensemble—complemented structurally and historically by the Qing ancestral palace complex in Shenyang—offers an invaluable material archive of East Asian statecraft, ritual architecture, and inter-ethnic synthesis.

The spatial layout of the Forbidden City represents the apex of ancient Chinese urban planning and palace construction. Archaeological and historical analyses demonstrate that the complex was deliberately arranged along a rigid north-south central axis, employing strict bilateral symmetry to visually reinforce the absolute authority of the Son of Heaven. Architecturally, the complex adheres to a functional dichotomy: the outer court, dominated by grand ceremonial halls where affairs of state were conducted, sits at the front, while the inner court, dedicated to the private domestic and ritual lives of the imperial family, occupies the rear (UNESCO/CPE). This rigorous spatial hierarchy absorbed and adapted earlier urban traditions, notably incorporating landscaped courtyards derived from the preceding Yuan dynasty layout, thereby anchoring dynastic legitimacy in a physical continuum of past imperial rule.

Crucial to understanding the global and regional significance of these palaces is their role as sites of cross-cultural exchange. While the Beijing complex showcases the zenith of classical Han official architecture, the imperial palace in Shenyang—constructed between 1625 and 1637 by Nurgaci for the Nuzhen and Manchu forebears of the Qing dynasty—provides vital physical evidence of northern traditions prior to the Manchu expansion into China proper (UNESCO/CPE). The layout of the Shenyang complex uniquely mirrors the "eight-banner" social and military organization of Manchu society (UNESCO/CPE). Furthermore, within both complexes, structural features such as royal Buddhist chambers and the Shamanistic sacrificial spaces preserved in Shenyang's Qingning Palace testify to a profound architectural and cultural integration among Manchu, Han, Mongolian, and Tibetan populations from the 14th century onward (UNESCO/CPE).

The historical value of the Forbidden City is further amplified by its contents. The complex houses nearly 10,000 rooms containing over a million precious objects, including imperial utility items, fine works of art, and priceless archival materials consisting of written records, architectural drawings, and physical models of ancient engineering techniques (UNESCO/CPE). These artifacts provide scholars with direct empirical evidence of court law, administrative regulations, and the sophisticated construction technologies that sustained wooden official architecture over centuries. Following the collapse of the Qing dynasty, the institutional transformation of these imperial sites into the Palace Museums in Beijing (1925) and Shenyang (1926) marked a critical turning point, shifting these once-forbidden precincts into public repositories of global cultural heritage protected by rigorous modern conservation legislation (UNESCO/CPE).
