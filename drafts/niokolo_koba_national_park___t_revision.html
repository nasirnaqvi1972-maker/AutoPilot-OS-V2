4. Draft Article

Landscapes of the Gambia River Basin: The Environmental and Conservation History of Niokolo-Koba National Park

Spanning an expansive area of approximately 913,000 to nearly one million hectares, Niokolo-Koba National Park represents a vital environmental archive of the Sudano-Guinean bioclimatic zone (UNESCO/CPE; Brief synthesis). Situated along the well-watered floodplains of the Gambia River and its tributaries—including the Sereko, Niokolo, and Koulountou—the park preserves a complex matrix of ecosystems that have historically defined human and animal interaction in the West African savanna belt.

From an ecological and geographical perspective, the park's historical significance lies in its botanical diversity. Comprising over 1,500 identified plant species, the landscape transitions dynamically from dense gallery forests along watercourses to herbaceous savanna floodplains, dry forests, rocky outcroppings, and barren iron-crusted plateaus known as Bowés (Brief synthesis). This varied topography historically provided resilient ecological refugia during regional climatic fluctuations.

The convergence of these distinct habitats supports a remarkably rich fauna, which underpins the property's designation under UNESCO Criterion (x). The park shelters more than 70 mammal species, 329 bird species, 36 reptiles, and 20 amphibians (Brief synthesis). Among its most notable zoological assets are the Derby eland—recognized globally as the largest living antelope species—alongside chimpanzees, leopards, African wild dogs, a significant elephant population, and lions celebrated regionally for their exceptional physical size (Brief synthesis).

Despite its natural abundance, the park's modern trajectory reflects profound conservation and management complexities. Administered directly by the State through the Ministry of Environment and Nature Protection and the National Parks Directorate, the protected area operates under a foundational management plan established in 2002 (Protection and management requirements). However, historical and contemporary pressures have severely tested the integrity of these frameworks.

In 2007, mounting environmental threats prompted the international community to inscribe Niokolo-Koba on the List of World Heritage in Danger (Protection and management requirements). Scholarly and conservation reports highlight multiple compounding vulnerabilities: rampant elephant poaching, the premature drying and vegetative choking of seasonal water ponds, and the ecological disruption threatened by proposed dam construction projects along the Gambia and Niokolo-Koba rivers (Integrity; Protection and management requirements). Furthermore, regional population growth and degraded peripheral soils have accelerated agricultural encroachment and the unauthorized wandering of livestock into protected zones, necessitating urgent cross-border cooperation, enhanced ecological monitoring, and strengthened buffer-zone protections (Protection and management requirements).
