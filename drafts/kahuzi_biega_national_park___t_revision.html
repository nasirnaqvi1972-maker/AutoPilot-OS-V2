Situated at the volatile intersection of the Albertine Rift and the Congo Basin, Kahuzi-Biega National Park represents one of the most ecologically rich yet historically pressured landscapes in Central Africa. Spanning over 600,000 hectares, the park is anchored by two towering extinct volcanoes—Mount Kahuzi (3,308 meters) and Mount Biega (2,790 meters)—whose dramatic elevations dictate a rare ecological transition zone. As documented in international registry assessments, the park preserves a continuous vegetal sweep from dense lowland rainforests up to sub-alpine prairies and heather communities above 2,600 meters.

Ecological Zonation and Biodiversity

The geographical morphology of Kahuzi-Biega allows for an unbroken continuity of flora and fauna across varying altitudes. A vital ecological corridor links a 60,000-hectare highland sector to a vast 540,000-hectare lowland expanse. Within this mosaic, researchers have cataloged an extraordinary breadth of life. According to biological surveys, including a comprehensive 2003 inventory by the Wildlife Conservation Society, the park supports 349 bird species—including 42 endemics—and harbors greater mammalian diversity than any other site in the Albertine Rift, protecting 136 distinct mammal species.

Among these are Central African forest dwellers such as the bush elephant, bush buffalo, hylochere, and bongo, alongside rare carnivores like the giant forest genet (Genetta victoriae) and the aquatic genet (Genetta piscivora). Furthermore, botanical assessments conducted by the IUCN and WWF in 1994 established the highland zone as a designated center of plant diversity, identifying at least 1,178 flora species, including the endemic highland senecio (Senecio kahuzicus).

The Stronghold of the Eastern Lowland Gorilla

Historically and ecologically, the preeminent significance of Kahuzi-Biega lies in its role as a sanctuary for the eastern lowland gorilla (Gorilla beringei graueri). Endemic to the eastern forests of the Democratic Republic of the Congo, this endangered sub-species relies heavily on the park's multi-tiered habitats. Early foundational survey data cited historical localized populations of approximately 250 individuals inhabiting the mist-shrouded elevations between 2,100 and 2,400 meters. Alongside the gorilla, the park protects thirteen other primate taxa, including chimpanzees, the colobus bai, and threatened guenons such as Hoest's and Hamlyn's monkeys.

Management, Land Tenure, and Historical Conflicts

The institutional history of Kahuzi-Biega reveals deep structural tensions between state-led conservation frameworks and local human populations. Managed by the Congolese Institute for Nature Conservation (ICCN), the park's administrative integrity has faced compounding historical challenges. When the park was expanded in 1975, several pre-existing human settlements were enclosed within its official boundaries. This administrative incorporation generated lingering land-use disputes that continue to impact community relations and conservation efficacy.

Compounding these socio-political friction points, regional instability has driven the displacement of thousands of people, placing severe anthropogenic stress on the park's resources. Under-staffed guard forces, logistical deficits, and historical funding shortages have historically left extensive tracts of the lowland sector unmonitored. Consequently, the park has confronted persistent threats from commercial and subsistence poaching, bush meat harvesting, and artisanal oil and mineral exploration. Addressing these interconnected crises—by resolving boundary demarcation disputes, controlling transit along the national highway that bisects the highland sector, and securing sustainable trust funding—remains paramount to preserving the irreplaceable heritage of the Albertine Rift.
