Outstanding Universal Value
Brief synthesis
The ancient city of Sabratha, located in present-day Libya, was originally a Phoenician trading post (emporia) at the end of the fifth century BCE, the latest of the three trading posts founded by the Phoenicians in Tripolitania (a century after Leptis Magna). Thanks to its strategic location on the Mediterranean coast and its small natural harbour, fairly well sheltered from the westerly winds, it played a crucial role in the export of olive oil and exotic products from Africa to the Mediterranean.
Excavations carried out by archaeologists have uncovered traces of the earliest settlements of the Phoenician village under the Roman city.
The urban network is characterized by an irregular plan that has survived in part despite the later reconstruction. A tophet, i.e. a Punic cemetery, and numerous stelae dedicated to the cult of the goddess Tanit have been discovered. Under the influence of Carthage, the first stone buildings appeared in the second half of the fourth century BCE. A significant phase of expansion and renovation took place in the second century BCE, probably reflecting growing economic independence as Carthage was in conflict with Rome. After the defeat of Carthage and the fall of the Numidian kingdom, Sabratha was integrated into the Roman province of New Africa in 46 BCE and began to transform. In the first century CE, an earthquake accelerated the revival of urban planning.
The nucleus of the original imperial city is concentrated around the forum, with the temple of Liber Pater, the temple of Serapis, and the basilica. The port is reinforced by an artificial pier. Urbanization became more complex with an orthonormal plan replacing the anarchic plan left by the Phoenicians. The stone used for the buildings is a very friable local limestone, which explains the relative deterioration of the monuments and the problems of conservation.
Under the last Antonines, Sabratha experienced an intense period of economic activity and a demographic increase (up to 30,000 inhabitants) in the second and third centuries CE, its surface area doubled, new public buildings made of imported marble were built and the older buildings were renovated. Having become a colony in 157, the city developed a new urban district along the sea, to the east of the existing city. A theatre, the archaeological jewel of Sabratha, two temples dedicated to Hercules and Isis, and an amphitheatre were built there. The heart of the city was refurbished with public baths and was renewed with the enlarged temple of Liber Pater, a new temple dedicated to Marcus Aurelius and Lucius Verus, a curia, and a capitol erected on the other side of the forum with a basilica to the south. The period was resolutely focused on trade with Rome, through the oases of Ghadames and Ghat.
Christianized quickly, Sabratha even became a bishopric in 253. However, four earthquakes, including that of 365 CE, precipitated its decline, and its vulnerability was increased by successive Vandal invasions in the fifth century. In 533 CE, the Byzantine Empire, under Emperor Justinian, reconquered North Africa, and Sabratha was reborn, recovered, and showed enough vitality to experience a new but last intense building activity with new ramparts encircling only a fraction of the city's former area. Most of the churches date from this period, such as the new church, "worthy of a great reputation" mentioned by Procopius of Caesarea with exceptional mosaics. Despite the brief Byzantine revival in the sixth century, Sabratha's importance continued to decline until the Arab conquest of Amr ibn el-Aas in 643, when the city died out and its trade moved to Tripoli in 748.
The excavations and rediscovery of Sabratha began in 1921, mainly under the impetus of Italian teams. British interventions also took place between 1948 and 1951. This archaeological work has been essential to uncover the rich history of the city, from its Phoenician origins to its Roman and Byzantine phases, thus reconstructing the narrative of a city that once shone bright white thanks to its stucco-covered sandstone buildings.
The remains of the ancient city, whose destiny is resolutely linked to maritime trade, including the forum, the baths, the temples, and the residential areas, are remarkably well preserved. They offer an eloquent example of Roman urban planning and infrastructure on the North African coast and are an exceptional testimony to the Romanization of a Punic city. Their spectacular and remarkably well-preserved Roman monuments bear witness to this, in particular, its theatre, one of the largest in Africa dating from the Severian period, and particularly remarkable for its architectural decoration, with three floors with exedras over a height of nearly 25 m, which has been the subject of a remarkable and exhaustive reconstruction. The complex history of Sabratha, of Carthaginian origin and then modelled on the Roman model, is reflected in its archaeological remains, which reveal a fascinating mix of Punic, Roman and early Christian influences.
Criterion (iii): The remains of the ancient city, including the forum, baths, temples and Basilicas (including the temples of Liber Pater, Serapis, Isis, and the Basilica of Justinian), in addition to the residential areas, are well-preserved. They provide a clear example of Roman urban planning and infrastructure on the North African coast, and stand as an exceptional testimony to the Romanization of a Punic city and for its spectacular and well-preserved Roman monuments, particularly its theatre, which is one of the best-preserved Roman theatres in the world and among the largest in North Africa. The layered history of Sabratha is evident in its archaeological remains, which show a fascinating blend of Punic, Roman, and early Christian influences.
Integrity
The architectural heritage of Sabratha is very extensive and most of its principal elements are largely intact. The monuments and archaeological finds of the ancient city represent all the attributes expressed in the Outstanding Universal Value within its designated area.
Authenticity
Sabratha has maintained a high level of authenticity, and the city has deservedly earned a reputation of having the most complete and impressive Roman Theatre in North Africa. The authenticity of the individual components and the ancient urban fabric as a whole is very high. The structures brought to light by excavations carried out in the first half of the twentieth century have been the object of consolidation, restoration, and maintenance operations and, to a lesser extent, reconstruction, in full respect of the original morphology of the elements involved.
Protection and management requirements
At the time of inscription, the city, including all of its individual monuments and archaeological sites, was legally protected and assured by the authority of the Department of Antiquities (DoA) and the local community of Sabratha through the provisions of Law No. 40/1968 governing the administration of antiquities. This was later replaced by Law No. 3/1994 and its executive regulations/1995 issued by the General People’s Congress.
The archaeological site of Sabratha, at the time of inscription, was managed by the superintendent of the Sabratha region/municipality. Since 2019, the DoA has established a local office overseeing the management and protection of the property, as well as the museums (the Punic Museum and Roman Museum).
Effective protection is guaranteed through collaboration between the local authority, the development partners and the Department of Antiquities, the Urban Planning Department, local City Council, and the Tourism and Antiquities Police. The law cited has, so far, guaranteed the preservation of the property.
The property faces significant environmental pressures. Vegetation encroachment is a major factor that needs constant monitoring and intervention. Furthermore, the site is threatened by coastal erosion, and the vulnerability of its calcarenite stone to weathering from marine spray and natural degradation underscores the urgent need for its long-term preservation.
A buffer zone that will limit urban development in the vicinity of the site of Sabratha and ensure that the neighbouring area is also conserved has been outlined and is under preparation.
