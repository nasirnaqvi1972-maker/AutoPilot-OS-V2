Castles and Town Walls of King Edward in Gwynedd — The Iron Ring of Wales

A quartet of fortresses and their walled towns that turned conquest into architecture

📍 Location: Gwynedd, North Wales, United Kingdom — comprising Beaumaris Castle (Anglesey), Caernarfon Castle and Town Walls, Conwy Castle and Town Walls, and Harlech Castle (Merionethshire)
📅 Period: 1277–1330 (principal construction); medieval through modern eras
🏷️ Category: Cultural
⚠️ Status: UNESCO World Heritage Site (inscribed 1986)
📏 Significance: The most complete and impressive surviving group of military architecture from the reign of Edward I; a masterclass in 13th-century castle-building by master mason James of St George; the finest surviving medieval town walls in Britain; a physical embodiment of Plantagenet imperial ambition in Wales

The Shadow the Mason Left

The tide creeps up the Menai Strait and the stone turns the colour of wet slate. From the quay at Caernarfon you can see the whole grammar of the thing at once: a castle that does not sit politely on the landscape but forces it into shape, a walled town behind it, and beyond that the mountains where the Welsh princes once held their ground. The rain arrives sideways, as it usually does. The towers do not flinch. They were built by men who expected worse.

This is the paradox at the heart of the Castles and Town Walls of King Edward in Gwynedd. These are not ruins in the romantic sense — broken arches, ivy, the melancholy of collapse. They are among the best-preserved medieval fortifications in Europe, and their preservation is itself a kind of argument. Edward I of England built them to hold down a conquered people, to make rebellion physically impossible and visually absurd. Seven centuries later they stand as the most eloquent surviving statement of what that ambition cost and what it produced. Four sites make up the inscription: Beaumaris and Caernarfon on the island of Anglesey and the mainland opposite, Conwy on the north coast, Harlech on the Merioneth shore. Together they form what historians call the "iron ring" — a chain of fortresses spaced so that each could be supplied by sea and each could threaten the next valley. It is a military system disguised as a collection of castles, and a political statement disguised as a building programme.

What makes the Gwynedd inscription unusual is that it does not honour a single monument. It honours a programme — a coordinated act of statecraft executed in stone across an entire province, designed by one of the great military architects of the Middle Ages and built by thousands of labourers whose names are mostly lost. To walk from Beaumaris to Harlech is to read a single argument in four chapters.

The King Who Built to Be Obeyed

Edward I came to Wales twice in the 1270s and 1280s, and both times he came with an army and a drawing board. The first campaign, in 1277, was short and decisive: Llywelyn ap Gruffudd, the last native Prince of Wales recognised by the English crown, was stripped of most of his lands east of the Conwy and forced into a humiliating peace. Edward did not simply occupy the territory. He began, almost immediately, to fortify it — at Flint, at Rhuddlan, at Aberystwyth and Builth — and he brought with him a Savoyard mason named James of St George, a man who had already built castles for the Count of Savoy and who would spend the next three decades turning Edward's ambitions into geometry.

The second campaign, in 1282, was a different matter. A widespread Welsh rising, triggered in part by the heavy-handed administration of the new English lordships, drew Edward back with a vast army. Llywelyn was killed near Builth in December 1282; his brother Dafydd was captured and executed at Shrewsbury the following year. Native Welsh princely power was extinguished. Edward now had a province to hold, and he chose to hold it with the most ambitious castle-building programme of the Middle Ages.

The chronology of the four inscribed sites maps onto this sequence precisely. Construction at Conwy and Caernarfon began in 1283, the year of victory, and at Harlech in the same year. Beaumaris came later, begun in 1295 after a revolt on Anglesey under Madog ap Llywelyn reminded the crown that the island was not yet pacified. The workforce was enormous — at times more than three thousand men at Caernarfon alone, drawn from across England and from the Welsh countryside itself, conscripted by royal writ. The cost was staggering: the four castles and their associated towns consumed something on the order of £60,000 to £80,000, a sum that would have funded a small war.

The genius of the scheme lay in its integration of castle and town. At Caernarfon and Conwy, Edward planted English boroughs beside the fortresses, enclosed within the same walls, populated by settlers granted trading privileges. The castle protected the town; the town supplied and financed the castle; both together created a permanent English presence in a Welsh heartland. It was colonisation by urban planning.

The Grammar of Stone

To understand these buildings you have to understand that they were designed by a professional, not improvised by local lords. James of St George's signature is everywhere: the concentric plan, the massive twin-towered gatehouses, the use of water as a defensive perimeter, the careful siting so that each castle could be supplied from the sea even when surrounded by hostile country. He was a specialist in what military historians call the "Edwardian castle" — a form that absorbed the lessons of the Crusades and the latest continental thinking and pushed them to their logical conclusion.

Caernarfon is the most theatrical of the four, and the most ideologically loaded. Its walls are banded with alternating courses of light and dark stone, and its towers are polygonal rather than round — a deliberate echo, scholars argue, of the walls of Constantinople, the imperial city of the Byzantine emperors. Edward was making a claim not just to Wales but to a kind of universal sovereignty. The Eagle Tower, where the king's son and heir was born in 1284 (the future Edward II), is the grandest of all. The castle's plan is a narrow figure-of-eight following the rock, with a great gatehouse and a Queen's Gate, and the whole is wrapped by the town wall — a circuit of some 800 metres with eight towers and two gateways, still almost complete. The town wall is arguably as significant as the castle itself: it is the finest surviving medieval town wall in Britain, and it still defines the shape of Caernarfon's streets.

Conwy is the most complete ensemble. The castle is a compact, powerful rectangle of eight towers and two gatehouses, built on a rock above the estuary. Beside it, the town is enclosed by a wall of roughly 1,200 metres with twenty-one towers and three original gateways — and unlike most medieval town walls, this one survives nearly intact, still walkable for almost its full length. The wall was built at speed, in a single campaign between 1283 and 1287, and its uniformity is part of its power: it reads as a single act of will. Conwy also has the finest surviving medieval merchant's house in Wales, Aberconwy House, a reminder that these were living towns, not garrisons alone.

Harlech is the most dramatic in setting, perched on a crag above Tremadog Bay with the mountains of Snowdonia behind it. Its plan is a compact rectangle with a massive twin-towered gatehouse and a smaller postern gate opening onto the sea cliff — the "Way from the Sea," a long stair defended by its own tower, which allowed the castle to be supplied by ship even when cut off by land. Harlech was besieged repeatedly and held out in the 1460s for seven years during the Wars of the Roses — the longest siege in British history, and the origin of the song "Men of Harlech."

Beaumaris is the most perfect and the most frustrating. Begun in 1295, it was designed on a flat, open site with no natural defences, which allowed James of St George to draw his ideal plan from scratch: a true concentric castle, with an inner ward, a lower outer ward, a water-filled moat, and a great gatehouse — the "Gate of the Knight" — that is the most sophisticated in Britain. But the money ran out. Work stopped in the 1330s, and Beaumaris was never finished. Its towers are truncated, its walls lower than intended. What survives is a design in outline — a textbook of castle-building left open at the first chapter.

Living Inside the Argument

A castle is a machine for a specific kind of life, and these castles were built to support two kinds of people at once: the garrison and the townsfolk. At Caernarfon and Conwy, the town walls enclosed streets of burgage plots — narrow frontages with long gardens behind — laid out on a grid and granted to English settlers. The towns were small by modern standards, a few hundred households, but they were planned as self-conscious outposts of English law and commerce. The castle constable was the effective governor of the town; the burgesses owed him rents and service; the whole was a single economic and military organism.

Daily life inside the walls was cramped and regulated. The town gates closed at night. The wall-walks were patrolled. The castle itself housed a garrison, a constable, a chaplain, and a household staff; it stored grain, salt fish, wine, and weapons; it had a chapel, a great hall, kitchens, and a well. At Caernarfon, the king's apartments in the Eagle Tower were among the most luxurious in medieval Britain, with glazed windows and a private chapel — a reminder that these were palaces as well as fortresses. At Conwy, the royal apartments in the inner ward were similarly grand, and the castle was used as a residence by Edward and later by English kings and princes.

The towns, meanwhile, were never purely military. They were markets, ports, and administrative centres. Conwy traded with Ireland and the Continent; Caernarfon exported wool and imported wine; Beaumaris, though never finished as a fortress, became a thriving port and county town. The walled towns were not ghettos but engines of colonisation — and, over centuries, of gradual accommodation between Welsh and English. By the late Middle Ages the towns had Welsh populations, Welsh trade, and Welsh grievances. The walls that were meant to keep the Welsh out became the walls within which a mixed society lived.

The Long Afterlife

The castles were not, in the end, militarily decisive. They were besieged, taken, and retaken. Harlech fell to Owain Glyndŵr in 1404 and was retaken by the English in 1409. Caernarfon was taken by Glyndŵr's forces in 1403. During the Wars of the Roses, Harlech held for the Lancastrians for seven years before surrendering in 1468. By the Tudor period the castles were already decaying; by the 17th century they were being used as prisons, quarries, and sources of building stone. Beaumaris, unfinished, was never really tested. Conwy was slighted after the Civil War. Harlech was used as a prison and then abandoned.

Their modern significance is a product of the 19th and 20th centuries. The Romantic movement rediscovered them as picturesque ruins; Victorian restorers stabilised them; the Office of Works and later Cadw took them into state care. In 1986, UNESCO inscribed the four sites together — a recognition not of any single building but of a coherent programme of medieval military architecture, and of the extraordinary completeness of the ensemble. The town walls of Caernarfon and Conwy are integral to the inscription, and they are what make the Gwynedd site unique: most World Heritage castles are isolated monuments, but here the castle and the town it was built to control survive together.

The challenges today are the ordinary ones of extraordinary places. Stone erodes; mortar fails; the coastal setting means salt, wind, and rain. Visitor numbers press on fragile wall-walks and narrow staircases. The tension between access and preservation is constant. Cadw, which manages the sites, has invested heavily in conservation and in new visitor centres — at Conwy and Caernarfon in particular — and the wall-walks have been made safe without being made tame. But the deeper challenge is interpretive: how to present these buildings honestly as instruments of conquest without reducing them to a morality tale, and how to let the Welsh story — the story of the people they were built to subdue — be heard within their walls.

The Stone and the Song

Stand on the wall-walk at Conwy on a clear evening and the whole system becomes legible. To the west, the mountains of Snowdonia — the country the castles were built to dominate. Below, the estuary — the supply line that kept them alive. Behind, the grid of streets laid out by English burgesses seven centuries ago, still the shape of the town. The castle is not a museum object. It is a piece of political geography that has never quite stopped working.

The Welsh have a complicated relationship with these buildings. They are, undeniably, the architecture of conquest — built by a king who extinguished native princely rule and planted English towns on Welsh soil. But they are also, undeniably, magnificent — and they are in Wales, of Wales, and now cared for by Welsh institutions. The castles have been absorbed into Welsh identity as much as they were once imposed upon it. Harlech has its song. Caernarfon has its investitures. Conwy has its walls, and the walls have their walkers. The argument the masons made in stone is still being answered, in Welsh, in English, and in the ordinary life of the towns.

✦ Conclusion

The Castles and Town Walls of King Edward in Gwynedd are the most complete surviving expression of medieval military architecture in Britain, and among the most important in Europe. They are not four separate monuments but a single coordinated programme — a system of fortresses and walled towns designed by a professional corps under a single patron, built to hold a conquered province, and surviving with a completeness that is almost without parallel. Caernarfon's banded towers, Conwy's walkable walls, Harlech's sea-stair, and Beaumaris's unfinished ideal together tell a story about power, money, engineering, and the human cost of all three. UNESCO's inscription recognises not just the beauty of the buildings but the coherence of the idea behind them.

What remains after seven centuries is not the conquest itself but its architecture — and architecture, unlike armies, does not leave. These castles are now part of the landscape of North Wales, cared for by Cadw, visited by hundreds of thousands each year, and woven into the identity of the towns that grew up inside their walls. To walk the wall-walk at Conwy or climb the Eagle Tower at Caernarfon is to stand inside a political argument that has never fully ended. The stone is patient. It was built to be.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: What is the Castles and Town Walls of King Edward in Gwynedd known for?
It is known as the most complete surviving group of military architecture from the reign of Edward I, comprising Beaumaris, Caernarfon, Conwy, and Harlech castles together with the medieval town walls of Caernarfon and Conwy. It is celebrated for the quality of its design, the completeness of the ensemble, and its role in the English conquest of Wales.

Q2: When was the site active and important?
The castles were built between 1277 and 1330, with the principal campaigns at Conwy, Caernarfon, and Harlech beginning in 1283 and Beaumaris in 1295. They remained militarily significant through the medieval period, including the Glyndŵr rising and the Wars of the Roses, before decaying from the 16th century onward.

Q3: Is it a UNESCO World Heritage Site?
Yes. The Castles and Town Walls of King Edward in Gwynedd were inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1986, under cultural criteria (i), (iii), and (iv).

Q4: How can one visit the site?
All four castles are managed by Cadw and are open to the public, with visitor centres at Caernarfon and Conwy. They are reached by road and rail from Bangor, Llandudno Junction, and Porthmadog; Caernarfon and Conwy are on the North Wales coast, Beaumaris on Anglesey, and Harlech on the Merioneth coast.

Q5: What are the biggest threats facing the site today?
The principal threats are stone erosion and mortar decay driven by coastal weather, pressure from high visitor numbers on fragile wall-walks and stairs, and the ongoing challenge of interpreting the sites honestly as monuments of conquest while keeping them accessible and economically viable for the towns around them.
