Historic Centre of Avignon — The City That Held the Popes

A medieval river city where cardinals' palaces, a broken bridge, and Provençal light converge

📍 Location: Avignon, Vaucluse Department, Provence-Alpes-Côte d'Azur, southern France, on the left bank of the Rhône
📅 Period: 14th century (Papal residency 1309–1377); bridge begun c. 1177; later additions through the 18th century
🏷️ Category: Cultural
⚠️ Status: UNESCO World Heritage Site (inscribed 1995)
📏 Significance: Europe's largest Gothic palace; a uniquely intact papal ensemble; the celebrated Pont d'Avignon; a living Provençal historic centre

The Light on the Water

Late afternoon in Avignon is a lesson in how stone behaves when the sun goes low. The Rhône runs wide and green-grey beneath the Rocher des Doms, and when the light drops the limestone of the Papal Palace turns the color of old honey. From the far bank at Villeneuve-lès-Avignon, the whole city reads as a single organism: a rock, a fortress, a cathedral, a bridge that stops in the middle of the river and goes no further.

That broken bridge is the first thing most travelers see and the last thing they remember. It has four arches left of the twenty-two it once carried. It has a chapel perched on one pier like a stone saddle. It has a song — "Sur le pont d'Avignon, l'on y danse, l'on y danse" — known to every French child and, through them, to half the world. But the bridge is only the introductory line. The real poem is the palace above it, the largest Gothic building in Europe, a fortress-palace that looks less like a home for vicars of Christ and more like a statement of intent carved in rock.

Avignon is a city that happened because of an accident of politics. In 1309 a French pope, Clement V, chose not to go to Rome. He chose instead this Provençal town at the confluence of the Rhône and the Durance, a place that belonged to the counts of Provence but sat close enough to the French crown to feel safe. For sixty-eight years, seven popes ruled the Western Church from here. They built. They built so much that the city is still, in its bones, a papal city.

What makes the Historic Centre of Avignon remarkable is not any single monument but the survival of a complete medieval ensemble. The palace, the cathedral, the episcopal buildings, the bridge, the ramparts, the narrow streets that once housed cardinals and money-changers — all of it remains legible. You can walk from the Place de l'Horloge to the palace steps in four minutes and pass through seven centuries without once leaving the 14th.

This is a city that has been besieged, sacked, flooded, occupied, and reinvented as a theater festival. It has been a papal capital, a French border town, and a stage. Through all of it, the stone has held.

Origins and Historical Context

Avignon is older than its popes. The name comes from the Celtic Avenio, and the site was settled by the Gallic Cavares before Rome made it a colony. Under the Romans it was a modest river town, prosperous enough to have an amphitheater and baths, obscure enough to escape the worst of the barbarian century. By the early Middle Ages it belonged to the counts of Provence, and by the 12th century it had a working bridge — the Pont Saint-Bénézet, begun around 1177 and finished in 1185 — that made it a crossing point on the great north-south route between Lyon and the Mediterranean.

The bridge made Avignon rich. The popes made it famous.

The story begins with a crisis. In 1303, agents of the French king Philip IV attacked Pope Boniface VIII at Anagni. The pope died weeks later. His successor, Benedict XI, lasted less than a year. When the cardinals met at Perugia in 1305, they chose a Frenchman, Raymond Bertrand de Got, Archbishop of Bordeaux, who took the name Clement V. Clement never went to Rome. He was crowned at Lyon, wandered between Poitiers and Bordeaux, and in 1309 settled at Avignon, in the Dominican convent of the town.

What began as a temporary stay became a residency. Clement's successor, John XXII, a former bishop of Avignon, chose to stay. By the time Benedict XII was elected in 1334, the papacy had been in Avignon for a quarter century and the old episcopal palace was too small. Benedict, a Cistercian and a builder of severe taste, tore down the old palace and began the Palais Vieux — the Old Palace — in 1335. His architect was Pierre Poisson of Mirepoix. The result was a fortress: a great rectangular block with walls up to four meters thick, a cloister, a consistory, and a chapel. Benedict's palace was built for defense first and ceremony second.

His successor, Clement VI, had different instincts. Clement was a Limousin nobleman, a diplomat, a patron, and a spendthrift. Between 1342 and 1352 he added the Palais Neuf — the New Palace — to the south and west, with the Grande Chapelle, the Great Audience Hall, and the monumental staircase that still dominates the courtyard. His architect was Jean de Louvres, and the result was the largest Gothic palace in Europe. Clement bought the city of Avignon from Queen Joanna I of Naples in 1348 for 80,000 florins, an act that made the popes sovereign lords of the town.

Then came the disasters. The Black Death reached Avignon in 1348, killing perhaps half the population. Clement VI stayed in the city and consecrated the cemetery at the Rocher des Doms. The Hundred Years' War brought English and Gascon companies raiding the Rhône valley. The popes built ramparts — twelve kilometers of them, with thirty-nine towers — which still define the city. In 1377 Gregory XI returned to Rome, ending the Avignon residency. In 1378 the Great Schism began, and Avignon became the seat of a rival line of popes — Clement VII and Benedict XIII — until 1408. Benedict XIII, stubborn to the end, held out in the palace until 1403, when he fled the French siege by a tunnel and made his way to Aragon.

After the schism, Avignon remained a papal territory under legates until the French Revolution. The palace became a barracks, then a prison, then a barracks again. The bridge, battered by floods, lost arches in 1603 and 1669 and was finally abandoned. By the 19th century Avignon was a provincial town with a ruinous monument. Its recovery — as a heritage site, as a festival city, as a place of pilgrimage for tourists — is a 20th-century story.

Key Features and Architectural Marvels

The Palais des Papes is not one building. It is two, joined into a single mass that covers 15,000 square meters and rises to a height of 50 meters at the Tour du Trouillas. The façade is a study in severity: pale limestone, narrow windows, buttresses, and a crenellated skyline. It looks less like a palace than a citadel that happens to contain a court.

Walk through the main gate and the Courtyard of Honor opens up. On the left is the Palais Vieux, Benedict XII's work, with its great staircase and the Consistory Hall where the pope met his cardinals. On the right is the Palais Neuf, Clement VI's extension, with the Grande Chapelle and the Great Audience Hall. Above, the towers — the Tower of the Angels, the Tower of the Winds, the Tower of the Trouillas — define the skyline.

Inside, the palace is a labyrinth. The most famous room is the Chapelle Saint-Martial, decorated by the Italian painter Matteo Giovannetti between 1344 and 1346 with scenes from the life of Saint Martial. Giovannetti, who came from Viterbo, also painted the Chapelle Saint-Jean and the ceiling of the Great Audience Hall. His work is one of the great survivals of 14th-century Italian painting outside Italy — a bridge between Giotto and the International Gothic.

The Chambre du Cerf — the Stag Room — is the most intimate space in the palace. It was the study of Clement VI, painted with hunting scenes, birds, and a stag. The frescoes are damaged but legible, and they show a pope who loved the pleasures of the world. The room is small, warm in color, and profoundly human.

The Grande Chapelle, built by Clement VI and completed under Innocent VI, was the ceremonial heart of the palace. It is 52 meters long and 15 meters wide, with a single nave and a polygonal apse. It was here that the popes celebrated the great liturgies of the Avignon court. The acoustics are extraordinary. Stand in the nave and speak, and the sound travels.

The cathedral is next door. Notre-Dame des Doms is a Romanesque building of the 12th century, with a nave and side chapels, and a gilded lead statue of the Virgin on top of the west tower, added in 1859. The cathedral was the seat of the bishop of Avignon, and during the papal residency it was the pope's parish church. The porch is decorated with a Romanesque frieze, and the interior holds the mausoleum of Pope John XXII, a Gothic masterpiece by an unknown sculptor.

The Petit Palais, just north of the cathedral, was built in the 14th century as the residence of the bishop of Avignon and later became the residence of the cardinals. It now houses a museum with a collection of Italian paintings from the 13th to the 16th century, including works by Botticelli, Carpaccio, and Crivelli. It is one of the finest small museums in France.

The Pont Saint-Bénézet is the most poetic monument in the city. It was begun in 1177 and finished in 1185, and it originally had 22 arches and a length of 915 meters. It was a marvel of medieval engineering, built on piers that had to withstand the Rhône's floods. It had a chapel, Saint-Nicolas, built on one of the piers, and a hospital. It carried pilgrims, merchants, and armies. It was repaired and rebuilt many times. Then, in 1603, a flood took several arches. In 1669, another flood took more. The bridge was abandoned and became a ruin. Today four arches remain, with the Chapel of Saint-Bénézet on the second pier. The bridge is a symbol of impermanence, a monument to what the river can take.

The ramparts are another survival. Built between 1355 and 1370 by order of Innocent VI, they encircled the city with 12 kilometers of walls and 39 towers. They were not a complete defense — the city was too large — but they defined the shape of Avignon for centuries. Much of the wall survives, especially on the north and west, and the gates — the Porte Saint-Roch, the Porte de la République — are still in use.

The Rocher des Doms is the green lung of the city. It is a limestone outcrop that rises above the Rhône, and it has been occupied since prehistory. In the 18th century it was laid out as a garden, and it now offers the best view of the palace, the bridge, and the river. It is also the site of the original settlement of Avignon, and archaeological excavations have revealed traces of the Roman town.

The historic centre itself is a maze of narrow streets and small squares. The Rue des Teinturiers, along the Sorgue canal, was the street of the dyers and weavers, and it still has its water wheels. The Place de l'Horloge is the civic heart of the city, with the Hôtel de Ville and the theater. The Rue Joseph Vernet, the Rue de la République, and the Rue des Marchands are lined with 17th- and 18th-century townhouses, the hôtels particuliers of the Avignon nobility.

Purpose and Lifestyle

What was it like to live in Avignon during the papal residency? The city was, for sixty-eight years, the capital of Western Christendom. It had a population of perhaps 30,000 to 40,000, making it one of the largest cities in Europe. It had a papal court, a college of cardinals, a bureaucracy, a university (founded in 1303), a mint, and a textile industry. It had money.

The papal court was the engine. The pope was surrounded by cardinals, most of them French, many of them from Limousin and Quercy. The cardinals built palaces in the city — the Livrée de Viviers, the Livrée de Poitiers, the Livrée d'Auvergne — and they competed in display. The papal household numbered in the hundreds: chaplains, chamberlains, scribes, cooks, grooms, and guards. The Apostolic Camera, the papal treasury, kept the accounts. The Penitentiary dealt with sins. The Chancery produced documents. The Rota heard cases. The city was a machine for the administration of the Church.

The palace was the stage. The pope appeared in the Grande Chapelle for the great feasts, in the Consistory for meetings with the cardinals, in the Audience Hall for ceremonies. The palace was also a fortress: it had a garrison, a treasury, and a prison. It had a garden — the Garden of the Pope — with fruit trees, vines, and a fountain. It had a menagerie, with lions and bears. It had a library.

The city around the palace was a place of money and pleasure. Avignon was famous for its taverns, its gambling, its courtesans, and its luxury goods. The poet Petrarch, who lived in Avignon as a young man, called it "the Babylon of the West" — a place of corruption and vice. He was not alone. The moralists complained about the wealth of the cardinals and the laxity of the clergy. But the city was also a place of learning and art. The university attracted students. The painters — Giovannetti, Simone Martini — brought the new Italian style. The musicians — the composers of the Ars Nova — worked at the papal chapel.

The bridge was the lifeline. It carried pilgrims on the way to Rome, merchants on the way to the fairs of Champagne, and armies on the way to war. The Chapel of Saint-Bénézet was a place of pilgrimage in its own right, dedicated to the saint who, according to legend, built the bridge with the help of an angel. The bridge had a hospital, the Hôpital du Pont, which cared for the poor and the sick. It had a toll house. It had a gate.

The Rhône was the other lifeline. The river carried goods from Lyon and the north to the Mediterranean, and from the Mediterranean to the north. Avignon was a port, with quays and warehouses. The river was also a threat: floods were frequent, and the bridge was repeatedly damaged. The city learned to live with the river, to build on the rock, and to keep the bridge in repair.

The papal residency ended in 1377, but the city did not become a backwater. It remained a papal territory, governed by legates and vice-legates. It remained a center of art and learning. It remained a place of pilgrimage. The palace was used by the legates, then by the French, then by the army. The bridge was used by travelers, then by no one. The city adapted.

Enduring Legacy and Modern Challenges

The UNESCO inscription in 1995 recognized the Historic Centre of Avignon as a cultural property of outstanding universal value. The justification was the completeness of the ensemble: the palace, the cathedral, the episcopal buildings, the bridge, the ramparts, and the streets. It is rare to find a medieval city that has preserved so much of its fabric, and rarer still to find one that was, for a time, the capital of the Western world.

The palace is now a museum and a cultural venue. It receives hundreds of thousands of visitors a year. It has been restored, in stages, since the 19th century. The frescoes of Matteo Giovannetti were cleaned and consolidated in the 1980s and 1990s. The Grande Chapelle was restored. The courtyard was repaved. The palace now hosts exhibitions, concerts, and conferences. It is also a stage for the Festival d'Avignon, the theater festival founded by Jean Vilar in 1947, which takes place every July and has made the city a capital of the performing arts.

The bridge is a ruin, and it is treated as a ruin. It is a monument, not a crossing. It is visited for its arches, its chapel, its view, and its song. It is also a reminder of the power of the river, and of the fragility of human works.

The historic centre faces the challenges of all historic centres: tourism, traffic, housing, and climate. The population of the intramural city has declined, as residents move to the suburbs. The streets are crowded in summer and empty in winter. The buildings need maintenance. The Rhône still floods — the great flood of 2003 reached the lower streets — and the city has to manage the risk.

But Avignon has advantages. It is a city of manageable size, with a compact centre and a strong identity. It has a university, a theater festival, a museum, and a monument. It has the light. It has the river. It has the song.

The city has also become a model for the adaptive reuse of heritage. The palace is not a frozen relic; it is a working cultural institution. The Petit Palais is a museum. The ramparts are a walking route. The Rocher des Doms is a park. The historic centre is a place where people live, work, and visit. The challenge is to keep it that way.

A City That Sings

Avignon is a city that has been many things, and it carries them all at once. It is a papal city, a French city, a Provençal city, a festival city. It is a city of stone and a city of song. It is a city that has been besieged and a city that has been celebrated. It is a city that has lost its bridge and kept its palace.

The palace is the great fact. It is the largest Gothic building in Europe, and it is the only surviving papal palace of the medieval period. It is a monument to the ambition of the papacy and to the skill of its architects. It is also a monument to the fragility of power: the popes left, and the palace became a barracks. The stone survived, but the institution that built it did not.

The bridge is the great poem. It is a ruin, and it is a symbol. It is a reminder that the Rhône is stronger than any bridge, and that the song is stronger than the river. "Sur le pont d'Avignon, l'on y danse, l'on y danse" — the song is about a bridge that no longer carries anyone, and it is sung by children who have never seen it. That is a kind of immortality.

The historic centre is the great lesson. It is a place where the past is not a museum but a neighborhood. It is a place where you can buy bread in a 14th-century street, drink coffee in a 17th-century square, and listen to music in a 14th-century palace. It is a place where heritage is not a barrier but a resource.

Avignon is not a large city. It is not a capital. It is not a metropolis. It is a provincial town with a monumental past, and it has learned to live with that past. It has learned to share it. It has learned to sing about it. And it has learned to keep the stone in repair.

Stand on the Rocher des Doms at sunset, and you can see the whole thing: the palace, the cathedral, the bridge, the river, the ramparts, the streets. You can see the light on the water. You can hear the song. You can understand why the popes stayed, and why they left, and why the city remains.

✦ Conclusion

The Historic Centre of Avignon is a rare survival: a complete medieval ensemble that was, for sixty-eight years, the capital of Western Christendom. Its significance lies in the combination of monuments — the Palais des Papes, the largest Gothic palace in Europe; the cathedral of Notre-Dame des Doms; the Petit Palais; the Pont Saint-Bénézet; the ramparts — and in the urban fabric that connects them. It is a place where the 14th century is not a period but a presence. It is also a place where heritage is alive: the palace is a museum and a theater, the bridge is a monument and a song, the streets are a neighborhood and a destination. For travelers interested in the medieval papacy, in Gothic architecture, or in the relationship between cities and rivers, Avignon is essential. It rewards slow walking, close looking, and a willingness to listen to the stone.

The last impression is one of light and endurance. The palace is severe, the bridge is broken, the river is wide, and the song is everywhere. Avignon is a city that has been through everything and kept its shape. It is a city that has learned to live with its past, and to share it. It is a city that sings. And in a world where heritage is often a battlefield, that is a rare and valuable thing. To walk from the Place de l'Horloge to the Rocher des Doms at sunset is to understand why the popes chose this place, and why the world has chosen to remember it. For those who wish to explore further, the story of Avignon connects naturally to the broader heritage of the Rhône valley and to the great Gothic monuments of France.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: What is Avignon known for?
Avignon is known for the Palais des Papes, the largest Gothic palace in Europe and the seat of the papacy from 1309 to 1377; for the Pont Saint-Bénézet, the broken bridge immortalized in the song "Sur le pont d'Avignon"; and for its well-preserved medieval historic centre, ramparts, and the annual Festival d'Avignon.

Q2: When was Avignon active/important?
Avignon was most important during the 14th century, when it was the residence of seven popes (1309–1377) and later of the rival popes of the Western Schism (1378–1408). It remained a papal territory until the French Revolution and has been a cultural center, especially since the founding of the Festival d'Avignon in 1947.

Q3: Is Avignon a UNESCO World Heritage Site?
Yes. The Historic Centre of Avignon: Papal Palace, Episcopal Ensemble and Avignon Bridge was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1995 under cultural criteria.

Q4: How can one visit Avignon?
Avignon is accessible by high-speed train (TGV) from Paris, Lyon, and Marseille, with a station at Avignon TGV and a central station at Avignon Centre. The palace, bridge, and historic centre are walkable. The palace is open year-round (hours vary by season); the bridge is open daily. The Festival d'Avignon takes place each July.

Q5: What are the biggest threats facing Avignon today?
The main threats are tourism pressure, the decline of the resident population in the intramural centre, the maintenance of historic buildings, and flood risk from the Rhône. Climate change and the need to balance conservation with visitation are ongoing challenges.
