The Rock

There is a limestone rock in the centre of Athens that rises approximately 70 metres above the surrounding city, flat on top, steep on most sides, approximately 270 metres long and 156 metres wide at its broadest, visible from almost every part of the city and from a considerable distance beyond it. The Greeks called it the Acropolis — the high city, the citadel — and they used it first, as people everywhere have always used high defensible rocks, as a place of refuge and a place of power. The earliest evidence of human occupation on the Acropolis of Athens dates to the Neolithic period, more than four thousand years ago. The first substantial buildings on its summit were Bronze Age structures — a Mycenaean palace and a circuit wall of massive limestone blocks, the Cyclopean masonry that later Greeks, unable to imagine how human beings could have moved stones so large, attributed to the mythological one-eyed giants.

What makes the Acropolis the most recognisable archaeological site in the world is not its Mycenaean past. It is what was built on its summit in a brief, extraordinary burst of architectural activity between 447 and 406 BCE — a period of less than fifty years in which four buildings were completed that would define the visual language of Western architecture for the next two and a half thousand years. The Parthenon, the Propylaea, the Erechtheion, and the Temple of Athena Nike: these four buildings, built on a rocky platform in the fifth century BCE by a city-state of perhaps 300,000 citizens, constitute the single most influential architectural achievement in the history of human civilisation.

Every classical building in the world — every government building with Doric columns, every bank with a triangular pediment, every university with a colonnaded portico — is, consciously or not, a reference to the Parthenon. The vocabulary of Western architecture was established on this limestone rock in the fifth century BCE, and it has not been abandoned since. Readers who have explored our post on Temples of Angkor, Cambodia will know that the Nabataean builders of Petra borrowed the Hellenistic architectural vocabulary developed in Athens and transformed it into something entirely their own — a measure of how far the influence of the Acropolis travelled even in antiquity.

The Context — Athens in the Age of Pericles

To understand the Acropolis, it is necessary to understand the moment that produced it. In 480 BCE, the Persian army of Xerxes I sacked Athens, destroyed the existing temples on the Acropolis, and burned the city. The Athenians, who had evacuated before the Persian arrival, returned after the Persian defeat at the Battle of Salamis to find their sacred hill desecrated. They left the ruins in place for a generation — a deliberate act of public memory, a reminder of the Persian sacrilege — and then, in 449 BCE, after a formal peace with Persia and with the Athenian treasury swollen by the tribute of the Delian League, the statesman Pericles proposed the most ambitious building programme in the history of the Greek world.

The Periclean building programme was not merely architectural. It was a statement of political philosophy — a claim about what Athens was and what it meant. Athens had led the Greek resistance to the Persian invasion. Athens was now the greatest city in the Greek world, the centre of the most advanced culture on Earth, the home of democracy, philosophy, tragedy, comedy, and the first systematic attempt to understand the world through reason rather than mythology. The buildings on the Acropolis were to be monuments to all of this — to Athens's greatness, Athens's piety, Athens's claim to be, as Pericles put it in the funeral oration recorded by Thucydides, an education to all Greece.

The architect Ictinus and the sculptor Pheidias were given the responsibility of executing this vision. What they produced exceeded it.

The Parthenon — Perfection Without Simplicity

The Parthenon is the most studied building in the world. It has been the subject of continuous scholarly attention since antiquity, and the more carefully it is studied, the more extraordinary it becomes. At first glance it appears to be a straightforward Doric temple — a rectangular building surrounded by columns supporting a horizontal entablature and a triangular pediment at each end, the canonical form of Greek religious architecture. On closer examination, nothing about it is straightforward, and nothing about it is simple.

The Parthenon contains almost no straight lines. Its columns are not vertical but lean slightly inward, converging at a point approximately 1.5 kilometres above the building — a correction that prevents the optical illusion of outward lean that would be created by truly vertical columns at this scale. The columns are not of uniform diameter but swell slightly at a point approximately one-third of their height — a refinement called entasis, designed to prevent the optical illusion of concavity that would be created by columns of perfectly uniform width. The stylobate — the platform on which the columns stand — is not flat but curves upward toward the centre along both its long and short axes, by approximately 60 millimetres on the longer sides — a curve so subtle it is invisible to the unaided eye, but whose absence would make the building look heavier and more earthbound than it does.

Every one of these refinements required mathematical calculations of considerable sophistication and demanded that each element of the building be manufactured to individual specifications rather than to a standard template. The result is a building in which every element appears perfectly regular precisely because no element is perfectly regular — a building whose perfection is the product not of mechanical precision but of the most exquisite judgment about the relationship between the mathematical and the visual, between what is geometrically correct and what appears correct to the human eye.

It was built as a temple to Athena Parthenos — Athena the Virgin — the patron goddess of Athens, and it housed one of the greatest works of ancient sculpture: the chryselephantine statue of Athena by Pheidias, approximately twelve metres tall, its skin of ivory, its armour and drapery of pure gold, its eyes of precious stone. The statue no longer exists. The Parthenon without the Athena of Pheidias is a building without its purpose; but the building itself is sufficient evidence of what its age was capable of.

The Sculptures — The Elgin Marbles and the Question of Return

The Parthenon was decorated with the most ambitious sculptural programme of the fifth century BCE. The triangular pediments at each end contained large groups of freestanding figures depicting mythological scenes — the birth of Athena from the head of Zeus on the eastern pediment, and the contest between Athena and Poseidon for the patronage of Athens on the western. The 92 metopes around the exterior were carved in high relief with scenes of mythological combat — Lapiths fighting Centaurs, Gods fighting Giants, Greeks fighting Amazons, Greeks fighting Trojans — allegories of the victory of civilisation over barbarism.

Running around the top of the inner wall of the colonnade was the continuous Ionic frieze — 160 metres of low-relief carving depicting the Panathenaic procession, the great festival of Athens at which a new robe was brought to the goddess, a scene of Athenian civic life rendered in stone with a naturalism, a sense of movement, and a humanity that had never been achieved in sculpture before and would not be surpassed for nearly two thousand years.

Approximately half of the surviving frieze, along with substantial portions of the pediment sculptures and several metopes, were removed between 1801 and 1812 by Thomas Bruce, the 7th Earl of Elgin, then British Ambassador to the Ottoman Empire. These sculptures — now known as the Elgin Marbles — are currently housed in the British Museum in London, which has consistently refused Greek requests for their return. The construction of the Acropolis Museum in Athens, completed in 2009 and specifically designed to house the Parthenon sculptures in their proper context, has given the question new urgency. The frieze is displayed in the Acropolis Museum as a continuous sequence with plaster casts replacing the sections in London. The gap is visible. The argument continues.

The Erechtheion — Where the Gods Were Present

While the Parthenon is the grandest building on the Acropolis, the Erechtheion — built between 421 and 406 BCE on the northern side of the summit — is in many ways the most sacred. It was built on the spot where, according to Athenian tradition, the original contest between Athena and Poseidon for the patronage of Athens had taken place: where Poseidon had struck the rock with his trident and produced a saltwater spring, and where Athena had planted the first olive tree. The spring and the olive tree — or rather, their successors — were still present within and adjacent to the building, physical reminders of the divine events that had made Athens what it was.

The Erechtheion housed several distinct sacred presences: the ancient olive-wood cult image of Athena Polias, the city-protector, to which the Panathenaic robe was brought; the tomb of the mythical Athenian king Erechtheus; the sacred precinct of Poseidon; and various other cult objects whose exact nature and arrangement were known only to the initiated. The building's irregular plan — its various projecting porches arranged at different levels to accommodate the uneven terrain and multiple sacred spots — reflects this complexity of sacred geography.

The most celebrated feature of the Erechtheion is the Porch of the Caryatids: a southern porch whose roof is supported not by conventional columns but by six draped female figures standing with the serene authority of young women who have been holding up a porch roof for twenty-four centuries and intend to continue doing so. Five of the six originals are currently in the Acropolis Museum, replaced on the building by exact copies. The sixth is in the British Museum.

The Propylaea and the Temple of Athena Nike

The approach to the Acropolis summit is controlled by the Propylaea — the great gateway built by the architect Mnesicles between 437 and 432 BCE, a complex of entrance halls and colonnades that transforms the act of approaching the sacred summit into a ceremonial experience. The central passageway of the Propylaea is flanked by Doric columns on its outer face and Ionic columns on its inner face — a combination that signals, with architectural eloquence, the transition from the ordinary world below to the sacred world above. The pinacotheca — the picture gallery — on the northern wing of the Propylaea housed paintings, making it one of the earliest purpose-built art galleries in history.

Perched on the small bastion to the south-west of the Propylaea, the Temple of Athena Nike — the smallest of the four Periclean buildings, barely eight metres wide and five metres deep — was built to honour Athena as the goddess of victory. Its frieze depicted the Battle of Marathon and other Greek victories over the Persians. The temple is Ionic in order, its columns slender and elegant in contrast to the robust Doric columns of the Parthenon and the Propylaea — a deliberate variation demonstrating the Periclean architects' command of the full range of the Greek architectural vocabulary.

The Propylaea — Gateway to the Sacred

The Propylaea is among the most ingenious architectural achievements on the Acropolis — a monumental gateway whose function was not merely to control access but to prepare the visitor psychologically for the experience of the sacred summit beyond. Its architect Mnesicles worked with a challenging and irregular site — a steep slope, an existing bastion for the Temple of Athena Nike, and the constraint of the sacred precinct above — and produced a building of remarkable spatial complexity and ceremonial grandeur.

The central hall of the Propylaea was divided into two aisles by a row of Ionic columns, creating a processional space of considerable height and dignity through which the Panathenaic procession, the great civic-religious festival of Athens, passed on its way to the Parthenon. The coffered ceiling of the central hall was painted in blue and gold — traces of the original paint survived into the nineteenth century, offering a reminder that the white marble world of the Acropolis was, in antiquity, a world of vivid colour. Every surface of every building on the summit was painted: the triglyphs of the Parthenon were blue, the metopes vivid with colour, the sculptural figures painted with naturalistic detail that has long since vanished, leaving only the white marble that later centuries mistakenly took to be the original appearance.

Two and a Half Millennia of Use and Abuse

The buildings of the Acropolis have been repurposed, damaged, and partially destroyed over the course of their long existence. The Parthenon was converted to a Christian church in the fifth century CE — dedicated to the Virgin Mary, a substitution that preserved the building but required modifications to its interior. It was then converted to a mosque after the Ottoman conquest of Athens in 1458 CE. In 1687, during the Venetian siege of Athens, a Venetian artillery shell ignited an Ottoman powder magazine stored inside the Parthenon. The resulting explosion blew out the central portion of the building, destroying the roof, the interior walls, and many columns, reducing what had been a substantially intact structure to the dramatic ruin that stands today.

The nineteenth century brought both scholarship and further damage: the accumulated buildings of the Byzantine, Frankish, and Ottoman periods were cleared from the summit in the name of revealing the ancient monuments, destroying the evidence of fifteen centuries of subsequent occupation. Restoration and consolidation work has continued throughout the twentieth and twenty-first centuries, with mixed results: some early restorations used iron clamps that rusted and expanded, cracking the marble they were meant to stabilise, requiring subsequent removal and replacement with titanium.

The current restoration programme, overseen by the Greek Ministry of Culture and the Committee for the Conservation of the Acropolis Monuments, is the most extensive and scientifically rigorous in the history of the site. Its goal is not to return the buildings to their original appearance but to stabilise and conserve what survives, while making clear through the use of different materials and reversible interventions what is original and what is modern restoration.

Reflection — The Standard That Endures

The Acropolis has been described as the most important ancient site in the world, and the description is defensible. Not because it is the most ancient — it is not — and not because it is the largest — it is not — but because the buildings on its summit represent, with a clarity and completeness unmatched elsewhere, the moment when human beings first articulated in built form certain ideas about beauty, proportion, reason, and the relationship between the human and the divine that have not ceased to be operative in the two and a half thousand years since.

UNESCO recognised this in 1987, inscribing the Acropolis under five criteria as the supreme expression of the development of the Classical Greek spirit and of ancient Greek civilisation. Readers who have explored our post on The Jerusalem of Africa, Ethopia will know that the World Heritage List encompasses sites of radically different kinds of significance — natural and cultural, living and archaeological. The Acropolis represents the cultural end of that spectrum at its most absolute: a site whose significance is entirely the product of human imagination, human faith, and human skill, and which has shaped the human world more profoundly than almost any other place on Earth.

Acropolis of Athens — The Cradle of Western Civilisation

✦ Conclusion

The Acropolis of Athens is the founding monument of the civilisation that produced democracy, philosophy, theatre, and the scientific method. Standing on its flat-topped rock above the city that gave the world more of its foundational ideas than perhaps any other place in history, the Parthenon has served for two and a half millennia as the supreme symbol of that extraordinary intellectual and cultural inheritance. Its proportions, its materials, its subtle optical corrections — all the optical illusions built into its columns and stylobate to make the whole appear perfectly straight — speak of an architectural mastery that still commands genuine awe.

What gives the Acropolis its particular power is precisely its continuity with the present. This is not a dead heritage site. It is the backdrop and the icon of a living European capital, visible from much of central Athens, climbing the city's skyline the way a lighthouse claims the sea. Every morning that Athenians and visitors look up at the Parthenon, they are looking at the same monument that Pericles inaugurated in 438 BCE, in the same city, under the same sky.

The ongoing dispute between Greece and the United Kingdom over the Elgin Marbles — the Parthenon Sculptures held in the British Museum since the early 19th century — remains one of the most significant debates in international heritage law. The New Acropolis Museum, built specifically to receive the sculptures should they be returned, makes the case for that return as powerfully as any argument. Whether they come home or not, the Acropolis will endure — it has survived twenty-five centuries already, and has no intention of stopping.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: When was the Parthenon built?

The Parthenon was built between 447 and 432 BCE, during the golden age of Athens under the statesman Pericles. It was designed by the architects Ictinus and Callicrates under the artistic direction of the sculptor Pheidias, who also created the massive gold-and-ivory statue of Athena that once stood within it.

Q2: What are the Elgin Marbles and why are they controversial?

The Elgin Marbles are a collection of marble sculptures removed from the Parthenon and other Acropolis structures by British diplomat Lord Elgin between 1801 and 1812. They are currently held in the British Museum in London. Greece has campaigned for their return for decades, arguing they were removed illegally and belong with the remaining sculptures in Athens.

Q3: Is the Acropolis a UNESCO World Heritage Site?

Yes, the Acropolis of Athens was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1987, recognised as an outstanding universal value as the supreme expression of the development of the ancient Greek spirit and civilisation, and as a symbol of the cultural heritage of the Western world.

Q4: What other structures besides the Parthenon are on the Acropolis?

The Acropolis complex includes the Erechtheion (with its famous Porch of the Caryatids), the Propylaia gateway, the Temple of Athena Nike, the Theatre of Dionysus (the world's first theatre), and the Odeon of Herodes Atticus, which still hosts cultural performances today.
