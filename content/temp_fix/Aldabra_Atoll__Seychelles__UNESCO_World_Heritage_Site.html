A Ring of Coral at the End of the Road

Far out in the western Indian Ocean, more than a thousand kilometres from the nearest busy port, a ring of low, jagged islands encloses a shimmering lagoon. There are no towns here, no roads and no shops. A handful of scientists and rangers live on the atoll at any one time, tending a research station beside the passes through which the tide pours twice a day. Everything else belongs to the animals, and above all to the tortoises, which wander across the islands in their tens of thousands, grazing on a close-cropped turf and sheltering from the sun beneath the scrub.

This is Aldabra, a raised coral atoll in the Seychelles that UNESCO describes as four large coral islands enclosing a shallow lagoon, the whole surrounded by a coral reef. Because of its remoteness and difficult access it has been protected from human influence, and it holds the world’s largest population of giant tortoises, estimated at about 150,000, as well as the last surviving flightless bird of the western Indian Ocean. Its seas teem with turtles, sharks, dugongs and fish in numbers that have long since vanished from most of the world’s reefs.

Aldabra is also a story of rescue. In the nineteenth century its tortoises were hunted for meat, and in the twentieth its isolation made it an attractive place for a military base. A group of scientists, including some of the leading naturalists of their day, campaigned to save it, and today it is one of the best-protected places on Earth. But it is also a place under new pressure, from warming seas and from a luxury resort on a neighbouring island. This post tells the story of the atoll, its life and its future.

A Raised Atoll

Aldabra is what geologists call a raised coral atoll. Most atolls are low rings of coral that barely rise above the waves, but Aldabra’s reef was lifted above sea level, leaving a rugged limestone platform. UNESCO’s description notes that most of the land surface consists of ancient coral reef, roughly 125,000 years old, which has been repeatedly raised above the sea. The atoll consists of four main islands, Grande Terre, Malabar, Polymnie and Picard, separated by narrow passes and enclosing a large shallow lagoon.

The coral limestone has been weathered into a sharp, jagged surface known as platin, riddled with holes and ridges, and sculpted into mushroom-shaped rocks called champignon along the coast. The result is a rugged topography that supports many distinct habitats. At each tide, huge volumes of water rush in and out of the lagoon through the passes, creating strong currents that are among the atoll’s most dramatic spectacles.

The Tortoises of Aldabra

The animal that made Aldabra famous is the Aldabra giant tortoise, a vast, slow-moving herbivore with a domed shell that can reach a metre in length and a lifespan that can exceed a hundred years. UNESCO notes that the atoll holds the largest population of giant tortoises in the world, and recent estimates put the figure at around 150,000 animals, with newer research suggesting that it may be higher still. The population is entirely self-sustaining and has remained stable over recent decades.

Giant tortoises once lived on many islands across the world until humans arrived and exterminated them, directly for food or through the predators they introduced. Aldabra is one of only two places where they still survive, the other being the Galapagos, and it is the last place in the Indian Ocean. The tortoises are not just residents but engineers: their heavy grazing maintains a distinctive habitat called tortoise turf, a diverse grassland found almost nowhere else, and their movements disperse seeds and shape the vegetation.

The Flightless Rail and the Birds of Aldabra

Aldabra is a major refuge for birds. UNESCO identifies the white-throated or Aldabra rail as the last surviving flightless bird of the western Indian Ocean, a secretive, chicken-sized bird that scuttles through the undergrowth and, having no predators until recently, lost the ability to fly. Other endemic land birds include the Aldabra drongo and the Aldabra fody, which was only recently confirmed as a distinct species, while a brush warbler is presumed extinct.

The seabirds are even more spectacular. Aldabra holds the second largest frigatebird colony in the world, with several thousand pairs of great and lesser frigatebirds nesting in the mangroves, and a census in 2022 and 2023 recorded more than 22,000 breeding pairs of red-footed boobies. The lagoon also supports one of only two oceanic flamingo populations and the endangered Madagascar pond-heron, birds that depend on its tidal flats.

Turtles, Dugongs and a Living Reef

The sea around Aldabra is as remarkable as the land. The atoll is the largest rookery in the western Indian Ocean for green turtles, whose nesting numbers have increased by hundreds of per cent over four decades of protection, and a small number of critically endangered hawksbill turtles nest on the lagoon beaches. A survey of the lagoon in 2022 and 2023 counted at least 23 dugongs, making Aldabra the last known home of these gentle marine mammals in the Seychelles and an important breeding area for the region.

The reef is also exceptionally rich. Aldabra’s corals include an estimated 118 species, and a National Geographic expedition in 2015 estimated fish biomass in the shallow waters to be more than ten times that of the Seychelles’ inner islands, with hammerhead sharks and giant groupers among the threatened species recorded. Such abundance is a rare glimpse of what tropical seas looked like before industrial fishing.

Mangroves, Pools and Beaches

The lagoon edge is lined with mangroves composed of seven species, which have been stable or even expanded slightly in recent years, and which shelter young fish, nesting birds and crabs. Behind them lie intertidal mudflats covering about thirteen square kilometres, where waders feed, and extensive seagrass meadows that feed turtles and dugongs. On the atoll’s outer coast about fifty beaches provide nesting places for turtles.

Inland, some twenty freshwater and brackish pools form in hollows in the limestone, varying from temporary puddles to permanent ponds. They provide water for tortoises, birds and land crabs, and some hold endemic red shrimps. Aldabra is also home to coconut crabs, among the largest land invertebrates in the world, and to bats, the only native mammals, three of whose four species are endemic.

From Exploitation to Protection

Aldabra’s history is one of exploitation followed by rescue. Arab seafarers knew the atoll for centuries, and in the eighteenth century it became a dependency of the French colony of Réunion, whose ships harvested tortoises for meat. In the nineteenth century sailors took them in great numbers; in 1842 two ships were reported to have removed 1,200 in a single visit. Coconut plantations and sisal cultivation cleared small areas, but the atoll’s remoteness and rugged terrain saved it from large-scale development.

The first call for protection came from naturalists. In 1874 a petition was circulated to save the Aldabra tortoises, and the great naturalist Charles Darwin was among those who supported it. Even so, tortoises continued to be taken, and it was only in the twentieth century that effective protection began, as scientists recognised how rare and valuable an undisturbed atoll had become.

The Aldabra Affair

In the 1960s Aldabra became the subject of an international controversy. British planners, with American interest, considered building a military facility on the atoll as part of defence arrangements in the Indian Ocean, and scientists around the world protested that the plan would destroy a unique ecosystem. The campaign, known as the Aldabra Affair, drew in the Royal Society of London, which organised a major scientific expedition to the atoll in 1967 and 1968 to document its natural history.

The military plan was abandoned, and the research that followed gave Aldabra its scientific reputation as a natural laboratory for the study of island ecology and evolution. After the Royal Society completed its work, the Seychelles Islands Foundation, a public trust established by the Seychelles in 1979, took over the management of the atoll. It was declared a Special Nature Reserve in 1981 and inscribed on the World Heritage List in 1982.

What UNESCO Recognised: Criteria (vii), (ix) and (x)

Aldabra was inscribed under three natural criteria. Criterion (vii) recognised the atoll as a superlative natural phenomenon, with four large coral islands enclosing a lagoon in a landscape of exceptional beauty. Criterion (ix) recognised it as an outstanding example of an oceanic island ecosystem in which evolutionary processes are active within a rich biota, with a high degree of endemism. Criterion (x) recognised the importance of its habitats for threatened species, especially the giant tortoise, the rail and the turtles.

The property is also a Ramsar wetland of international importance, a status added in 2010, and its marine environment has been recognised as an important marine mammal area and, in 2019, as an outstanding marine protected area with a Blue Park designation. The whole atoll lies within the property, and the surrounding marine protected area, expanded in 2018, now covers 2,559 square kilometres.

Managing a Remote Paradise

The Seychelles Islands Foundation manages Aldabra from a permanent research station with about fifteen staff, supported by an office in Mahé. The IUCN World Heritage Outlook rates the protection and management of the atoll as highly effective. The research station runs almost entirely on solar power, and long-term monitoring programmes track tortoises, birds, turtles, reefs and vegetation. The atoll is partly funded by entrance fees from the Foundation’s other World Heritage site, the Vallée de Mai on Praslin.

Management has achieved remarkable results. Feral goats were eradicated in 2012, two invasive bird species were removed in 2016 and 2017, and the main invasive plant, sisal, has nearly been eliminated. A feasibility study for the eradication of rats and cats began in 2023. In 2024 the Foundation signed an agreement with the Seychelles Coast Guard to strengthen surveillance against illegal fishing. The outlook is good, though not without concerns.

Threats: Heat, Plastic and Invasive Species

The greatest long-term threat is climate change. Sea temperatures near Aldabra reached about 36 degrees Celsius in 2016, during a global bleaching event that killed about half of the hard coral cover, and further bleaching occurred in 2024 and 2025. The reef has recovered faster than many others, but the intervals between disasters are shrinking. Drought has also become more frequent, with the number of dry months per year roughly tripling in recent decades, which could affect the tortoises and vegetation.

Marine litter is another challenge. Ocean currents deposit plastic on the remote beaches, and a clean-up expedition in 2019 removed 25 tonnes while estimating that around 500 tonnes remained, among the highest accumulations reported for any island in the world. A further clean-up in 2023 removed about 20 tonnes. The threat that worries the managers most is the arrival of new invasive species, since rats and cats are already present and others, such as crazy ants, African land snails and mynas, could be devastating if they reached the atoll.

The Assumption Island Resort

The invasive-species risk has been heightened by a development on the neighbouring island of Assumption, which lies about 27 kilometres from Aldabra and belongs to the same island group. A plan backed by a Qatar-based developer provides for 37 luxury villas, four restaurants and an enlarged airstrip, and construction began in 2025. Conservation groups, including the Seychelles Islands Foundation, opposed the scale of the project, and the IUCN Conservation Outlook of 2025 states that the approval of the resort significantly increases the risk of invasive species reaching the atoll.

The matter became a major political issue in the Seychelles. The newly elected president pledged in late 2025 to suspend the project, and a commission of inquiry into the lease and development of the island delivered its final report in June 2026. A youth-led group, Friends of Aldabra, called on UNESCO to put the issue on the agenda of the World Heritage Committee’s 2026 session. I could not confirm from the sources available what, if anything, the Committee decided, and readers should check the latest UNESCO and Seychelles news for the outcome.

Visiting Aldabra

Aldabra is one of the hardest World Heritage sites to reach. There is no airport, and visitors travel by expedition ship or yacht from Mahé, a voyage of more than a thousand kilometres, or by charter from other outer islands. Tourism is strictly limited to daytime visits supervised by Seychelles Islands Foundation staff, with landings restricted to a small area near the research station and a single anchoring point over sand to avoid damaging the reef. Strict biosecurity measures apply, including cleaning shoes and bags and a briefing for all passengers.

For many people the most accessible way to support Aldabra is to visit the Vallée de Mai on Praslin, the Foundation’s other World Heritage site, home of the giant coco de mer palm, whose entrance fees help to pay for Aldabra’s protection. Anyone planning a voyage to Aldabra should book through operators authorised by the Foundation and check the current rules, as access arrangements can change.

Reflection — The Value of a Place Left Alone

Aldabra offers a glimpse of something that has become almost unimaginable: a tropical island ecosystem in which the large animals still rule. Tortoises graze where they have always grazed, turtles nest on beaches that have never had lights, and the sea around the atoll holds more fish than any reef within reach of a town. Seeing it, one understands how much has been lost elsewhere, and how much can be saved when the will exists.

It is a lesson that the tortoises of the Galapagos Islands, Ecuador and the reefs of the Great Barrier Reef, Australia have also taught. Protection works, but it is never finished: each generation has to defend it anew against the next proposal, the next invasive species and the next warming of the sea. Aldabra’s history of rescue, from Darwin’s petition to the Aldabra Affair, shows that such defence can succeed.

✦ Conclusion

Aldabra Atoll is one of the great natural treasures of the planet. Its raised coral islands, its tortoises, its flightless rail and its teeming reefs make it a living laboratory of island ecology and a symbol of what protection can achieve. Its inscription in 1982 recognised both its beauty and its scientific importance, and the care of the Seychelles Islands Foundation has kept it remarkably intact.

The threats are real: warming seas, plastic, invasive species and development nearby. But the atoll’s history shows that when people decide to protect a place, they can. The future of Aldabra will depend on that decision being renewed, by the Seychelles, by the international community and by visitors who care.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

Why is Aldabra a World Heritage Site?

It was inscribed in 1982 under criteria (vii), (ix) and (x) as a raised coral atoll of outstanding beauty and scientific value, with an intact ecosystem that includes the world’s largest population of giant tortoises, the last flightless bird of the western Indian Ocean and major turtle and seabird colonies.

How many giant tortoises live on Aldabra?

Recent estimates put the population at about 150,000, the largest in the world, and newer research suggests it may be higher. Older UNESCO texts cite more than 100,000. The population has been stable for at least fifteen years.

What is the Aldabra rail?

The white-throated or Aldabra rail is the last surviving flightless bird of the western Indian Ocean. It lives in the undergrowth of the atoll, and a population has been reintroduced to Picard Island.

What is the threat from Assumption Island?

A luxury resort with 37 villas, four restaurants and an enlarged airstrip is being developed on Assumption Island, about 27 kilometres from Aldabra. The IUCN has said it significantly increases the risk of invasive species reaching the atoll. The project has been contested in the Seychelles, and readers should check current news.

Can I visit Aldabra?

Yes, but only by expedition ship or yacht from Mahé on supervised day visits arranged through operators authorised by the Seychelles Islands Foundation. Landings and activities are strictly limited and biosecurity rules apply.

🧳 Visitor's Guide

Access — By expedition ship or yacht from Mahé, more than 1,000 kilometres away; there is no airport on the atoll

Best Time to Visit — Check with authorised operators; voyages depend on sea conditions and the monsoon seasons

Permits — Visits are supervised by the Seychelles Islands Foundation and must be arranged through authorised operators

Activities — Daytime wildlife viewing, snorkelling and diving in designated areas with a staff member

Biosecurity — Visitors must clean shoes, bags and clothing and attend a briefing to prevent introducing invasive species

What to Bring — Sun protection, reef-safe gear, sturdy water shoes and a camera; no food or plants may be taken ashore

Etiquette — Keep to permitted areas, never touch tortoises or nesting turtles and take all litter away

Alternative — Visit the Vallée de Mai on Praslin, the Foundation’s other World Heritage site, whose fees support Aldabra
