The Last Water Before the Desert

For a bird flying south from Europe in autumn, the Sahara is a wall of sand more than two thousand kilometres wide, with almost no water, shade or food. Every year, billions of small birds make the crossing, flying through heat by day and cold by night, and many fall exhausted on the way. For those that survive, the first sight of green on the southern edge of the desert must seem like a miracle. That green is the delta of the Senegal River, and at its heart lies the Djoudj National Bird Sanctuary.

Djoudj occupies about 16,000 hectares of wetland in the north-west corner of Senegal, on the border with Mauritania, not far from the old colonial city of Saint-Louis. Its lakes, backwaters, reed beds and mudflats form a mosaic of habitats that is rare in the Sahel, and in the dry season, when the surrounding country is parched, the sanctuary is the wettest place for hundreds of kilometres. Nearly four hundred bird species have been recorded here, among them some of the largest gatherings of waterbirds in Africa.

UNESCO inscribed Djoudj on the World Heritage List in 1981 under criteria (vii) and (x), recognising both its superlative natural phenomenon, the mass arrival of migratory birds, and its importance for the conservation of biological diversity. It is a place where the pattern of the seasons across two continents becomes visible, and where the fate of European songbirds is bound up with the water levels of a West African river.

A Delta on the Edge of the Sahel

The Senegal River rises in the highlands of Guinea and flows for some 1,700 kilometres through Mali, Mauritania and Senegal before reaching the Atlantic near Saint-Louis. In its lower reaches, the river spreads out over a broad, flat floodplain, and in the rainy season, from July to September, its waters spill across the land, filling lakes and channels and flooding the grasslands. Djoudj lies on this floodplain, in the basin of a brackish side channel known as the Djoudj marigot, which connects it to the main river.

The landscape is shaped by water and its absence. During the flood, the lakes and ponds fill, and fish and invertebrates multiply, creating a feast for birds. As the dry season advances, the water recedes, and the wetland shrinks to a network of deep pools and channels, concentrating fish and attracting great flocks of feeding birds. The vegetation reflects the same rhythm: reed beds and floating plants in the water, salt-tolerant plants such as Salicornia on the saline flats, and scattered acacia and tamarisk on the higher ground.

A significant part of the wider delta lies across the border in Mauritania, and the Diawling National Park on the other bank forms a natural continuation of the Djoudj. The two countries have cooperated in managing the delta as a single ecological unit, and the area was recognised as a transboundary biosphere reserve in 2005, a sign of the importance of cooperation in protecting migratory species that ignore national frontiers.

The Great Arrival

The spectacle that made Djoudj famous begins in October and November, when the first migrants arrive from Europe and western Asia. They come in waves: ducks such as the garganey and the northern pintail, waders such as the ruff and the black-tailed godwit, and countless smaller birds that depend on the reed beds for food and shelter. By midwinter, hundreds of thousands, and in good years well over a million, waterbirds are gathered in the sanctuary, their calls rising from the lakes at dawn and dusk in a continuous roar.

For some species, Djoudj is a place of exceptional importance. The aquatic warbler, a small, secretive songbird that breeds in the marshes of eastern Europe and is one of the continent's rarest migratory birds, winters in the Senegal delta, and the sanctuary is thought to be one of the key sites for its survival. Other European breeders rely on the delta as a stopover before moving further south, and the loss of the wetlands would have consequences far beyond Senegal.

The scale of the migration is a reminder that the natural world is an interconnected system. A reed bed in Poland, a flooded meadow in the Netherlands and a lake in northern Senegal are all parts of the same ecological machine, and the health of each depends on the others. Djoudj is, in this sense, one of the hinges on which the migration of the Old World turns.

Pelicans, Flamingos and the Resident Birds

Not all of Djoudj's birds are visitors. The sanctuary is home to one of the largest breeding colonies of great white pelicans in West Africa, where tens of thousands of these enormous birds gather to nest on low islands and mudbanks. Watching a flotilla of pelicans herd fish into the shallows, plunging their great bills in unison, is one of the most memorable sights in African ornithology. Their white plumage glows against the pale water, and when they take off together the sky seems to fill with wings.

Flamingos, both greater and lesser, wade in the shallow lagoons, filtering algae and crustaceans, while African spoonbills sweep their bills from side to side through the water. Herons and egrets stand motionless at the edge of the reeds, among them the huge goliath heron and the elegant purple heron, and cormorants and darters dry their wings on the branches of dead trees. Overhead, fish eagles and other raptors patrol the lakes, and the chorus of calls changes with the hour.

The variety is part of the wonder. In a single morning, a visitor may see more than a hundred species, from tiny warblers to giant pelicans, and the experience of drifting through a reed-lined channel, with birds rising on every side, is one that few places on earth can match.

Crocodiles, Manatees and Other Creatures

Birds dominate the sanctuary, but they are not alone. The channels and lakes of Djoudj shelter Nile crocodiles, which bask on the muddy banks in the heat of the day, and the waters are home to the West African manatee, a shy, slow-moving mammal that grazes on aquatic vegetation and is rarely seen. The manatee is a vulnerable species across its range, and the quiet waters of the delta provide one of its remaining refuges.

On land, warthogs trot along the tracks, jackals and mongooses hunt through the scrub, and patas monkeys, the fastest primates in the world, race across the open ground. Gazelles, which had been lost from much of the region, have been reintroduced to the sanctuary, and their presence is a sign of the park's recovery. Reptiles, including monitor lizards and several species of snake, add to the diversity.

These animals depend on the same water that sustains the birds, and the health of the wetland is the key to the survival of all of them. When the flood is good, the whole system flourishes; when it fails, the effects ripple through every level of the food web.

Dams, Weeds and the Danger List

The history of Djoudj as a World Heritage Site is dominated by the problem of water. In the 1980s, the construction of the Diama Dam near the mouth of the river, intended to prevent salt water from moving upstream and to supply irrigation for rice farming, changed the natural flood regime of the delta. At the same time, plans for agricultural development in the basin threatened to drain or alter the wetland, and in 1984 the World Heritage Committee placed Djoudj on the List of World Heritage in Danger, where it remained until 1988.

The danger returned in 2000, when an invasive aquatic plant, giant salvinia, spread rapidly across the waterways, forming thick mats that choked the channels, blocked the light and starved the water of oxygen. The weed threatened to destroy the habitat on which the birds and fish depend, and the sanctuary was once again placed on the Danger List. Intensive efforts, including mechanical removal and biological control, brought the infestation under control, and Djoudj was removed from the list in 2006.

The episode demonstrates how vulnerable wetlands are to changes far beyond their borders. The water that reaches Djoudj is controlled by dams, irrigation schemes and political decisions made hundreds of kilometres away, and the sanctuary's survival depends on continued cooperation among the people and governments of the river basin.

People of the River

The delta has never been empty of people. Fishing communities, herders and farmers have lived along the Senegal River for centuries, and the delta's resources have supported the Wolof, the Fulani, the Moors and other peoples. The colourful wooden pirogues of the fishermen are a familiar sight at Saint-Louis, and the seasonal cycle of flood and retreat has shaped the pattern of fishing, grazing and planting.

The creation of the sanctuary in 1971 placed restrictions on some of these activities, and the relationship between conservation and local livelihoods has not always been easy. Over time, however, management has moved toward a more cooperative approach, involving local communities in the protection of the wetland, supporting ecotourism and recognising the importance of traditional uses where they are compatible with conservation.

The old city of Saint-Louis, itself a World Heritage Site, lies about sixty kilometres to the south and serves as the main base for visitors. Its colonial buildings, bridges and lively waterfront provide a fine contrast with the wildness of the sanctuary, and the combination makes northern Senegal one of the most rewarding regions of West Africa to explore.

Visiting Djoudj

The best season to visit is from November to April, when the migratory birds are present, the weather is relatively cool and the roads are passable. Most visitors travel from Saint-Louis, a drive of an hour or two on the road north and then a track through the Sahelian scrub. A park fee is payable at the entrance, and guides and boats are available for hire.

The classic experience is a pirogue trip along the channels, led by a local guide who knows where the birds are gathering. The boat drifts quietly past the pelican colonies, the flamingo flocks and the reed beds, and the guide identifies the species. Walks and drives along designated routes allow visitors to see the mammals, and several observation points provide fine views over the lakes. Binoculars, a hat and sun protection are essential, and a camera with a long lens will repay the investment.

The wet season, from July to October, brings heavy rain and flooding, and some tracks may be impassable. Accommodation is available in Saint-Louis and in a small number of lodges near the entrance. Visitors should check current conditions with local operators before setting out and travel with a reputable guide.

Reflection

To stand on the bank of a Djoudj lake at dawn, as the first light spreads across the water and a hundred thousand birds begin to stir, is to be reminded of how much of the natural world depends on places we scarcely notice. The delta is not grand in the way of a mountain or a canyon; it is flat, hot and quiet. But it is a crucial hinge in the great seasonal journey of birds between two continents, and its survival has required the attention of scientists, governments and villagers on both sides of a river. That a place so humble can matter so much is one of the most hopeful lessons that a World Heritage Site can teach.

✦ Conclusion

Djoudj National Bird Sanctuary is one of the world's great wetlands, and a vital link in the chain of habitats that sustains migratory birds between Europe and Africa. Its pelicans and flamingos are spectacular, its migrants arrive in numbers that stagger the imagination, and its quieter creatures reward patient observation.

It is also a lesson in the fragility of natural systems. Dams and weeds have twice brought the sanctuary to the edge of crisis, and its recovery shows what international cooperation and local commitment can achieve. To visit Djoudj is to witness a wonder of the natural world and to understand how much it depends on human care.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

1. Where is Djoudj National Bird Sanctuary?

It lies in the Senegal River delta in north-western Senegal, on the border with Mauritania, roughly sixty kilometres north of the city of Saint-Louis.

2. Why is it a World Heritage Site?

It was inscribed in 1981 under criteria (vii) and (x) as a wetland of exceptional importance for migratory birds and for the conservation of biological diversity.

3. What birds can be seen there?

Great white pelicans, flamingos, spoonbills, herons, cormorants and many species of duck and wader are present, along with Palaearctic migrants such as the ruff and the rare aquatic warbler.

4. Has Djoudj ever been on the Danger List?

Yes, from 1984 to 1988 because of water management changes, and again from 2000 to 2006 because of an invasion of giant salvinia.

5. When is the best time to visit?

November to April is the best period, when migratory birds are present and the roads are dry.
