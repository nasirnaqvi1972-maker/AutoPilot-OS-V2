The Day They Moved a Mountain

In the 1960s a team of engineers, archaeologists and stonecutters did something that would have seemed absurd to any earlier age. They took two temples carved into a cliff on the edge of the Nile, temples three thousand years old and as large as cathedrals, and cut them into thousands of pieces. They numbered each block, lifted them one by one to a plateau high above the river and reassembled them, stone by stone, inside a new artificial mountain. They did it because the river was about to rise and drown them.

The temples were those of Abu Simbel, built by Ramesses II in the thirteenth century BC, and the threat was the Aswan High Dam, whose reservoir, Lake Nasser, would flood the valley for hundreds of kilometres. The rescue of Abu Simbel, and of the sanctuary of Isis at Philae and other monuments along the river, became the greatest archaeological salvage operation ever mounted. It was organised by UNESCO, funded by dozens of nations and watched by the world.

Today the Nubian Monuments from Abu Simbel to Philae are a World Heritage site, inscribed in 1979, and they stand for two ideas at once: the grandeur of ancient Egypt and Nubia, and the modern conviction that the heritage of humanity belongs to everyone. The story of the campaign led directly to the creation of the World Heritage Convention in 1972. This post follows the monuments from their ancient builders to their modern rescuers.

The Land of Nubia

Nubia is the region of the Nile valley that stretches south from Aswan, in the south of Egypt, into northern Sudan. For thousands of years it was a frontier and a bridge between Egypt and the African interior, a source of gold, ivory, ebony and soldiers, and the home of peoples with their own languages and kingdoms. The Nile in this region flows through a succession of rapids called cataracts, narrow, rocky stretches that made travel difficult and shaped the history of the valley.

Pharaohs repeatedly sought to control Nubia, building fortresses and temples along the river as symbols of power, and the Nubian kings of Kush in turn conquered Egypt and ruled it as the Twenty-fifth Dynasty. The monuments of the World Heritage site, from the south at Abu Simbel to the north at Aswan and Philae, record this long relationship of conflict, trade and cultural exchange.

Ramesses II and the Temples of Abu Simbel

The most spectacular of the monuments are the two temples at Abu Simbel, carved into a sandstone cliff on the western bank of the Nile. The Great Temple was built over about twenty years and completed around the twenty-fourth year of the reign of Ramesses II, a date corresponding to about 1265 BC. Its facade, roughly thirty metres high and thirty-five metres wide, is dominated by four colossal seated statues of the pharaoh, each about twenty metres tall, wearing the double crown of Upper and Lower Egypt.

Beyond the facade, the temple extends deep into the rock through a pillared hall, whose walls are carved with scenes of the pharaoh’s military campaigns, including the Battle of Kadesh, to an inner sanctuary where statues of the gods Amun-Ra, Ra-Horakhty, Ptah and the deified Ramesses sit in the dark. The message was unmistakable. Placed at the southern edge of Egypt’s domain, the temple announced to any Nubian arriving by river that the pharaoh was a god and his power was absolute.

The Small Temple of Nefertari

Beside the Great Temple stands a smaller one, dedicated to the goddess Hathor and to Ramesses’s chief queen, Nefertari. Its facade is distinguished by six standing statues, four of the pharaoh and two of the queen, and unusually the statues of the queen are the same height as those of the king, a mark of extraordinary honour. Inside, the walls depict Nefertari in the company of the gods in scenes of tenderness and beauty.

The temples were forgotten for centuries as the desert sand drifted over the facade. They were rediscovered by European travellers in the early nineteenth century: the Swiss explorer Johann Ludwig Burckhardt noticed the top of the facade in 1813, and in 1817 the Italian adventurer Giovanni Belzoni succeeded in entering the Great Temple. Since then Abu Simbel has been one of the most celebrated monuments of antiquity.

The Sun and the Sanctuary

The ancient architects built a remarkable astronomical effect into the Great Temple. On two days of the year, about 22 February and 22 October, the rising sun sends a shaft of light along the full length of the temple, some sixty metres, to illuminate the statues in the inner sanctuary. By tradition the light falls on the statues of Ramesses, Amun-Ra and Ra-Horakhty, while the statue of Ptah, a god associated with darkness and the underworld, remains in shadow.

The phenomenon is celebrated each year by crowds who gather before dawn. Because the temple was moved in the 1960s, the alignment now occurs a day later than it did in antiquity, a small reminder of the scale of the engineering achievement, which preserved the temple’s orientation almost perfectly while lifting it dozens of metres above its original position.

Philae: The Island of Isis

Hundreds of kilometres to the north, near the First Cataract at Aswan, stands another of the great monuments of Nubia: the sanctuary of Isis at Philae. The complex was founded in the seventh or sixth century BC, but most of its surviving buildings date to the Ptolemaic period, from about 282 to 145 BC, with later additions by Roman emperors, including the elegant riverside kiosk known as Trajan’s Kiosk.

Philae was the centre of the worship of Isis, the great goddess of magic, motherhood and healing, whose cult spread throughout the Mediterranean world. Pilgrims came from Egypt, Nubia and beyond, and the temple remained active long after the rest of ancient Egyptian religion had faded. The last known hieroglyphic inscription anywhere, dated to the year 394 AD, was carved here, and the temples were finally closed in the sixth century when Christianity had become dominant.

More Than Two Temples: The Ten Groups of Monuments

The World Heritage property is much broader than its two famous names. Egypt’s Ministry of Tourism and Antiquities lists ten groups of monuments between Aswan and Abu Simbel: the temples of Ramesses II at Abu Simbel, Amada, Wadi es-Sebua, Kalabsha, Philae on the island of Agilkia, the ancient granite quarries and the unfinished obelisk at Aswan, the Islamic cemetery, the ruins of the ancient city of Elephantine, the Monastery of St Simeon and the Old and Middle Kingdom tombs known as the Tombs of the Nobles.

Together they trace more than three thousand years of history, from the pharaohs and the Ptolemies to Coptic Christianity and the early Islamic period. The unfinished obelisk in the Aswan quarries, which cracked before it could be freed from the rock, offers a rare insight into how the Egyptians quarried and shaped monoliths. Elephantine, an island in the Nile at Aswan, preserves the remains of a town and a temple that were central to the border life of ancient Egypt.

The Dam That Threatened the Past

The crisis began in the 1950s, when Egypt decided to build a great dam across the Nile at Aswan to control the annual flood, generate electricity and irrigate farmland. The Aswan High Dam, built from 1960 to 1970, would create an enormous reservoir, Lake Nasser, that would submerge the Nile valley upstream for hundreds of kilometres, including the homeland of the Nubian people and dozens of ancient sites.

Tens of thousands of Nubians were relocated, and archaeologists realised that a vast heritage was about to disappear. In 1959 the governments of Egypt and Sudan appealed to UNESCO, and the organisation launched an international campaign the following year, calling on the nations of the world to help save the monuments of Nubia before the waters rose.

The Greatest Rescue in Archaeological History

The campaign, which ran from 1960 to 1980, became a landmark in international cooperation. Dozens of countries contributed funds and expertise, and archaeologists carried out rescue excavations across Nubia, recording sites that would soon be lost. Several smaller temples were dismantled and moved to new locations, and the temple of Amada was famously moved on rails to a new site.

The centrepiece was Abu Simbel. Engineers cut the two temples out of the cliff into blocks, which were numbered, lifted and reassembled on a site some sixty-five metres higher and about two hundred metres back from the river, inside an artificial dome built to recreate the original cliff. The operation was completed in 1968. Philae followed, dismantled and rebuilt on nearby Agilkia Island in the 1970s, so that the sanctuary of Isis could once more rise above the water.

Gifts to the World

As a gesture of gratitude to the countries that had helped, Egypt offered several temples from Nubia to donor nations. The Temple of Dendur now stands in the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York, the Temple of Debod in a park in Madrid, the Temple of Taffeh in the National Museum of Antiquities in Leiden and the Temple of Ellesyia in the Egyptian Museum in Turin. They are not part of the World Heritage property, but they spread awareness of Nubian heritage far beyond Egypt.

The campaign also changed the way the world thought about heritage. The success of the rescue showed that nations could cooperate to protect monuments of universal value, and the idea of shared responsibility it embodied led directly to the creation of the World Heritage Convention, adopted by UNESCO in 1972. In that sense, every World Heritage site owes something to Nubia.

What UNESCO Recognised: Criteria (i), (iii) and (vi)

The Nubian Monuments were inscribed on the World Heritage List in 1979 under criteria (i), (iii) and (vi). Criterion (i) recognises the monuments as masterpieces of human creative genius, a description that fits the colossal sculpture of Abu Simbel and the refined architecture of Philae. Criterion (iii) recognises them as exceptional testimony to the civilisations of the Nile valley, Egyptian, Nubian, Greek, Roman and Christian, across more than three millennia.

Criterion (vi) recognises the association of the property with ideas and events of universal significance, which in this case includes the international campaign itself, the moment when the world united to save its common heritage. UNESCO describes the area as an outstanding archaeological zone that contains such magnificent monuments as the temples of Ramesses II at Abu Simbel and the sanctuary of Isis at Philae, saved from the rising waters of the Nile by the International Campaign launched from 1960 to 1980.

Challenges of a Moved Heritage

Rescue did not end the need for protection. The monuments face the pressures of mass tourism, the heat and humidity of the Nile valley, the rising groundwater that salts can bring to stone, and the pressure of development around the sites. In 1999 the World Heritage Centre learned that a structure for a sound and light show was being built in front of the Abu Simbel temples, which would have seriously affected the site’s value. After the alert, the Egyptian Minister of Culture ordered its demolition and the structure was removed.

The incident shows how continuing vigilance is needed even for the most famous monuments. Conservators monitor the stone, the internal dome at Abu Simbel and the microclimate within the temples, and the numbers of visitors have to be managed carefully to protect reliefs and paint that have survived for over three thousand years.

Visiting the Nubian Monuments

Most visits begin in Aswan, a city of white-sailed feluccas, granite islands and a relaxed riverside atmosphere. From there Philae is a short boat trip, and the Aswan quarries, Elephantine Island, the Tombs of the Nobles and the Monastery of St Simeon are all within reach. The Nubian Museum in Aswan provides context for the whole region.

Abu Simbel lies about 280 kilometres south of Aswan. Visitors reach it by road, often in early morning convoys, or by a short flight, and many stay overnight to see the temples at dawn. The cooler months, from about October to April, are by far the best time to go, as summer temperatures are extreme. As conditions and security arrangements can change, it is sensible to check current advice and book transport in advance.

Reflection — A Rescue That Changed How We See Heritage

The Nubian campaign was born of a dilemma that has not gone away. A nation needed water, power and food, and a development project promised them, but the price was the loss of irreplaceable monuments and the displacement of a people. The solution was not to abandon the dam or the temples but to find a way to honour both, and the cost was enormous in money, labour and ingenuity. It was also a reminder that the Nubians themselves paid a heavy price, and that their culture is as much a part of this heritage as the stones.

The monuments stand, in their new places, as a double testimony: to the pharaohs who built them and to the people who saved them. They belong to the same extraordinary world as Ancient Thebes with its Necropolis, Egypt, and like the rock-cut city of Petra, Jordan, they show how ancient builders used the living rock as a medium for power and devotion.

✦ Conclusion

The Nubian Monuments from Abu Simbel to Philae are among the most extraordinary survivals of the ancient world, and also among the most extraordinary rescues. Their colossal statues, painted reliefs and island sanctuaries speak of the power of pharaohs and the devotion of pilgrims, while their relocation speaks of a modern ambition to protect heritage for all.

To stand before the facade of Abu Simbel at dawn is to face the ancient world at its most imposing, and to know that it is standing there because people from dozens of nations decided it should. Visit with respect, with an eye to both the ancient builders and the modern rescuers, and the monuments will repay the long journey south.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

What are the Nubian Monuments from Abu Simbel to Philae?

They are a group of ancient monuments in the Nile valley between Aswan and Abu Simbel in southern Egypt, including the temples of Ramesses II at Abu Simbel, the sanctuary of Isis at Philae, Amada, Wadi es-Sebua, Kalabsha, the Aswan quarries and unfinished obelisk, Elephantine, the Monastery of St Simeon and the Tombs of the Nobles. They were inscribed on the World Heritage List in 1979.

Why were the temples moved?

The construction of the Aswan High Dam in the 1960s created Lake Nasser, whose rising waters would have flooded the valley and submerged the monuments. UNESCO launched an international campaign in 1960 to save them, and temples including Abu Simbel and Philae were dismantled and rebuilt on higher ground.

How was Abu Simbel moved?

The two temples were cut into numbered blocks, lifted and reassembled about sixty-five metres higher and two hundred metres back from the river, inside an artificial dome built to recreate the cliff. The operation was completed in 1968.

What is the sun festival at Abu Simbel?

On about 22 February and 22 October, the rising sun sends light along the length of the Great Temple to illuminate statues in the inner sanctuary. Since the relocation the alignment occurs a day later than in antiquity.

When is the best time to visit?

The cooler months from about October to April are best, as summer heat is intense. Abu Simbel is about 280 kilometres from Aswan and is usually reached by road convoy or a short flight, so check current transport and security advice.
