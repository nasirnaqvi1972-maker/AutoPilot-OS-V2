A Tree, a City and Thirteen Centuries

In the dry plains of northern Sri Lanka, among scrub forest and shimmering reservoirs, a series of enormous white domes rises from the trees. They are the dagobas of Anuradhapura, solid mountains of brick built to enshrine relics of the Buddha, and they have been rising over this landscape for more than two thousand years. Around them stretch the foundations of monasteries, the ruins of palaces, ancient ponds and a forest of stone pillars. At the centre of it all stands something even older and more astonishing: a tree.

The tree is the Jaya Sri Maha Bodhi, a sacred fig believed to have grown from a cutting of the very tree at Bodh Gaya under which the Buddha attained enlightenment. According to tradition it was planted in 288 BC, which would make it the oldest living human-planted tree with a known planting date anywhere in the world. UNESCO describes the sacred city as established around this cutting, brought to the island in the third century BC, and as the capital of a political and religious state that flourished for 1,300 years.

Anuradhapura is a ruin, but it is also a living pilgrimage. Each day thousands of people, dressed in white and carrying flowers and lamps, walk barefoot through its sacred precincts to offer prayers at the Bodhi tree and the great stupas. This post follows the city from its foundation through its golden age to its abandonment and revival, and explains why it holds a unique place among the world’s Buddhist sites.

The Founding of the City

Anuradhapura was founded, according to ICOMOS, in the fourth century BC, on the banks of the Malwathu Oya river in the island’s north-central plain. The Mahavamsa, the great Sinhalese chronicle, describes how the city was planned with the help of soothsayers and architects and how an early king built the first city wall, with a moat in front. The chronicle recounts the city’s growth with the pride of a people who saw their capital as the centre of the island.

The location was well chosen. The dry zone of Sri Lanka receives limited rainfall, but the rivers and the fertile plain allowed rice cultivation, and the city’s kings learned to store and distribute water on a large scale. Over time Anuradhapura grew into a metropolis of palaces, monasteries and markets, a place where traders from the Indian Ocean world met and where the kings of Sri Lanka built their power.

The Coming of Buddhism

The decisive event in the city’s history was the arrival of Buddhism in the third century BC. According to the chronicles, the great Indian emperor Ashoka sent his son, the monk Mahinda, to Sri Lanka, where he met the king Devanampiyatissa at the hill of Mihintale, near Anuradhapura, and converted him. The king embraced the new faith and offered the monk the royal park, the Mahamegha garden, as the site of the first monastery.

The new religion transformed the city. Kings became patrons of the monastic community, and the capital became a centre of Theravada Buddhism, the tradition that has endured in Sri Lanka to the present day. The birthplace of the Buddha at Lumbini, Nepal lies far to the north, but Anuradhapura became one of the great centres where his teachings were preserved and passed on.

The Sri Maha Bodhi: The Oldest Planted Tree

The most precious relic of the city is a living one. According to tradition, Mahinda’s sister Sanghamitta, a Buddhist nun, brought to Sri Lanka a sapling cut from the Bodhi tree at Bodh Gaya, and it was planted in Anuradhapura in 288 BC. The Jaya Sri Maha Bodhi, as it is known, still stands within a walled enclosure, supported by props and surrounded by railings, its heart-shaped leaves shimmering in the breeze. It is believed to be the oldest living, documented, human-planted tree in the world.

For Sri Lankan Buddhists the tree is a source of deep devotion, and it has been tended by kings and guardians for twenty-three centuries. Heads of state have traditionally sought its blessing before beginning important work. Pilgrims approach it barefoot, offering flowers, incense and water, and the quiet intensity of the scene is among the most moving in the Buddhist world.

Giants of Brick: The Great Dagobas

The most striking monuments of Anuradhapura are its dagobas, the Sinhalese name for stupas, huge hemispherical domes of brick built over relics. The Thuparama is the oldest in Sri Lanka and perhaps the oldest visible stupa in the world, said to enshrine the collar-bone of the Buddha. The Ruwanwelisaya, a gleaming white dome ringed by a wall of carved elephants, is considered a marvel of architecture and is sacred to Buddhists throughout the world.

Larger still are the Abhayagiri and the Jetavanaramaya. The Jetavanaramaya is the tallest brick structure in the world, a mass of baked clay that once rose to a height of well over a hundred metres and dominated the skyline of the ancient city. Building such monuments required immense quantities of bricks, engineering knowledge and labour, and they stand as testimony to the wealth and devotion of the kings who raised them.

Monasteries of Learning: Maha Vihara, Abhayagiri and Jetavana

Around the stupas grew vast monastic complexes. Three great fraternities dominated Anuradhapura: the Maha Vihara, the oldest and most orthodox, centred on the Bodhi tree and the Ruwanwelisaya; the Abhayagiri, a major centre of learning that welcomed new ideas and teachers from other Buddhist traditions; and the Jetavana, a later foundation. Their monks studied, copied texts, treated the sick and advised kings, and the monasteries became centres of scholarship that drew pilgrims from across Asia.

The Chinese pilgrim Faxian, who spent two years in Sri Lanka in the early fifth century, described the city and its monasteries in his account of travels, noting the great numbers of monks and the splendid processions. Among the architectural remains is the Lovamahapaya, or Brazen Palace, a monastery building whose forest of hundreds of granite pillars still stands, evidence of a multi-storeyed structure that once rose in the heart of the Maha Vihara.

Stone Carving and Sacred Art

Anuradhapura also developed a distinctive tradition of art. The city’s craftsmen carved semicircular stone slabs, known as moonstones, which lie at the foot of the steps to shrines and are decorated with concentric bands of animals, flowers and geese, a symbolic path of the cycle of existence. Guardstones, carved with figures of protective deities and guardians, flank the entrances to buildings.

Among the best-known sculptures are the serene seated statue known as the Samadhi Buddha, a masterpiece of meditative calm, and the carvings at Isurumuniya, a rock temple whose reliefs include a famous panel of a loving couple. The bathing ponds of Kuttam Pokuna, built of finely worked stone, show the sophistication of the monastic water systems. Together they reveal a culture of great artistic confidence.

Engineers of Water

The sacred city could not have existed without water, and the kings of Anuradhapura became some of the greatest hydraulic engineers of the ancient world. They built great reservoirs, known in Sinhala as wewa or tanks, by damming rivers and gathering rainwater, and linked them with canals that carried water over long distances. Tanks such as the Basawakkulama, the Tissa Wewa and the Nuwara Wewa served the city, while others irrigated the rice fields that fed its population.

The system allowed agriculture in a region of seasonal drought and supported a city of great size. Like Angkor, Cambodia, Anuradhapura was a hydraulic civilisation, one whose monuments depended on the careful management of water, and many of its tanks are still in use today, a continuity that gives the landscape a living quality.

Abandonment and Rediscovery

Anuradhapura’s long reign as capital came to an end after invasions from southern India. UNESCO records that the city was abandoned following an invasion in 993, and the Sinhalese kings moved their capital to Polonnaruwa, further south-east. The monasteries decayed, the tanks silted up and the forest slowly reclaimed the city, leaving the great dagobas as hills in the jungle.

Interest revived in the nineteenth century, when British colonial administrators and archaeologists began to explore and clear the ruins, and a long process of restoration began. In 1949 the Government of Ceylon commissioned a preservation plan that led to the creation of a new planned town east of the old one, so that the ancient sacred precincts could be protected from encroachment, an early example of urban heritage planning that shaped the present landscape.

What UNESCO Recognised: Criteria (ii), (iii) and (vi)

Anuradhapura was inscribed in 1982 under three criteria. Under criterion (ii), UNESCO recognised that the sacred city exerted considerable influence on the development of architecture in the country for several centuries, particularly through its giant dagobas. Under criterion (iii), it recognised that Anuradhapura attests in a unique way to the Sinhalese civilisation, a culture that remained remarkably continuous. Under criterion (vi), it recognised the city as one of the high places of Buddhism, with stupas that have enshrined relics since the third century BC and the sacred Bodhi tree.

The inscription protects an area of about 4,000 hectares, including the sacred precincts, the ruins of monasteries and palaces and the surrounding landscape. The property data records no separate buffer zone, a reminder that the protection of the setting depends heavily on planning and cooperation with the modern city that has grown nearby.

A Living Pilgrimage

Anuradhapura is not a dead city. Each month, on the day of the full moon, called Poya, thousands of Buddhists come to worship at the Bodhi tree and the great stupas, wearing white, carrying lotus flowers and lamps and circling the dagobas clockwise in the traditional manner. The largest festival is Poson, in June, which commemorates the arrival of Buddhism in Sri Lanka and fills the city with pilgrims.

This living devotion is part of the heritage. It has not always been peaceful: in 1985, gunmen attacked worshippers at the Sri Maha Bodhi during the civil war, killing well over a hundred people, a tragedy that is remembered at the site. The persistence of worship in the face of such events shows how deeply the city is rooted in the life of the nation.

Protecting a City Within a City

The challenges of conservation are considerable. The ruins stand within and beside a modern town, and traffic, development and the pressures of tourism threaten the setting of the monuments. The tropical climate, with its heat, monsoon rains and vegetation, constantly attacks brick and stone, and the large stupas require expensive maintenance. Many monuments are also living places of worship, which complicates decisions about restoration and access.

Sri Lanka’s Central Cultural Fund and Department of Archaeology manage the site, and ongoing work includes conservation, excavation and the improvement of visitor facilities. The task is to balance the needs of pilgrims, residents and visitors with the strict protection of the ancient fabric, and to ensure that the sacred city remains a place of devotion rather than a theme park.

Visiting Anuradhapura

Anuradhapura lies about 205 kilometres north of Colombo and can be reached by road or rail, with the journey taking several hours. The ruins are spread over a wide area, so visitors often hire a bicycle, tuk-tuk or car to move between sites, and a visit of at least a day, and preferably two, is needed to see the main monuments. Many travellers combine it with other sites of Sri Lanka’s ancient cities, such as Mihintale, Polonnaruwa and Sigiriya.

Visitors must respect the religious character of the site. Modest clothing covering shoulders and knees is expected, and white is traditional for pilgrims. Shoes and hats must be removed before entering sacred precincts, and visitors should never pose with their backs to a Buddha image or climb on monuments. A ticket for the main archaeological sites is usually required, so confirm current prices and arrangements locally. The weather is hot year round, and the early morning and late afternoon are the most pleasant times.

Reflection — A Capital Built Around Faith

Most ancient capitals were built around palaces, fortresses or marketplaces. Anuradhapura was built around a tree. Its heart was not a throne but a sacred plant, and its greatest monuments were not for kings but for the Buddha. That choice shaped everything: the kings who ruled by serving the faith, the monasteries that became centres of learning, and the water systems that sustained the pilgrims and farmers alike.

In this the city belongs with other great Buddhist monuments of Asia, from the terraced pilgrimage mountain of Borobudur Temple Compounds, Indonesia to the birthplace of the Buddha itself. Yet Anuradhapura has something none of them can claim: a living tree that has been tended for more than two thousand years, linking the present directly with the earliest days of the faith.

✦ Conclusion

The Sacred City of Anuradhapura is one of the most extraordinary places in South Asia. Its great dagobas, its ruined monasteries, its ancient tanks and above all its sacred Bodhi tree record 1,300 years as the capital of a Buddhist kingdom, and they continue to draw pilgrims today. Its inscription in 1982 recognised both its historical importance and its living religious significance.

To visit is to walk among stupas older than most of the world’s great cathedrals, to see devotion unbroken across twenty-three centuries and to feel the calm that comes from places where people have prayed for a very long time. Go with respect, wear white if you can, and let the city teach you its pace.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

Why is Anuradhapura a World Heritage Site?

It was inscribed in 1982 under criteria (ii), (iii) and (vi). The city was the political and religious capital of Sri Lanka for 1,300 years, established around a cutting of the Buddha’s tree of enlightenment, and it preserves giant dagobas, monasteries and a living pilgrimage.

What is the Sri Maha Bodhi?

The Jaya Sri Maha Bodhi is a sacred fig tree in Anuradhapura, believed to have grown from a cutting of the original Bodhi tree at Bodh Gaya brought to the island and planted in 288 BC. It is considered the oldest living human-planted tree with a known planting date.

What are the main sights?

The Sri Maha Bodhi, the Ruwanwelisaya, the Thuparama, the Abhayagiri and Jetavanaramaya dagobas, the Lovamahapaya pillars, the Samadhi Buddha statue, the Isurumuniya temple and the Kuttam Pokuna ponds.

Why was Anuradhapura abandoned?

UNESCO records that the city was abandoned after an invasion in 993. The Sinhalese kings moved their capital to Polonnaruwa, and the ruins were gradually reclaimed by the forest.

What should visitors wear?

Modest clothing covering shoulders and knees, preferably white, is expected at sacred sites. Shoes and hats must be removed in sacred precincts, and visitors should behave respectfully, as many sites are active places of worship.
