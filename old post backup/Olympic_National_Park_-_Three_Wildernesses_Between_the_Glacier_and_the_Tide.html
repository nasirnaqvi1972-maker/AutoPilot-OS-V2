Olympic National Park — Three Wildernesses Between the Glacier and the Tide

A Washington Peninsula Where Glacier-Crowned Mountains, One of the Great Temperate Rainforests of the World and Sixty Miles of Wild Pacific Coast Lie Within a Single Boundary, Home to Roosevelt Elk, Endemic Flora and the Ancestral Lands of the Peninsula's Tribes | UNESCO World Heritage Site

Location:Olympic Peninsula, Washington State, United States of America (Clallam, Jefferson, Grays Harbor and Mason counties; gateways: Port Angeles, Forks and Lake Quinault)
Year of UNESCO Inscription:1981 (no extensions)
Category:Natural
UNESCO Reference:151

Coordinates:47°58′10″N 123°29′55″W
Area:369,659.8 hectares as listed by UNESCO (the National Park Service cites 922,650 acres, about 3,734 square kilometres)

The Peninsula at the Edge of the Continent

There is a corner of the American map where the land runs out of road. In the far north-west of Washington State, beyond the ferries of Puget Sound and the long ribbon of Highway 101 that circles it, the Olympic Peninsula thrusts into the grey Pacific like a clenched fist, and in the centre of that fist rises a mountain range that seems to belong to no other. The Olympic Mountains are not the highest in the West, nor the most famous, but they are among its most self-contained: a crown of ragged, glacier-draped peaks surrounded on three sides by water, and ringed below by a green so dense and so wet that first-time visitors often fall silent when they step into it. This is the country of Olympic National Park, and it is one of the few places on the continent where a person can pass in a single day from the ice of a high ridge, through a cathedral of ancient trees, to a beach littered with driftwood the size of locomotives.

The park was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1981, under reference 151, three years after Yellowstone. Yellowstone National Park, the first national park in the world, is a landscape of fire, steam and open valleys; Olympic is its opposite, a landscape of rain, moss and shadow. UNESCO recognised it under two natural criteria. Criterion (vii) honours its superlative natural phenomena and scenic beauty, the way coast, forest and glaciated summit meet within a short distance of each other. Criterion (ix) honours its ecological processes, the diversity of habitats, and the remarkable endemic wildlife that has evolved in the isolation of the peninsula. Few properties on the list can claim to protect such a variety of worlds within a single fence.

What gives Olympic its particular character is a geographical accident of the most productive kind. Moist air sweeps in from the Pacific, meets the wall of the Olympic Mountains, and is forced upward, cooling and releasing its burden on the western slopes in quantities that dwarf the rainfall of most of the continent. The Hoh Rain Forest on the western side is commonly said to receive somewhere between 129 and 140 inches of precipitation a year, which is to say more than eleven feet of water falling out of the sky. On the leeward side, in the rain shadow of the same peaks, the climate is dry enough that visitors scarcely believe the two places belong to the same peninsula. The park is therefore not one wilderness but several, and it is that multiplicity, held within a single boundary, that makes it so rewarding to explore.

Mountains Made of the Sea Floor

A Crown of Ice above the Rain

The heart of the park is a mountain mass of ridges, cirques and deep, glacier-carved valleys, crowned by Mount Olympus, which rises to 7,965 feet, or 2,428 metres. That is a modest altitude by the standards of the Rockies or the Cascades, but the Olympics rise almost directly from the sea, and their relief is therefore dramatic. The mountain's heavy snowfall feeds a number of glaciers, among them the Hoh Glacier, a river of ice of about five kilometres in length, flowing from the flanks of Olympus. The ice is not what it was. National Park Service figures cited in the standard reference record a decline from some 266 glaciers in 1982 to 184 in 2009, a loss that is among the plainest signals of a warming climate in the park, and one that gives the high country a certain elegiac beauty.

It is also true that these mountains have an unusual character beneath their snow. They are the product of the slow collision of tectonic plates, in which rocks from the ocean floor were scraped upward and folded against the continent, and the peninsula still stands on a coastline where a Pacific seafloor plate sinks beneath North America. The result is a range whose summits carry traces of marine origin, and whose steep walls are cut by rivers that run in every direction from the central massif, to the Hoh and Queets and Quinault on the west, to the Elwha and Dungeness to the north, to the Hamma Hamma and Skokomish toward the east.

The Wilderness That Remains

Nearly all of the park is wilderness in the legal sense. About ninety-five per cent of its land, some 3,500 square kilometres, was designated as the Olympic Wilderness in 1988, and the area was renamed the Daniel J. Evans Wilderness in 2017 in honour of the former governor and senator who had championed its protection. The designation means that there are no roads through the interior, no motor vehicles and no permanent structures, and that anyone wishing to cross the range must do so on foot, carrying a pack. The great roads of the park therefore run only a short way up from the outer edge, to viewpoints and trailheads, and the interior is left to the elk, the cougar, the black bear and the marmot.

The Rain Forest

A Temperate Rainforest at Full Strength

Most people think of rainforest as a tropical idea, and the notion of a rainforest in the cool latitudes of the northern Pacific is an astonishment to them. Yet the valleys of the western Olympics hold one of the great temperate rainforests of the world, and UNESCO's own summary calls the park one of the largest areas of virgin temperate rainforest on the planet. The Hoh, the best known of its valleys, is a stretch of about twenty-four square miles of low-elevation forest, dominated by Sitka spruce and western hemlock, some of which are reported to exceed ninety metres in height and seven metres in diameter, with Douglas fir, western red cedar and bigleaf maple among their companions. Walking among them is an exercise in scale. The trunks rise like the pillars of a church, the light is green and diffuse, and the ground is a soft, thick carpet of fallen needles and moss.

The moss is the real signature of the place. In a forest this wet the epiphytes have taken over: mosses, lichens and ferns grow on every available surface, hanging in curtains from the maples, wrapping the limbs of the spruce, and covering the logs that have fallen to the forest floor. On the short Hall of Mosses trail, a little under a kilometre long, the maple branches are so heavily upholstered with spikemoss that the whole grove seems to have been dressed for a theatrical performance. The nurse log is another of the forest's great inventions. A fallen spruce or hemlock lies on the ground and slowly rots, and on its decaying back young trees take root in a line, so that a straight row of mature trees often marks the grave of a vanished giant. The forest renews itself upon its own dead.

Quinault and the Southern Valleys

South of the Hoh the same forest continues in the valleys of the Queets and the Quinault. Lake Quinault, at the southern edge of the park, is owned by the Quinault Indian Nation, and a fishing permit from the nation is required to use its waters. The glacier-carved valley around it holds the Quinault Rainforest, and the lake itself, which receives an average of about 131 inches of precipitation a year, is surrounded by loop trails that were designated National Recreation Trails in 1979. The historic lodge on its southern shore gives a place to sit, with a fire in the hearth and the rain on the roof, and to understand what the rainforest is by listening to it.

The Wild Pacific Shore

Sixty Miles of Surf, Rock and Driftwood

The third of Olympic's wildernesses is its coast. A strip about sixty miles, or ninety-seven kilometres, in length and only a few miles wide, the Pacific shore of the park is among the wildest stretches of coastline left in the contiguous United States. There is no coastal highway here. The beaches are reached on foot, by trails that drop through the forest to the edge of the sand, and the visitor who arrives finds a shore strewn with enormous logs, hurled up by winter storms and bleached to silver. Sea stacks, those pillars of rock left standing when the surrounding cliffs have been eaten away by the waves, rise from the surf in dark ranks, each topped with a tuft of spruce and each a nesting place for seabirds.

At low tide the rocky headlands yield another world. Tide pools between the rocks hold sea stars, anemones, mussels and small crabs, and the intertidal zone is one of the richest habitats on the peninsula. At high tide the same places are impassable, and the walker must watch the water and the tide table with equal attention. Whales pass offshore on their long migrations, sea otters and seals work the kelp beds, and bald eagles patrol the beaches. The land and the sea are in constant argument here, and the visitor stands at the point where the argument is most visible.

The Buried Village at Ozette

The coast is also a place of deep human memory. At Ozette, on the northern shore, a village of the Makah people was buried by a mudslide some centuries ago, which the standard references place at around the sixteenth century, and the mud sealed its houses and their contents in a waterlogged tomb that preserved perishable materials that would normally have rotted away. After a winter storm exposed the remains in 1970, an excavation went on for about eleven years, and recovered more than 55,000 artifacts, around 30,000 of them of wood. Whaling and sealing gear, fishing tackle, baskets, tools and children's toys came out of the earth, an inventory of a living maritime culture. Many of these objects are now displayed in the Makah Museum at the Makah Cultural and Research Center in Neah Bay, which opened in 1979 and also presents replica cedar longhouses and canoes.

The Peoples of the Peninsula

Eight Tribes, One Landscape

To speak of the park as untouched wilderness, as its early champions sometimes did, is to forget that the peninsula has been inhabited for millennia. The National Park Service recognises eight tribes with ties to the park: the Lower Elwha Klallam, the Jamestown S'Klallam, the Port Gamble S'Klallam, the Skokomish, the Quinault, the Hoh, the Quileute and the Makah. Their connection to the land rests on traditional use, origin stories, belief and spiritual practice, and their ancestors lived throughout the peninsula, on the rivers, the shores and the foothills. Through treaties signed in 1855 and 1856 the tribes ceded their lands and waters to the federal government, and today they live on reservations along the peninsula's shores, among them the Hoh on the river that bears their name, the Quileute at La Push, and the Makah at Neah Bay.

For these peoples the forest, the river and the sea are not scenery but sustenance and kinship. Cedar gave them canoes, houses, clothing and baskets. Salmon gave them food and ceremony. The coast gave them whales, seals and shellfish. The Tribal Journey, a canoe voyage in which many Pacific Northwest tribes travel the waters of the peninsula and beyond, is one living expression of this continuity. Any honest account of Olympic must therefore recognise that its wildness has been tended, not merely found.

The Making of a Park

The protection of the Olympics came in stages. In 1909, President Theodore Roosevelt proclaimed Mount Olympus National Monument, largely to protect the calving grounds and summer range of the great elk that would eventually bear his name. In 1938 Congress and President Franklin D. Roosevelt redesignated the monument as a national park. Decades later the world took notice. In 1976 UNESCO designated Olympic an International Biosphere Reserve, and in 1981 it entered the World Heritage List. The progress from monument to park, and from park to world treasure, is the story of a landscape whose value was recognised slowly, and defended steadily.

Elk, Marmot and Salmon

The Elk That Made a Monument

The animal most closely tied to the park's history is the Roosevelt elk. It is the largest of the North American elk, native to the coastal forests of the Pacific Northwest, and it was in order to protect its calving grounds and summer range that Mount Olympus National Monument was created in 1909. Herds can be seen in the open bottomlands of the western valleys, especially in the rainforests, where the animals browse the understorey and keep the forest floor open and park-like. In autumn the bulls bugle in the mist, an eerie, whistling cry that carries across the valleys, and visitors who hear it on a still morning are not likely to forget it.

Species Found Nowhere Else

The long isolation of the Olympic peninsula, cut off by water and by successive glaciations, has allowed a number of species to evolve in their own way. The Olympic marmot, a stocky, sociable rodent of the high meadows that is found nowhere else, is the emblem of the alpine country, and plants such as Piper's bellflower and Flett's violet are found only in these mountains. It is this endemism, together with the ecological diversity of habitats from tide pool to glacier, that persuaded UNESCO to recognise the park under criterion (ix). The mountains are an island of evolution, even though they are connected to the mainland by land.

The River That Was Set Free

Two Dams on the Elwha

If one story captures the modern spirit of Olympic, it is that of the Elwha River. For most of the twentieth century two dams stood on the river within and beside the park: the Elwha Dam, 108 feet high, and the Glines Canyon Dam, 210 feet high, which together blocked the passage of fish on the lower river from 1911 until the early twenty-first century. A river that once saw some four hundred thousand adult salmon returning to about seventy miles of habitat saw fewer than four thousand, confined to less than five miles of river below the lower dam. All five species of Pacific salmon and several kinds of trout and char had been cut off from the mountain headwaters where their ancestors had spawned, and the Lower Elwha Klallam people, whose reservation lies at the river's mouth, had lost a part of their world.

A Bold Act of Restoration

The decision to remove the dams was one of the boldest acts of environmental restoration in American history. Work began in September 2011, and was completed in August 2014, with pauses to protect spawning fish from silt. About twenty-five million cubic yards of sediment had accumulated behind the dams, and as the reservoirs drained the river began to carry it down to the sea, rebuilding sandbars and, by late 2014, creating some seventy acres of new estuary. The process of recovery continues, and the Elwha has become a laboratory of ecological renewal studied around the world. It tells us that damage is not always final, and that a wild river, given back its freedom, can do much of the work of repair itself.

The Northern Gateway

Hurricane Ridge and the High Meadows

For most visitors the high country of the park begins at Hurricane Ridge, reached by a road of about eighteen miles from the town of Port Angeles on the northern shore. At an elevation of 5,242 feet the ridge offers a sweeping panorama of the central Olympics, a grand horizon of peaks, glaciers and sky, with the Strait of Juan de Fuca shining to the north. In summer the meadows are bright with wildflowers, and the paved Hurricane Hill trail, about 1.6 miles each way with a climb of roughly seven hundred feet, is one of the most rewarding short walks in the park. Snow can linger on the trails into July, and in winter a small, family-oriented ski area operates on the ridge.

The visitor centre at Hurricane Ridge, built in 1952, was destroyed by fire on 7 May 2023. The area reopened that June with a daily cap on private vehicles, though a project to rebuild the lodge has been delayed by budget constraints, and the traveller should check current conditions before setting out. It is a reminder that even the most beloved places in a park are fragile, and that the infrastructure of access is always at the mercy of fire and weather.

Protecting the Park in a Changing Climate

A Warming Mountain

The pressures on Olympic are real, and the clearest is climate change. The shrinking of the park's glaciers, from 266 in 1982 to 184 in 2009 according to the figures commonly cited, is the most visible evidence, and with it come changes in snowpack, river temperatures and the timing of floods. Salmon, so dependent on cold, clean water, are the first to feel them. Wildfire, once rare in the wet western forests, has become a growing concern, and the fire that destroyed the Hurricane Ridge lodge in 2023 was a sobering lesson in what can happen when a dry spell arrives.

Reflection

Standing in the Hoh on a wet morning, one has the odd sense of being inside a living organism, a body of water, wood and light that breathes in time with the weather. Everything here is alive, and everything is connected: the elk to the forest, the forest to the river, the river to the salmon, and the salmon, in the end, to the sea and to the people who have followed them for millennia. The mountains above are slowly dissolving into the ocean below, and the cycle of rain, ice and tide is the oldest clock the peninsula has.

What Olympic teaches is not a lesson in conquest but in attention. It asks us to look closely at moss, at the rings on a stump, at the line of young trees on the back of a nurse log, and to see in them the long patience of the living world. The world heritage designation of 1981 acknowledged that this patience belongs to all of humanity. It is up to us to honour it.

✦ Conclusion

Olympic National Park is a place of unusual completeness. Within a single boundary it holds the glacier and the rainforest, the elk meadow and the tide pool, the ancient village and the freed river, and each of these is a lesson in how the natural world works when it is allowed to. Its inscription in 1981, under criteria (vii) and (ix), acknowledged both its beauty and its ecological value, and the decades since have only deepened the reasons for that judgement, as the Elwha has run free again and the old forests have stood through another century of storms.

The park's future, like that of every wild place, depends on the choices of the people who love it. The glaciers will continue to shrink if the climate continues to warm; the forests will continue to grow if they are left alone; and the tribes whose ancestors have lived on this peninsula for millennia will continue to be its most faithful guardians. To visit Olympic is to step into a long conversation between land, water and people, and to realise that one is a guest in it.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

1. When was Olympic National Park inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List?

It was inscribed in 1981 as reference 151, under natural criteria (vii) and (ix). The park had been established in 1938, having begun as Mount Olympus National Monument in 1909, and it had been designated an International Biosphere Reserve in 1976.

2. Why is the Hoh Rain Forest so special?

It is a temperate rainforest in which annual precipitation is commonly given as 129 to 140 inches, dominated by Sitka spruce and western hemlock, and draped in mosses, lichens and ferns. It covers about twenty-four square miles and is often called the wettest forest in the contiguous United States. The Hall of Mosses trail, about 0.8 miles long, and the 1.2-mile Spruce Nature Trail offer easy introductions.

3. What happened on the Elwha River?

Two dams, the 108-foot Elwha Dam and the 210-foot Glines Canyon Dam, were removed between September 2011 and August 2014, restoring salmon and steelhead access to the upper river. It remains one of the largest dam-removal projects ever carried out, and the recovery of fish, sediment and estuary continues to be studied.

4. Which Indigenous nations are connected to the park?

The National Park Service lists eight tribes with ties to the park: the Lower Elwha Klallam, Jamestown S'Klallam, Port Gamble S'Klallam, Skokomish, Quinault, Hoh, Quileute and Makah. Many of them live on reservations on the peninsula's shores, and the park lies within their ancestral homelands.

5. What is the best way to see the park?

The park has no road crossing its interior, so visitors usually choose among several areas: Hurricane Ridge in the north, the Hoh and Quinault rainforests in the west, the Pacific coast beaches, and the northern lakes. A trip of several days is needed to see more than one. Allow for rain at any time of year, check road and weather conditions in advance, and note that the Hurricane Ridge visitor centre was destroyed by fire in 2023.

🧳 Visitor's Guide

Nearest Towns — Port Angeles (north), Forks (west) and Lake Quinault (south-west)

Opening Hours — The park is open 24 hours a day, year-round; some roads, campgrounds and facilities are seasonal

Entrance Fee — The National Park Service lists $30 per private vehicle, $25 per motorcycle and $15 per person on foot or bicycle (ages 15 and under free), each valid for seven consecutive days, with an Olympic annual pass at $55; fees change, so confirm before travelling

Best Time to Visit — Summer for Hurricane Ridge and the high country; spring and autumn for quieter rainforest walks; the rain can fall in any season

Hurricane Ridge — About 18 miles by road from Port Angeles; the visitor centre burned in May 2023, so check conditions before travelling

Hoh Rain Forest — Hall of Mosses trail (0.8 miles) and Spruce Nature Trail (1.2 miles) near the visitor centre

Road and Weather Information — The park's Road and Weather Hotline is (360) 565-3131

Conduct — Stay on marked trails, respect tribal lands, watch the tides on the coast, and keep a safe distance from wildlife
