Asante Traditional Buildings — Houses of Mud, Memory and the Gods

Houses That Outlived a Kingdom's Palaces

Drive east from Kumasi along the road to Accra and the city thins out quickly into a green country of cocoa trees, plantain groves, roadside kiosks and small towns whose names are known mainly to the people who live in them. Somewhere in the villages off that road, behind a modern shopfront or at the end of a red laterite track, stands a low building of the colour of the earth itself, its steep thatched or corrugated roof sheltering a courtyard and a wall whose surface is cut with patterns. Yet these modest buildings are what remains, in material form, of one of the most sophisticated states ever to rise in West Africa.

The Asante Traditional Buildings were inscribed on the World Heritage List in 1980, at the fourth session of the Committee, under a single criterion, the fifth, which is reserved for outstanding examples of a traditional settlement or form of building that represents a culture and has become vulnerable under the pressure of irreversible change. UNESCO's own summary describes them as the last material remains of the great Asante civilization, and the choice of words is sobering. The Asante court at Kumasi was famous across the nineteenth-century world for its palaces, its gold, its umbrellas and its ritual. Almost none of that architecture survives. What does survive is a group of ten shrines, called in older literature fetish houses, which owe their existence not to royal patronage but to the quieter persistence of ritual and village life.

This is a strange sort of World Heritage property, and an honest account of it must begin by saying so. It has no single gate, no skyline and no ruin that stirs the imagination from a distance. It is a scattering of small buildings in a living landscape, each one tended, when it is tended at all, by a caretaker, a priest or a village elder, and each one under real pressure from rain, termites, rising costs and changing belief. To visit one is to be reminded that heritage is sometimes less a monument than a practice, a continuing act of repair that can fail in a single bad season.

The Kingdom Behind the Walls

To understand why such buildings matter, one has to understand the kingdom that raised them. The Asante state grew in the forest country of central Ghana in the late seventeenth century, when a confederation of small Akan polities under the leadership of Osei Tutu, working closely with the priest and statesman Okomfo Anokye, was welded together around a powerful symbol. That symbol was the Golden Stool, which the Asante tradition holds to have descended from the sky, and which came to embody the spirit and unity of the whole people rather than the authority of any one ruler. In 1701, according to the usual chronology, Asante forces defeated their overlord Denkyira at the battle of Feyiase, and the kingdom entered the century of expansion that would make it a power from the forest to the Atlantic coast.

Kumasi, the capital, was the centre of an elaborate system of chiefs, officials, craftsmen and priests, and the wealth that flowed into it from gold, kola and trade made it a place of remarkable display. European visitors in the nineteenth century wrote with amazement of its wide streets, its great palace compounds and the carved and decorated walls of its royal buildings. Such descriptions tell us that the shrine houses now protected by UNESCO were once part of a far larger architectural culture, a culture in which the courtyard house, the relief wall and the symbolic pattern belonged to ordinary and royal life alike. Those readers who know the stone enclosures of Great Zimbabwe may find a useful contrast here, for the Asante built for the most part in earth, timber and thatch, materials that carry a culture's meaning just as fully as stone but that leave far less behind when a civilization is overthrown.

And overthrown, in the material sense, the Asante capital was. The British burned the Asantehene's palace in Kumasi during the war of 1874, and after the final conflict of 1900, often called the War of the Golden Stool, the kingdom was annexed, with the Ashanti Crown Colony declared in 1901 and the territory folded into the Gold Coast colony in 1902. The royal buildings of Kumasi did not survive these decades. What was left in the villages were the shrines, which belonged to the religious life of the people and which, for that reason, were sometimes spared the fate of the palaces. Wikipedia's account of the property even records that the royal mausoleum was destroyed in 1895 during the British expedition, a reminder that the loss of the capital's architecture was not an accident of time but a consequence of conquest.

A Courtyard Plan Built for Gods and Men

The form that unites the surviving buildings is the courtyard house. Each shrine, as UNESCO and the descriptive literature explain, is made up of rooms arranged around a central open court and raised on a plinth, with the rooms facing inward rather than out. In the classic plan there are four ranges, three used for the activities of ritual and living, the fourth a closed shrine room reserved for the deity and its priest. A visitor stepping into the courtyard therefore enters a space that is at once domestic and sacred, open to the sky yet enclosed on every side, a place where people could gather while the god remained, in a manner of speaking, at the head of the household.

The construction is as elegant as it is humble. A frame of timber and bamboo is filled and covered with mud plaster in the technique called wattle and daub, the walls built up thick and then smoothed by hand. Roofs were originally thatched, steep and deeply overhanging, which sheds the torrential rains of the forest zone and keeps the interior cool. Few of these roofs now survive in their original form, but where a visitor sees a thatched shrine, as at Besease, the effect is of a house that has grown out of the ground, its colours and textures borrowed directly from the red earth around it.

Such architecture is, in the best sense, a response to place. Mud walls breathe in the humidity and hold the temperature of a shaded room, while the raised plinth keeps the floors above the worst of the seasonal wet. The courtyard collects light and air. The thatch, bamboo and timber are all grown within a day's walk.

Walls That Speak: Relief, Symbol and Adinkra

The decoration of the Asante shrines is what first draws the attention of the visitor and what has made the buildings important to art historians. Their walls carry bas-relief designs modelled in the wet plaster, bold spirals and arabesques, animals, birds and plants, and geometric forms. UNESCO's description links many of these to the Adinkra symbols, the visual vocabulary of the Asante, which pack proverbs and values into compact signs, and which are today instantly recognisable in cloth, jewellery and graphic design far beyond Ghana. On a shrine wall they are not decoration in the modern sense but statements, passed down from one generation of builders and priests to the next.

The reading of such images is a subject on which scholars and tradition-bearers have written at length, and a post of this kind cannot claim to decode any single wall. What one can say is that the reliefs turn the building into a text. A spiral may suggest a quality of leadership or the winding of a path, a creature may recall a proverb about patience or power, and the composition on a wall may identify the god who is housed within.

Anyone interested in how architecture can carry belief will find a distant echo in the carved sanctuaries of the rock-hewn churches of Lalibela, though the Ethiopian monuments are cut from living stone and the Asante shrines modelled in earth. In both cases the sacred is not a matter of abstract doctrine but of walls, thresholds and hand-made surfaces that a worshipper can touch. The difference is fragility. Stone will wait out centuries of neglect, whereas a mud relief that is not maintained will dissolve within a few rainy seasons, and this single fact explains almost everything about the history of these buildings.

Ten Shrines, One Property

UNESCO inscribed the property as a group, and the list it publishes names ten shrines: Abirim, Asawase, Asenemaso, Bodwease, Ejisu Besease, Adarko Jachie, Edwenase, Kentinkrono, Patakro and Saaman. Most lie north-east of Kumasi, strung along or near the roads that lead out of the city, while Patakro stands to the south, in the Adansi country of the Ashanti Region. Spelling varies from one source to the next, since Akan names pass into English in many forms, and some tourist sources write Aduko Jachie or Kentikrono, but the sites are the same. A reader should also know that the older literature and UNESCO's own reports sometimes speak of thirteen buildings, because several shrine villages contain more than one structure, so the number of buildings and the number of components do not exactly coincide.

Each shrine has its own character. Besease, near Ejisu about twenty kilometres east of Kumasi, is the best known and the easiest to reach, and, according to the property's periodic report, has been open to visitors since 1999 and has a small site museum. One account gives its construction as around 1850 and its restoration as 1998, and it is the building that has been brought back most fully to a traditional appearance. Asawase lies within the urban sprawl of Kumasi itself and has become a focus for community work. Patakro, to the south, has recently been much in the news for the wrong reasons, as we shall see. The others, scattered in villages of farmers and traders, receive few visitors, and for some of them no visitor records exist at all.

What binds the ten is not a single design but a shared religious and social function. They are shrine houses, places where deities were housed and consulted, where priests and priestesses performed their duties, and, in some cases, where the dead of a ruling family were remembered. Several still serve their communities in that way, and this is a crucial point. They are not museum pieces. Their survival owes much to the fact that people continue to care about what happens inside them, and the traditional rhythms of maintenance, including the application of fresh clay on days the community regards as appropriate, are as much a part of the heritage as the walls themselves.

The Forest, the Rain and the Slow War on Mud

If the shrines of Ashanti face one enemy above all others, it is water. The climate of the forest zone brings torrential seasonal rains and long, humid spells, and a building of earth and timber is under constant attack. UNESCO's own periodic reporting lists mould on walls and wooden parts, termites in the thatch, lintels, doors and frames, storm damage to roofs, and erosion at the base of walls and in the courtyards. It is a catalogue of small, relentless injuries, none of which is dramatic and all of which, over time, are fatal.

The problem is made worse by changes in the surrounding economy and society. The reports note that deforestation, farming and building have reduced the supply of thatch, bamboo and good timber and raised their cost, so that a roofing material that was once gathered from the village edge must now be bought. At the same time, younger generations are less likely to learn the old building and maintenance skills, and the spread of new religions and new technologies has loosened the hold of the practices that kept the shrines alive. Illegal occupation of site boundaries and attempts to steal relics from the shrine rooms have also been reported.

The response, understandably, has often been practical rather than purist. UNESCO's authenticity assessment records that twelve of thirteen roofs have been replaced with corrugated iron, and that paved floors have replaced rammed earth. Corrugated metal lasts longer and requires less upkeep than thatch, but it changes the sound, the heat and the appearance of a shrine, and the compromise sits uneasily with a criterion that exists to protect a traditional form.

Custody, Neglect and the World Monuments Watch

The modern history of the buildings is the story of an institution trying to do a task too large for its means. According to the periodic report, the Ghana Museums and Monuments Board acquired the shrines in the years 1960 to 1970 and scheduled them as a National Monument. A report to the World Heritage Committee in 1996 records that most repair work took place in the 1960s and early 1970s, and that the economic downturn of the mid-1970s to the late 1980s slowed the programme drastically. The same report speaks of inadequate staffing and pay at the Board, damage from rain and insects, and the growing difficulty of finding traditional materials, and it describes the buildings, with unusual directness, as fragile. Bureau members asked for comprehensive conservation and management plans.

The World Monuments Fund, which has long worked with threatened earthen architecture, took up the case. It added the Asante shrines to its World Monuments Watch in 2012 and, with support from the United States Embassy in Ghana and the Ambassadors Fund for Cultural Preservation, helped carry out documentation and a condition assessment. In 2014 it returned for emergency repairs and for training in traditional skills, and held a community Watch Day at the Asawase shrine. The shrines were placed on the Watch again in 2022, a sign that the problem had not been solved. The Fund's own analysis is revealing: it notes that in the mid-twentieth century the work of maintenance passed from local custodians to a government body, that cheaper methods such as cement plaster and metal roofing replaced the old techniques, and that craftsmanship declined as a result.

That same year, in 2022, Ghana News Agency reported that the Patakro shrine was near collapse. Its site manager described collapsed walls at the old palace, damaged roofs at the shrine and mausoleum, eroded reliefs and repeated burglaries, and called for the rebuilding of walls, the replacement of roofs, the repair of reliefs and better doors for security. It is worth being precise about what this does and does not mean. As far as the sources consulted for this article show, the Asante Traditional Buildings are not on UNESCO's List of World Heritage in Danger, but they have been named repeatedly as at risk by the World Monuments Fund, and the evidence of the periodic report is that management plans, boundaries, a buffer zone and technical staff are all still needed.

Visiting the Shrines

Visiting the Asante Traditional Buildings requires a different mindset from the one that serves at the great monuments, and rewards patience. The sensible starting point is Besease, which is near Ejisu on the Kumasi to Accra road and is served by public transport from Kumasi. It has a small museum, explanatory text in English on its walls and a caretaker who can lead visitors through the courtyard. One recent traveller's review reported an entry fee of only a few cedis, and another noted that the guardian is not always on site and may have to be fetched from the village, so a visit goes best with a driver or guide who knows the area and a small amount of local goodwill.

For those with time, the other shrines are worth seeking out, ideally with the help of the Ghana Museums and Monuments Board's regional office in Kumasi, which supervises the sites and can say which are open and who holds the key. These are working religious buildings, so visitors should dress modestly, follow the guardian's instructions about where to walk and what may be photographed, and ask before entering any room. One study cited by travellers' sources also reports that women may not enter during menstruation, a restriction that deserves respect, as do all the customs of a place that is still sacred to the people around it.

It is also worth pairing the shrines with the city that gave them their meaning. In Kumasi, the Manhyia Palace and its museum tell the story of the Asante kings. The palace there was built in 1925 and presented to Prempeh I on his return from exile, and the current royal institution carries the Asante tradition forward in unbroken form. Seen together, the palace and the shrines show how a kingdom's two kinds of memory, the one royal and public and the other village and sacred, have survived into the twenty-first century. Travellers who go on to the coast may then continue the story at the Atlantic island of Gorée in Senegal, a reminder that the gold, the guns and the captives that shaped Asante history also shaped the entire Atlantic world.

Reflection

The Asante Traditional Buildings are low, small, earthen and dispersed, and they would be unimpressive to anyone looking for a grand facade. Their significance lies elsewhere, in the fact that they are the last pieces of a built culture whose greatest monuments were destroyed, and in the fact that they are still inhabited by belief. They invite us to ask what we mean by authenticity when a building is rebuilt every few years by the hands that use it, and whether a replaced roof or a repaired wall makes a place less true or more alive.

The most hopeful part of the story is that the skills still exist. The training programmes of recent years, the attention of the World Monuments Fund and the quiet work of caretakers suggest that the shrines might be kept as living architecture, as their builders intended, rather than frozen as relics. Whether they will be depends on decisions about funding, about management plans and about the place of traditional knowledge in modern Ghana. For the traveller, the lesson is simple: go, look carefully, and leave the place a little better regarded than you found it.

✦ Also Read

Great Zimbabwe National Monument, Zimbabwe — Another African statecraft written in architecture, this time in dry stone, where the walls of a medieval trading capital still enclose the memory of a kingdom. Read alongside the Asante shrines, it shows how differently African societies built their power, in stone in one place and in earth and symbol in another.

✦ Conclusion

The Asante Traditional Buildings are the small survivors of a large story. The kingdom that created them was among the most powerful in pre-colonial West Africa, with a capital that dazzled visitors and a political culture that centred on the Golden Stool. When its palaces were burned and its territory annexed, the shrines of the villages were what remained, and in 1980 UNESCO recognised them as the last material remains of the Asante civilization, under criterion (v).

Their future is far from secure. Rain, termites, rising costs for traditional materials, shrinking knowledge and the sheer difficulty of managing ten scattered properties have left several in a poor state, and the World Monuments Fund has twice placed them on its Watch. Yet the shrines are also a place where heritage is still practised rather than merely displayed, and that is a reason for hope as well as concern. To visit them with respect, to support the work of the Ghana Museums and Monuments Board and its partners, and to value the hands that keep them standing, is to take part in the long tradition of repair that has carried them through to the present.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

1. When were the Asante Traditional Buildings inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site?

They were inscribed in 1980 as a cultural property under criterion (v). Their UNESCO reference number is 35.

2. How many buildings are there?

UNESCO's statement lists ten shrines as components of the property: Abirim, Asawase, Asenemaso, Bodwease, Ejisu Besease, Adarko Jachie, Edwenase, Kentinkrono, Patakro and Saaman. Some UNESCO documents and local sources speak of thirteen buildings, because several sites have more than one structure, and the sources are not fully consistent.

3. Are the Asante Traditional Buildings on the List of World Heritage in Danger?

Not according to the sources consulted for this article. They have, however, been listed on the World Monuments Watch (2012 and 2022), and the Patakro shrine was reported to be in a poor state of conservation in 2022.

4. Which shrine is best to visit?

Besease, near Ejisu about 20 kilometres east of Kumasi, is the easiest to reach and the most visitor-ready. It has been open to visitors since 1999 and has a small site museum, though opening arrangements should be checked locally.

5. What are the buildings made of?

They are built of timber and bamboo framing covered with mud plaster in the wattle-and-daub technique, around courtyards, with roofs that were originally thatched. Many roofs have been replaced with corrugated iron, and their walls carry bas-relief decoration related to Adinkra symbols.
