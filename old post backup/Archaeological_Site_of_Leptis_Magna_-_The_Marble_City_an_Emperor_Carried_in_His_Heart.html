Archaeological Site of Leptis Magna — The Marble City an Emperor Carried in His Heart

A Phoenician Trading Port on the Libyan Coast That Became the Home of Septimius Severus and the Most Splendid Roman City of Africa, Preserved by Drifting Sand, Admired by Travellers and Now Watched Over by Its Own Neighbours on the List of World Heritage in Danger | UNESCO World Heritage Site

A City at the Edge of the Sand

The road from Tripoli runs east along a coast of olive groves, low white houses and the occasional gleam of the sea, and then, quite without ceremony, the ruins begin. There is no medieval wall to announce them and no hill to crown, only a sudden opening of the landscape in which columns of grey and rose marble rise from the pale ground as though a forest had been petrified in the act of growing. This is Leptis Magna, the Great Leptis, and for anyone who has walked the Forum of Rome or the avenues of Ephesus the first sensation is one of quiet disbelief. Here, on the edge of the Sahara, in a country few travellers now reach, stands one of the best-preserved Roman cities in the entire Mediterranean world.

What makes Leptis so affecting is the way it is arranged. The ancient city was built on a gentle rise at the mouth of a seasonal river, the Wadi Lebda, and its principal monuments are spread along that watercourse and the old harbour at its foot. The visitor wanders from the arch to the forum, from the forum to the baths, and each stage of the walk seems to belong to a different age of the same ambition. The columns, the veneers and the sculptured details are largely of imported marble, brought at astonishing expense by a city that wished the world to understand how rich it had become.

Yet this is not simply a Roman place set down upon African soil. Beneath the paving of the imperial city lies an older, Punic town, and the emperor who gave Leptis its supreme moment of glory was a native son who spoke the Punic language in his childhood. The site is a meeting of civilisations, where Carthaginian merchants, Roman governors and Byzantine soldiers each left their mark. UNESCO recognised this in 1982 when it inscribed the site on the World Heritage List under criteria (i), (ii) and (iii), describing a Phoenician trading port that grew under Roman rule and was embellished by the Severan emperors into one of the most beautiful cities of the empire.

The Phoenician Landfall

The Trading Port of Lpqy

Long before the first Roman soldier saw the Libyan coast, seafarers from the Levant had learned its harbours. The Phoenicians, whose cities of Tyre and Sidon sent merchant ships the length of the Mediterranean in search of metals, ivory and markets, planted small trading posts along the shores of what is now Tunisia and Libya, and the mouth of the Wadi Lebda was an obvious place for one. The Punic name of the settlement, written Lpqy, survives in inscriptions, and the later Greek and Latin forms Leptis and Lepcis descend from it. The Punic city is usually said to have been founded in the later seventh century before Christ, an outpost on the long sea road between the Phoenician heartland and the western colonies, with Carthage as its most powerful neighbour.

The city appears briefly in Greek history around 515 BC, when the Spartan prince Dorieus, according to Herodotus, tried to found a colony nearby and was driven out by the local Libyans and the Carthaginians. Leptis, however, learned to survive between its stronger neighbours. It lay in the fertile coastal belt of Tripolitania, where olives flourished.

When Carthage fell in 146 BC, Leptis did not share its fate. It became a tributary ally of Rome, governed by its own Punic magistrates and enjoying an unusual degree of freedom. By the time of Julius Caesar's African campaign in 46 BC, the city was obliged to deliver a vast annual levy of olive oil, some three million pounds of it according to the tradition, a figure which tells us more about the agricultural wealth of the hinterland than any list of monuments could.

A Punic Town Learns the Roman Language of Stone

The Augustan Transformation

The real metamorphosis began under Augustus. In the closing decades of the first century before Christ, the wealthier citizens of Leptis, many of them still bearing Punic names, began to build in the Roman manner, and they did so at their own expense, as proud benefactors who wished to see their city take its place among the cities of the empire. The old forum took shape with its temples dedicated to Roman deities and to the traditional gods of the town, among them Liber Pater and Hercules, who in this part of the world are the Roman guises of the Punic Shadrapa and Melqart. A market was laid out with inscriptions in both Punic and Latin, and the theatre rose beside the harbour, financed by Annobal Tapapius Rufus, whose double name embodies the doubled culture of his city so perfectly that no historian could have invented it.

Leptis was never wholly Romanised nor wholly Punic; it was both at once for generations, and the inscriptions continued to employ the local language long after the formal change of status. The city became a municipium in the sixties of the first century AD and was raised to the rank of a colony under Trajan, a promotion that gave its leading families Roman citizenship and a seat among the governing classes of the empire. An arch in honour of Trajan, which still stands, marks the occasion.

Under Hadrian, in the early second century, a great bathing complex was constructed close to the old centre of the city, one of the largest of its kind in Africa and a monument to the Roman conviction that cleanliness and sociability were among the foundations of civilised life. Even in ruins, the Hadrianic Baths suggest the scale of the municipal pride that would soon be eclipsed by a single and far greater impulse.

The Emperor from the Wadi

Septimius Severus and the Making of a Capital

In the spring of 145 a child was born in Leptis Magna to a family of local aristocracy, the descendants of Roman settlers on one side and of Punic nobility on the other. His name was Lucius Septimius Severus, and he would become the first emperor of African birth. He grew up speaking Punic as his first language, learned Latin and Greek as the languages of education and government, and is said to have retained a faint accent of his native city for the rest of his life. In 193, in the chaos that followed the murder of the emperor Pertinax, the legions on the Danube hailed him emperor, and he founded a dynasty. His wife, Julia Domna, came from the Syrian city of Emesa, and the Severan court therefore combined the two ends of the empire's southern shore, a connection that recalls the great Syrian capital described in our account of the Ancient City of Damascus.

Severus never forgot Leptis. He visited the city around 203, with his wife and sons, and the visit was the signal for a building programme of almost unimaginable scale. He is recorded as having granted the city the privilege of Italian law, which exempted it from imperial taxes, and imperial funds poured into the town to create a new city centre fit for the emperor's birthplace. The harbour was enlarged, a colonnaded street was driven from the sea towards the heart of the city, and a new forum and a monumental basilica were laid out beside it. The works continued after his death in 211, at York, and were finished by his sons.

It is worth pausing to consider what this meant for the ancient inhabitants. A provincial town of respectable size was suddenly turned into a capital in all but name. Estimates of its population in the early third century run as high as a hundred thousand, though such figures are uncertain. The marble that reached the quayside came from the great quarries of the empire, and the sculptors who worked it appear to have come from Asia Minor, above all from the workshops of Aphrodisias. The Severan quarter therefore carries the imprint of the eastern Mediterranean as vividly as it does of Rome.

The Arch, the Forum and the Basilica

Stone as Propaganda

The Severan Arch is the monument that most visitors carry in memory. Built about 203, apparently for the emperor's African journey, it is a four-way arch, or tetrapylon, standing at the crossing of the two principal streets of the city. Eight Corinthian columns frame its faces, each of which carried a great frieze. The panels show a triumphal procession, a sacrifice, a ceremonial handshake of concord between emperors, and the chariot of Severus himself with his sons Caracalla and Geta at his side. In its original form the arch must have been a most dazzling statement of dynastic confidence. After it fell in the centuries of decline, some of its sculpture is said to have been carried away and reused in a Byzantine church of Justinian's time. It was found buried and broken in 1928, and the Italian archaeologists of the day rebuilt it piece by piece.

A short walk away lies the Severan Forum, an enormous rectangle of paved open space enclosed by colonnades whose arches were decorated with the heads of Medusa, serpent-haired and wide-eyed, a motif that has become one of the emblems of Leptis. At the northwestern end of the forum rose a temple; at its southwestern side stood the great basilica, a hall designed for the administration of justice and the conduct of commerce, its interior divided into a nave and aisles by tall columns of imported stone and finished with a spacious apse at either end.

The basilica is among the finest buildings of its kind to survive in Africa. The piers that framed its apses carry carved decoration of astonishing richness, winding vine-scrolls inhabited by animals and figures, and scenes of the labours of Hercules and of Dionysus, in which the sculptors showed all their skill. Long after the pagan world had passed away, the building was converted into a church under the Byzantines. The carving is so crisp, even now, that in raking light the vine leaves seem to tremble.

The Pleasures of a Roman Town

Theatre, Baths, Market and Arena

Ancient cities were not merely administrative machines; they were stages for public life, and Leptis was equipped with all the instruments of Roman civic pleasure. The theatre, built in the opening years of the first century AD and paid for by a notable named Annobal Tapapius Rufus, stands in a curve of the land close to the sea, and its auditorium looks out across the water, so that spectators might raise their eyes from the performance to the horizon. Beneath its stones archaeologists found a necropolis of a much older date, the burials of the Punic town that preceded the Roman city. The theatre therefore sits above the dead of the earlier city, as though the present had been built on the shoulders of the past.

Close to the shore lie the famous Hunting Baths. Their reputation rests on a remarkable circumstance: the baths were swallowed by drifting sand, and the dunes that buried them also protected their structure, so that their vaulted rooms, including a barrel-vaulted cold room, domed chambers and cross-vaulted plunge baths, survive in a state that is exceptional for an African monument. A fresco of a leopard hunt in an apse of the cold room gives the building its popular name. The baths show that even in this remote province the Roman engineers could build vaults and domes of a sophistication that would not be out of place in Rome itself.

The market of Leptis, with its pair of octagonal pavilions and its stone counters, tells the story of the city's daily commerce, and nearby are carved the standard units of measurement that governed trade, a delightful reminder that imperial architecture rested on the humble business of weighing out grain and oil. In a hollow east of the city, perhaps once a quarry, stands the amphitheatre, dated to the age of Nero, and beside it the long arena of the circus, where chariots raced on days of festival.

The Harbour and the River

Engineering Against the Wadi

The prosperity of Leptis rested upon its port, and the port rested on a problem. The Wadi Lebda is a seasonal river which can swell in a few hours into a violent torrent, carrying silt and gravel down to the harbour mouth. The Severan engineers recognised the danger, and the harbour basin they built was artificial, with breakwaters, quays and warehouses, accompanied by works that diverted the wadi's floods and sediment. UNESCO itself cites the ancient port and its regulating structures as a notable example of Roman engineering. The great design succeeded for a time, but the silt eventually defeated the city's sea defences. As trade declined and the labour of maintenance faltered, the harbour slowly filled, and a city whose existence depended on ships found itself without a landing place.

The story of the harbour is therefore the story of the city's decline. In the third century the empire entered a long period of political and financial instability, and trade slackened. In the fourth century the town suffered earthquakes, and large parts of it were gradually abandoned. Diocletian, in his reorganisation of the empire, made it a provincial capital once more, and for a moment the old splendours returned. But the great days could not be recalled.

In 439 the Vandals took the city from the Romans, and their king Gaiseric ordered its walls to be demolished so that it could not serve as a base for resistance. A Berber raid in 523 sacked the place, and when the armies of the Byzantine empire returned in 533 and 534, they found a shrunken and wounded town, which they surrounded with a new and much smaller wall. By the time the Arab conquest reached the coast in the middle of the seventh century few people remained, and the great buildings lay empty, with the wind and the sand moving gradually among them.

Sand, Rediscovery and the Italian Excavations

A City Preserved by Its Own Burial

The dunes saved Leptis. For more than a thousand years the wind-blown sand piled steadily against the standing walls and over the fallen columns, until the greater part of the city lay under a sandy blanket that protected it from stone robbers, lime burners and farmers. UNESCO's own assessment states that the integrity and authenticity of the site are high. The ruins were not entirely forgotten; European travellers described them in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, and columns were carried away in this period to adorn palaces and parks. Part of an ancient temple went to the British Museum in 1816 and was set up in 1826 among the ornamental ruins at Fort Belvedere, near Windsor.

The systematic excavation of Leptis began in the 1920s, under the Italian administration of Libya. Renato Bartoccini directed the early campaigns, beginning in 1925 and 1926, and the arch of Septimius Severus was among the first monuments to be brought to light. The work has since been criticised, for it served the political ambitions of a colonial power which wished to present itself as the heir of imperial Rome. Yet without the excavators' efforts the city would be less intelligible, and the present form of the site, with its upright columns and readable streets, owes a great deal to their labours.

Discoveries have continued. In 2000, archaeologists found a series of mosaics near the Wadi Lebda, and these were kept quiet for several years to protect them from looters before being made public in 2005. The episode reveals the double life of the site in the modern age: an inexhaustible treasury for scholars, and a fragile one, tempting to thieves and always in need of vigilance.

A Heritage in Danger

Protecting Leptis Magna in an Uncertain Age

Libya has endured a long period of turmoil since the uprising of 2011, and its heritage has not been untouched. In that year there were reports that forces loyal to the old regime had used the ruins to hide military vehicles, and the site might have been struck. After 2016, local people organised themselves to protect the ruins, a remarkable act of civic stewardship. In the same year the World Heritage Committee placed Leptis Magna, together with the other Libyan properties, on the List of World Heritage in Danger, where it remains today.

The threats are many. Political instability has limited the funds and the expert personnel available for conservation, and the urban growth of Khoms presses towards the ancient boundary. UNESCO notes that the site has no formal buffer zone; one has been outlined and is under preparation. The ancient city that once defeated the Wadi Lebda with engineering must now be defended by patient administration and by the goodwill of those who live beside it.

The visitor should also be realistic about travel. The British Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office, in advice updated on 21 July 2026 and still current on 18 October 2026, advises against all travel to Libya except to a short list of named areas (Tripoli, Misrata, and the eastern cities of Benghazi, Al-Bayda and Derna with the surrounding Jebel Akhdar), and against all but essential travel even to those. Leptis Magna and Khoms are not on that list, so the stricter advice against all travel applies.

Reflection

Leptis Magna teaches an unusual lesson in the relationship between power and place. An emperor was born here, and when he came to command the world he did not forget the small port of his boyhood but dressed it in marble. The Severan city was an act of love as much as an instrument of propaganda, and it is difficult to stand in the colonnaded forum without feeling something of the strange tenderness which the building programme expresses. That the city faded so soon after its great moment, its harbour choked by silt and its streets reclaimed by sand, makes the tenderness all the more poignant.

There is a further lesson in the fate of the ruins. The sand that buried the city also preserved it; the neglect that ended its prosperity guaranteed its survival. Heritage depends upon accidents and upon the choices of ordinary people, from the ancient masons to the modern townspeople who kept watch over the arches in a time of confusion. To remember Leptis is to be reminded that the great monuments of humanity are held in trust, and that the trust is renewed, or broken, in each generation.

✦ Conclusion

Leptis Magna earns its place on the World Heritage List because it preserves, as few other sites can, the whole life of a Roman port city: its Punic origins, its Augustan embellishment, its Severan zenith and its slow decline. The arch, the forum, the basilica, the theatre, the baths and the harbour are not isolated monuments but the parts of a planned and living urban organism, and their survival in the shelter of the sand makes the site a document of rare completeness. The criteria under which it was inscribed, for artistic achievement, for influence on later architecture and for exceptional testimony to a vanished civilisation, are easily understood by anyone who sees it.

Its future, however, is not secure. Since 2016 the site has stood on the List of World Heritage in Danger, and the task of protecting it has fallen to a small number of Libyan archaeologists, local guardians and international partners who work under difficult conditions. The best hope for Leptis lies in a settled peace, in the establishment of its buffer zone and in the patient continuation of conservation. Until then, it remains a monument to what a small African harbour could become, and to the fragile gratitude of an emperor for the place where he was born.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

1. When was Leptis Magna inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site?

Leptis Magna was inscribed in 1982 as a cultural property under criteria (i), (ii) and (iii), with UNESCO reference number 183. The inscribed area is 387.485 hectares.

2. Who founded Leptis Magna?

It began as a Phoenician and later Punic trading settlement, usually dated to the seventh century BC, though accounts differ. It came under Roman influence after the fall of Carthage in 146 BC, became a municipium in the mid-first century AD and a colony under Trajan.

3. Why is Septimius Severus important to Leptis Magna?

Severus was born at Leptis in 145 and became emperor in 193. He visited around 203 and lavished imperial funds on the city, which received a new forum, a great basilica, an enlarged harbour and a triumphal arch. Much of what the visitor sees today dates from his time or that of his sons.

4. Why is Leptis Magna on the List of World Heritage in Danger?

The site was added to the danger list in 2016 amid the instability in Libya, which has limited conservation resources and monitoring. It remains on the list.

5. Can I visit Leptis Magna today?

Travel to Libya is strongly discouraged by many governments. The UK Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office, in advice updated on 21 July 2026 and still current on 18 October 2026, advises against all travel to Libya except to named areas, which are themselves subject to advice against all but essential travel. Leptis Magna is not among them. Anyone considering a visit should consult up-to-date official advice and the site authorities first.
