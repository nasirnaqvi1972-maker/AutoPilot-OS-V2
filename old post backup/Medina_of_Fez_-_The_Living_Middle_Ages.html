The City That Time Forgot to Change

There is a paradox at the heart of Fez that strikes every visitor who descends into its ancient lanes for the first time: a city of approximately 150,000 people, living in the twenty-first century, with mobile phones and satellite dishes and the full apparatus of modern Moroccan life, inhabiting a physical urban structure — streets, buildings, water systems, craft workshops, religious institutions — that has remained essentially unchanged in its fundamental character for a thousand years. Fez is not a museum. It is a living city. But it is a living city in which the forces of modernisation that have transformed virtually every other medieval urban centre in the Islamic world have been, for a complex of economic, political, and cultural reasons, largely held at bay.

The Medina of Fez — specifically the old city of Fes el-Bali, the older of the city's two historic urban cores — is the best-preserved medieval Islamic city in the world. Its street network, laid down in the ninth and tenth centuries and modified only marginally since, is a labyrinth of approximately 9,000 lanes, alleys, cul-de-sacs, and passages — some of them barely wide enough for two people to pass — that no vehicle wider than a donkey can navigate. Its buildings — the mosques, the madrasas, the fondouks (caravanserais), the hammams (bathhouses), the private houses with their inward-facing courtyards — are, in many cases, substantially as they were built or last significantly modified in the medieval period. Its craft workshops — the tanners, the weavers, the potters, the leather workers, the wood carvers, the metalworkers, the embroiderers — continue to produce, by largely traditional methods, the same products they have produced for centuries.

UNESCO inscribed the Medina of Fez on the World Heritage List in 1981, under criteria (ii) and (v), recognising it as an outstanding example of a traditional human settlement and land-use which is representative of a culture — the medieval Islamic urban tradition — that has become vulnerable under the impact of irreversible change. The inscription was a recognition of something that the residents of Fez had always known: that their city was extraordinary, that what had survived here had not survived anywhere else, and that its survival could not be taken for granted.

The Foundation — Idris I and the Birth of Fez

The foundation of Fez is attributed to the Idrisid ruler Idris I — the founder of the first Islamic dynasty in Morocco and a descendant of the Prophet Muhammad through his grandson Hassan — who established a settlement on the eastern bank of the Oued Fez in approximately 789 CE. His son, Idris II, expanded the settlement significantly after 808 CE, founding the city of Fes el-Bali on the western bank and attracting settlers from the great centres of the Islamic world — approximately 8,000 families from Kairouan in Tunisia and approximately 2,000 families from Córdoba in Andalusia, according to the medieval chronicles, their different cultural and architectural traditions fusing in the new city into the distinctive Moroccan-Andalusian synthesis that characterises Fassi (Fez-ian) culture to this day.

The early medieval period was the first golden age of Fez. In 859 CE, the Kairaouine Mosque — the spiritual and intellectual centre of the city, and, in the reckoning of UNESCO and many historians, the oldest continuously operating university in the world — was founded by Fatima al-Fihri, the daughter of a wealthy merchant from Kairouan. The university of the Kairaouine attracted scholars from across the Islamic world and beyond — the Jewish philosopher Maimonides is said to have studied here, as did the historian Ibn Khaldun — and established Fez as one of the great intellectual centres of the medieval Islamic world, a role it maintained for centuries.

The Kairaouine Mosque and University

The Kairaouine Mosque is the largest mosque in Africa and, in the opinion of many scholars, the oldest continuously operating university in the world — a claim that is disputed by some historians but that reflects the genuine antiquity and the genuine educational function of the institution, which has been teaching Islamic law, theology, rhetoric, grammar, and the traditional sciences without interruption since its foundation in 859 CE. The mosque's current form is the product of multiple expansions and renovations over the centuries — the Almoravid Sultan Ali ibn Yusuf undertook a major expansion in the twelfth century that established its current dimensions — and it is capable of accommodating up to 22,000 worshippers.

Non-Muslims are not permitted to enter the Kairaouine Mosque, but its presence permeates the entire Medina: the sound of the Quranic recitation that drifts from its precincts at prayer time, the students in traditional dress moving to and from its libraries and lecture halls, the booksellers and scribes in the surrounding lanes who service its scholarly community — all of these are reminders that the Kairaouine is not merely an historical monument but a functioning institution of Islamic learning, still teaching, still producing graduates, still playing its role in the intellectual life of the Islamic world that it has played for more than eleven centuries.

The Souks — A Medieval Economy Still Functioning

The souk system of Fez is one of the most extraordinary survivals of medieval economic organisation anywhere in the world. The great souks of the Medina are organised by craft and trade — each occupying its own quarter, its workshops and retail stalls concentrated in specific streets and lanes according to a system of spatial organisation that was established in the medieval period and has been maintained, with modifications, ever since. The organisation reflects both practical logic — craftsmen of the same trade benefit from proximity to their suppliers and their customers, and from the regulatory oversight of the guild system — and a medieval Islamic hierarchy of purity and prestige that determined the physical location of each trade within the city.

The most celebrated of the Fez craft traditions — and the one that draws the most visitors — is the tannery. The Chouara Tannery, the largest and oldest of the three surviving tanneries in the Medina, has been operating in essentially the same location, with essentially the same methods, since the eleventh century. Its stone vats — filled with natural dyes of poppy, indigo, henna, saffron, and cedar — are visible from the surrounding terrace levels of the leather goods shops, the skins of sheep, goat, camel, and cow passing through the soaking, scraping, and dyeing processes that transform raw hides into the distinctive Fassi leather whose soft texture and rich colours have made it famous across the Islamic world and beyond for a thousand years.

The contrast between the pre-industrial craft methods of the tannery — the workers standing in the dye vats up to their thighs, moving the skins by hand, the process unchanged in its essentials since the medieval period — and the smartphones and delivery motorbikes of the surrounding city is one of the most vivid expressions of the paradox of Fez: a city that has managed, against all odds and against all the pressures of modernity, to keep its medieval craft economy alive as a functioning system rather than a tourist spectacle.

The Madrasas — Stone Books of Islamic Learning

The madrasas of Fez are among the finest examples of medieval Islamic architecture in the world — religious schools built around central courtyards of extraordinary decorative richness, their interiors covered from floor to ceiling with the three-layered decorative scheme that characterises Moroccan architectural tradition at its finest: zellige tilework at the base, carved plaster stucco in the middle, and carved cedarwood at the top.

The Bou Inania Madrasa — built between 1350 and 1355 by the Marinid Sultan Abu Inan — is the largest and most elaborately decorated of the Fez madrasas, and one of the finest buildings in Morocco. Its central courtyard, entered through a monumental gate of carved cedarwood and bronze, is a composition of extraordinary spatial and decorative refinement: the central pool reflecting the carved surfaces above it, the play of light and shadow on the stucco and the wood creating a constantly changing visual effect, the sound of the water and the call to prayer from the madrasa's own minaret combining in an acoustic environment of great beauty and serenity.

The Attarin Madrasa, adjacent to the Kairaouine Mosque, is smaller but arguably more perfect in its proportions — a courtyard of exceptional elegance in which every surface is covered with decoration of extraordinary quality, executed by craftsmen who were at the absolute peak of the tradition they had inherited from their medieval predecessors.

Reflection — The City That Chose Itself

Fez is, among other things, a statement about the relationship between a city and its own identity. Other Moroccan cities — Casablanca, Rabat, Marrakech — have modernised, expanded, and been partially transformed by the forces of the twentieth and twenty-first centuries. Fez has modernised too — it has its new city, built by the French in the colonial period, its modern infrastructure, its universities and hospitals and airports. But the Medina of Fez — the old city, the medieval heart — has maintained a character and a way of life so distinctive that it constitutes, in the words of the UNESCO inscription, a traditional human settlement representative of a culture that has become vulnerable under the impact of irreversible change.

The reason for this survival is not merely poverty or neglect — though both have played a role in limiting the pace of change. It is also, genuinely and substantially, a choice: the choice of the residents of the Medina, the scholars of the Kairaouine, the craftsmen of the souks, and the religious and civic institutions of the city to maintain their distinctive way of life and their distinctive built environment against the pressures of modernisation. It is not a perfect choice, and it is not a choice without costs. But it is a choice that has preserved, for the world, something that cannot be recreated once it is lost: the living experience of what a great medieval Islamic city actually looked and sounded and smelled and felt like, in its streets and its souks and its mosques and its madrasas, every day.

✦ Conclusion

The Medina of Fez is one of the most remarkable places on Earth — not for its individual monuments, extraordinary as those are, but for the totality of the experience it offers: the experience of walking in a medieval city that is still alive, still inhabited, still operating as a functional urban system after more than a thousand years. The lanes of Fes el-Bali are the same lanes that medieval scholars walked on their way to the Kairaouine. The craft workshops are the workshops of craftsmen whose techniques and whose spatial organisation were established in the same period. The mosques and the madrasas still call the faithful to prayer, still teach the students of Islamic learning, still anchor the spiritual and intellectual life of a city that has been at the centre of Moroccan civilisation for more than eleven centuries.

UNESCO recognised this in 1981. The recognition was warranted — and it was also, in a real sense, urgent: the forces that have transformed every other medieval Islamic city in the world have not spared Fez, and the preservation of the Medina requires continuous effort, continuous investment, and continuous commitment from the Moroccan state, the international conservation community, and the residents of the Medina themselves. What is being preserved is not merely a collection of beautiful buildings. It is a way of life, a way of organising urban space and economic activity and religious devotion and intellectual pursuit, that has almost entirely disappeared from the rest of the world. It deserves everything that is being done to protect it, and more.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: Why is the Medina of Fez so well preserved? The preservation of the Medina of Fez is the result of a combination of factors: the narrow street network that has always prevented vehicular traffic and large-scale redevelopment; the economic marginalisation of the old city during much of the twentieth century, which limited the resources available for demolition and replacement; the resilience of the traditional craft economy; and the cultural and religious attachment of the city's residents to their built environment and their way of life.

Q2: Is the Kairaouine the world's oldest university? The Kairaouine Mosque and University, founded in 859 CE, is claimed by UNESCO and by many historians to be the oldest continuously operating university in the world. This claim is disputed by scholars who argue that the medieval institution was primarily a mosque and Quranic school rather than a university in the modern sense, but its antiquity and its continuous educational function are beyond dispute.

Q3: What is zellige tilework? Zellige is a form of mosaic tilework consisting of hand-cut geometric pieces of glazed terracotta tile arranged in complex patterns. It is one of the defining elements of Moroccan architectural decoration, used extensively on the floors, walls, and fountains of mosques, madrasas, palaces, and other important buildings. The zellige workshops of Fez are among the most respected in Morocco.

Q4: Can non-Muslims visit the mosques and madrasas of Fez? Non-Muslims are not permitted to enter the Kairaouine Mosque. The major madrasas — Bou Inania, Attarin, Cherratine — are generally open to non-Muslim visitors, though some may require a small entrance fee and appropriate dress. The Bou Inania Madrasa is also unusual among Fez religious buildings in being open to non-Muslims for part of the day.

Q5: How do you navigate the Medina of Fez? Navigation in the Medina of Fez is notoriously challenging — the approximately 9,000 lanes of Fes el-Bali are not all mapped, signage is limited, and it is easy to become disoriented. A licensed local guide is strongly recommended for a first visit. GPS and maps are useful supplements but not substitutes for local knowledge. Getting lost is, in any case, one of the more rewarding ways to experience the Medina.
