The Cliff That Became a Cathedral

There is a basalt cliff in the Charanandri Hills of Maharashtra, in the Deccan plateau of central India, that was transformed — between approximately 600 and 1000 CE — into the most extraordinary concentration of rock-cut religious architecture on Earth. The Ellora Caves are not caves in any natural sense. They are thirty-four temples, monasteries, and shrines carved by human hands from a single two-kilometre-long escarpment of volcanic basalt, working inward from the cliff face and downward from the cliff top, removing hundreds of thousands of tonnes of rock to create spaces of extraordinary architectural and sculptural ambition.

What makes Ellora unique in the history of religious art — and what UNESCO recognised when it inscribed the site on the World Heritage List in 1983 — is not merely the scale of the rock-cut architecture or the quality of the sculpture, though both are outstanding. It is the fact that the thirty-four excavations represent three different religious traditions — twelve Buddhist caves (approximately 600–800 CE), seventeen Hindu caves (approximately 600–900 CE), and five Jain caves (approximately 800–1000 CE) — carved at the same site over a period of four centuries, in apparent coexistence and in mutual artistic dialogue. Ellora is the most powerful surviving physical evidence that the India of the early medieval period was a place of religious plurality and artistic exchange — a place where Buddhist monks, Hindu craftsmen, and Jain patrons worked side by side on the same cliff face, their different theological convictions expressed in different architectural and sculptural vocabularies but united by a shared mastery of the medium of cut rock.

The site is dominated, in the visual and architectural imagination of every visitor, by one structure above all others: the Kailasa Temple — Cave 16 in the conventional numbering — the largest monolithic rock-cut structure in the world, carved in the eighth century CE by the Rashtrakuta king Krishna I as a monument to Lord Shiva and to the cosmic mountain of Kailash in the Himalayas, the abode of Shiva himself. Readers who have explored our post on the Imperial Palaces of the Ming and Qing Dynasties in China will find in the Kailasa Temple a parallel ambition — both are monuments of a scale and a conception that required not merely skill but a vision of extraordinary boldness, a willingness to commit to a project whose completion lay beyond the horizon of any individual human lifetime.

The Buddhist Caves — The Earliest Excavations

The Buddhist caves of Ellora — numbered 1 to 12 in the conventional sequence, though the numbering does not correspond precisely to the chronological order of excavation — represent the earliest phase of the site's development, dating from approximately the sixth to the eighth centuries CE. They are, in the main, viharas — monasteries, their basic form a large central hall surrounded on three sides by cells for individual monks, with a shrine chamber containing a large image of the Buddha at the far end. The scale of the Buddhist caves increases over time: the earliest excavations are relatively modest, while the later Buddhist caves — particularly Cave 5, a large columned hall used as a communal dining hall, and Caves 11 and 12, multi-storeyed excavations of considerable architectural ambition — demonstrate a growing confidence in the manipulation of the rock-cut medium.

Cave 10 — the Vishvakarma Cave, named for the divine craftsman of the Hindu tradition — is the most architecturally spectacular of the Buddhist excavations: a large chaitya (prayer hall) with a horseshoe-shaped facade of great elegance, its interior dominated by a large seated Buddha in the teaching mudra, the vaulted ceiling carved with simulated wooden ribs in an extraordinary translation of the structural logic of a timber building into the non-structural medium of carved rock. The carved ribs serve no structural purpose — the ceiling is solid rock — but they demonstrate the continuity of the Indian rock-cut tradition with the timber architecture that preceded it, the craftsmen carving in stone the forms that their predecessors had assembled in wood.

The Hindu Caves — The Great Sculptural Achievement

The seventeen Hindu caves of Ellora — numbered 13 to 29 — represent the most sculptural phase of the site's development, their walls covered with relief carvings of exceptional quality and exceptional scale depicting the mythology of the Hindu tradition: the great narratives of Shaivite and Vaishnavite religion, the battles of gods and demons, the cosmological dramas of creation and destruction that animate the Puranic literature of classical Hinduism. The Hindu caves range in date from approximately the sixth to the ninth centuries CE and represent the work of craftsmen working under the patronage of multiple successive dynasties — the Chalukyas, the Rashtrakutas, and others — each contributing to the accumulating density of sculptural achievement on the cliff face.

Cave 16 — the Kailasa Temple — is the culmination and the masterpiece of the Hindu phase, and the supreme achievement of the entire Ellora complex. It was not carved inward from the cliff face, as all the other Ellora excavations were, but downward from the cliff top: three large trenches were cut into the top of the basalt plateau, isolating a rectangular mass of rock approximately 84 metres long and 47 metres wide and 33 metres deep, and then the mass itself was carved from the top down into the complete architectural complex of the Kailasa Temple — the entrance gateway, the Nandi pavilion, the main shrine tower (shikhara) rising 29 metres, the pillared halls, the subsidiary shrines, the courtyard elephants, the gallery of divine figures on the surrounding walls — all emerging from the single rock mass as the craftsmen worked downward and inward, removing approximately 200,000 tonnes of basalt in a process that is estimated to have taken approximately 100 years and involved the labour of thousands of craftsmen across multiple generations.

The Kailasa Temple is approximately 1.5 times the area of the Parthenon in Athens. It contains some of the finest Hindu sculpture produced in the early medieval period — the large panels depicting scenes from the Ramayana and the Mahabharata in the surrounding galleries, the magnificent image of Ravana shaking Mount Kailash while Shiva and Parvati look on with expressions ranging from alarm to serenity, and the great image of Shiva as Nataraja, Lord of the Dance, whose dynamic form anticipates the bronze Nataraja figures that would become the most celebrated image of Hindu art in subsequent centuries.

The Jain Caves — The Final Phase

The five Jain caves of Ellora — numbered 30 to 34 — represent the latest phase of the site's development, dating from approximately the ninth to the eleventh centuries CE, a period when Jainism was enjoying significant royal patronage in the Deccan under the Rashtrakuta and Yadava dynasties. The Jain caves are smaller in scale than the great Hindu excavations but of exceptional decorative refinement — their interiors covered with carvings of extraordinary delicacy, their ceilings elaborately decorated with floral and geometric patterns of great complexity.

Cave 32 — the Indra Sabha — is the finest of the Jain excavations and one of the most beautiful interiors at Ellora: a two-storeyed excavation whose upper storey is a large open court with a free-standing monolithic shrine at its centre, the surrounding walls carved with images of the Jain Tirthankaras (enlightened beings) in an iconographic programme of great theological precision. The carved lotus on the ceiling of the inner shrine — a single flower of extraordinary complexity, every petal individually carved, the whole composition reading as a meditation on the Jain concept of the cosmos as an infinitely detailed organism of perfect order — is one of the supreme achievements of Indian decorative carving.

Three Faiths on One Cliff — The Meaning of Ellora

The coexistence of Buddhist, Hindu, and Jain excavations at a single site — worked over four centuries in apparent mutual tolerance and in artistic dialogue — is the most historically significant aspect of Ellora, and the aspect that UNESCO's inscription under criterion (vi) specifically recognised: the site is directly associated with ideas of outstanding universal significance, specifically the idea that different religious traditions can coexist, exchange artistic vocabularies, and produce works of outstanding universal value in proximity and without conflict.

The artistic dialogue between the three traditions is visible in specific formal parallels across the excavations: the translation of the Buddhist vihara form into the Hindu cave shrine; the adoption of the Buddhist narrative relief tradition by Hindu and Jain sculptors; the shared use of the dvarapalas (door guardians) as a threshold marker across all three traditions. The three communities of craftsmen working on the same cliff face were not working in isolation from each other. They were in conversation — absorbing, adapting, and transforming each other's formal solutions in a process of artistic exchange that produced, in the end, a site of greater richness than any single tradition could have achieved alone.

Reflection — The Rock That Became Religion

Ellora is, ultimately, a demonstration of what happens when a tradition of extraordinary technical skill is placed in the service of an equally extraordinary religious conviction — when the craftsmen who carve the rock are not merely executing a brief but expressing a faith, and when that faith is sufficiently powerful and sufficiently specific to drive the carving of 200,000 tonnes of basalt in the service of a single architectural vision. The Kailasa Temple is the supreme expression of this combination: a monument of such ambition and such technical achievement that it remains, after twelve centuries, one of the most extraordinary things that human hands have ever made.

The UNESCO inscription of 1983 recognised Ellora as a masterpiece of human creative genius, as an outstanding example of the cultural exchange between religious traditions in the India of the early medieval period, and as a site directly associated with ideas of outstanding universal significance. All three recognitions are deserved. Ellora is one of those places that confirms, in the most direct possible way, that the capacity for the creation of extraordinary beauty is not the property of any single culture or any single tradition, but a human universal — expressed here, in the basalt of the Deccan plateau, with a completeness and a conviction that few other places on Earth can match.

Conclusion

The Ellora Caves are one of the supreme achievements of human religious art — a monument not to a single faith or a single dynasty or a single period but to the extraordinary creative energy of the Indian subcontinent in the early medieval centuries, expressed in the most demanding medium available to the craftsmen of the time: the solid basalt of the Deccan plateau, carved by hand, over four centuries, into thirty-four temples, monasteries, and shrines of outstanding beauty and outstanding historical significance. To visit Ellora — to stand in the courtyard of the Kailasa Temple and look up at the carved gallery above, or to sit in the dim interior of Cave 10 and watch the light change on the carved ribbed vault above the seated Buddha — is to encounter one of the great concentrated acts of human artistic faith available anywhere in the world. UNESCO was entirely right to inscribe it in 1983. It deserves far more visitors than it receives.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: How many caves are at Ellora?

Ellora contains 34 rock-cut excavations in total — 12 Buddhist caves (approximately 600–800 CE), 17 Hindu caves (approximately 600–900 CE), and 5 Jain caves (approximately 800–1000 CE). They extend along a two-kilometre basalt escarpment in the Charanandri Hills of Maharashtra.

Q2: What is the Kailasa Temple?

The Kailasa Temple — Cave 16 at Ellora — is the largest monolithic rock-cut structure in the world, carved downward from the top of a basalt plateau by the Rashtrakuta king Krishna I in the eighth century CE. Approximately 200,000 tonnes of basalt were removed over an estimated 100 years to create the complete temple complex dedicated to Lord Shiva.

Q3: Is Ellora a UNESCO World Heritage Site?

Yes. The Ellora Caves were inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1983, recognised under criteria (i), (iii), and (vi) as a masterpiece of rock-cut architecture and sculpture representing three religious traditions — Buddhism, Hinduism, and Jainism — in artistic coexistence and dialogue.

Q4: How far is Ellora from Aurangabad?

Ellora is approximately 30 kilometres northwest of Aurangabad in Maharashtra, accessible by road in approximately 45 minutes. Aurangabad is also the nearest city to the Ajanta Caves, approximately 100 kilometres to the north — the two sites are commonly combined in a single visit.

Q5: What is the best time to visit Ellora?

October to March (cool dry season) offers the most comfortable visiting conditions. The site is open every day except Tuesdays. Early morning is recommended to visit the Kailasa Temple before the crowds arrive and in the best light.
