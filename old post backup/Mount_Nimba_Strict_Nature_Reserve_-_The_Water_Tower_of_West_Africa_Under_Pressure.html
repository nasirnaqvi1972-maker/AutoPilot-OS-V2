Mount Nimba Strict Nature Reserve — The Water Tower of West Africa Under Pressure

Mount Nimba Strict Nature Reserve — The Water Tower of West Africa Under Pressure

A Mountain Range on the Borders of Guinea, Côte d’Ivoire and Liberia That Rises Abruptly Above the Savannah, With Forest on Its Slopes, Grassy Summits Above, About Fifty Springs and Rare Species Such as the Live-Bearing Toad and Chimpanzees That Use Stones as Tools, Protected for Decades but Threatened by Iron-Ore Mining and Listed Among World Heritage in Danger | UNESCO World Heritage Site

Location:Border region of Guinea and Côte d’Ivoire in West Africa, at the point where the two countries meet Liberia, in the Nimba Range of the Guinea Highlands
Year of UNESCO Inscription:1981 (Guinea), extended 1982 (Côte d’Ivoire); on the List of World Heritage in Danger since 1992
Category:Natural
UNESCO Reference:155 bis
Criteria:(ix)(x)
Coordinates:Approximately 7°36′N 8°23′W
Area:17,540 hectares (12,540 hectares in Guinea and 5,000 hectares in Côte d’Ivoire)

A Mountain Standing Above the Forest

West Africa is mostly low and flat. Its great rivers wander across plains, and its forests spread over gentle hills. Then, on the borders of Guinea, Côte d’Ivoire and Liberia, the land suddenly stands up. The Nimba Range rises abruptly from the surrounding savannah and forest to a summit of 1,752 metres, a long ridge of rock and grass where the clouds gather and the climate changes. Its flanks are covered in dense forest, its summits in open mountain pasture, and its slopes are laced with streams.

UNESCO describes it as harbouring an especially rich flora and fauna, with endemic species such as the viviparous toad and chimpanzees that use stones as tools. It is also a veritable water tower, with about fifty springs feeding the rivers of the region. For the people of the surrounding lowlands it is a source of water and a sacred landmark; for scientists it is one of the most important mountain ecosystems in West Africa.

Mount Nimba is also one of the most contested World Heritage sites in Africa. Beneath its grass and forest lies some of the richest iron ore in the world, and the temptation to mine it has hung over the mountain for more than half a century. It has been on the List of World Heritage in Danger since 1992. This post tells the story of the mountain, its extraordinary wildlife and the long struggle between conservation and extraction.

Where Three Countries Meet

Mount Nimba sits at a tripoint. The range straddles the borders of Guinea, Côte d’Ivoire and Liberia, and the World Heritage property covers the parts in Guinea and Côte d’Ivoire, a total of 17,540 hectares, of which 12,540 lie in Guinea and 5,000 in Côte d’Ivoire. UNESCO notes that the property includes almost the whole of the Nimba massif in the two countries, and it forms part of a larger transboundary landscape that continues into Liberia.

The highest peak is Mount Richard-Molard, at 1,752 metres, the highest point in both Guinea and Côte d’Ivoire. The range is relatively narrow, a long chain of crests and ridges, but it rises so steeply from the surrounding plains that it creates its own weather. The range is shared between the two countries, and the property was drawn to protect the massif as a single ecological unit.

The Water Tower

The mountain’s hydrological role is one of its greatest values. The cool, moist climate of the heights, with clouds, mist and heavy rains, feeds around fifty springs that give rise to streams and rivers flowing to both Guinea and Côte d’Ivoire, and the upper basin of the Cavally River lies in the Nimba region. These waters sustain villages, farms and forests far below, which is why the mountain is often called a water tower.

The Nimba Range is also remarkable for its microclimatic diversity. UNESCO and its advisers describe a sub-equatorial montane climate, with sharp differences between the sheltered, humid valleys, the exposed ridges and the cool summits. The variety of conditions explains the variety of life, and each microclimate shelters its own community of plants and animals.

From Forest to Grassland

The vegetation of Nimba changes with altitude. At the foot of the range there is lowland rainforest and, on the plains, savannah. As one climbs, the forest changes in character, becoming montane forest, with trees festooned in moss and ferns, and higher up it gives way to open grassland, where tough grasses and low shrubs grow on the thin, windswept soils of the crests.

This mosaic of forest and grassland is a major reason for the reserve’s richness. UNESCO’s criterion (ix) recognises Nimba as part of the rare mountain chains of West Africa, rising abruptly above a rolling panorama and giving way to forested plains at the lower altitudes, a landscape that records the ecological and biological processes that shape life on the continent.

The Toad That Gives Birth

Among the most unusual residents of Nimba is the viviparous toad, Nimbaphrynoides occidentalis. Unlike most toads, which lay eggs in water, the females of this tiny species give birth to fully formed young, an adaptation that allows them to live in the cool, high grasslands without ponds. UNESCO notes that the toad is critically threatened with extinction because its breeding area is very reduced, and that it lives only in high-altitude habitats.

Its survival is closely tied to the mountain’s upland grasslands, which are the habitat most exposed to mining. Any disturbance of the summit areas, from clearing to the building of roads and spoil heaps, could wipe out a species that exists nowhere else, which is why the toad has become a symbol of the stakes of the Nimba debate.

Otter Shrews, Bats and Hidden Mammals

The mammals of Nimba include several found almost nowhere else. UNESCO names the Nimba otter shrew, or Micropotamogale of Mount Nimba, Micropotamogale lamottei, a small, semi-aquatic mammal that hunts in the mountain streams, as one of the threatened species of special interest. The Nimba range is also the home of Lamotte’s roundleaf bat, a rare species known from very few places.

Other animals include several species of duiker, the small forest antelopes, as well as big cats, civets and a variety of monkeys, birds and insects. Studies have listed around 600 butterfly species in the Nimba Mountains and surrounding forests, and new species continue to be discovered, a sign of how much remains to be learned about this mountain.

Chimpanzees That Use Stones as Tools

One of the most celebrated features of the Nimba region is its population of West African chimpanzees that use stones as tools. Around the village of Bossou, in the Guinean part of the wider Nimba landscape, chimpanzees crack open oil-palm nuts using a stone as a hammer and another as an anvil, a technique that young animals learn by watching their elders. The behaviour is one of the best-documented examples of animal tool use and cultural transmission.

The Bossou chimpanzees have been studied by scientists for decades, and their number is very small, a reminder of how fragile such traditions are. UNESCO names the stone-using chimpanzees among the remarkable populations of the property. The Bossou hills form part of the wider Biosphere Reserve rather than the strictly protected core, and the group depends on the continued protection of the forest corridors that link it with Nimba.

Protected Since the 1940s

Nimba has a long history of protection. In the 1940s, under French colonial rule, the northern part of the range was placed under strict protection as a nature reserve, a status that was inherited by Guinea and Côte d’Ivoire after independence. In 1980 the Guinean part was recognised by UNESCO as a Biosphere Reserve, and in 1981 the Guinean section was inscribed on the World Heritage List under criteria (ix) and (x).

In 1982 the Ivorian part of the massif was added to the property, in order to preserve the integrity of the whole. A strict nature reserve is a highly protected category in which activities are limited to scientific research and monitoring, and tourism is formally prohibited. The designation reflects how seriously the mountain was valued by those who first understood its importance.

The Iron Mountain

The trouble is the rock beneath. The Nimba Range contains some of the richest iron-ore deposits in the world, and for decades mining companies have wanted to exploit them. On the Liberian side of the range, large-scale iron mining has taken place for decades. In Guinea, a project to exploit a deposit that lay within the area that was inscribed on the World Heritage List in 1981 was planned in the 1980s, and in 1989 the World Bank decided not to finance it, a decision taken on economic grounds.

The Committee soon recognised the danger. In 1992 it inscribed the property on the List of World Heritage in Danger, citing the threat of the iron-ore concession in Guinea and the arrival of large numbers of refugees in the region, which put pressure on the forests. The property has remained on the Danger List ever since, longer than almost any other natural site in Africa.

The Committee’s Position on Mining

The World Heritage Committee has taken a firm line. In 2021 it reiterated its position that mining exploration or exploitation is incompatible with World Heritage status, a policy supported by the International Council on Mining and Metals, whose members have committed not to undertake such activities in World Heritage properties. It has repeatedly asked Guinea to carry out comprehensive environmental and social impact assessments for proposed projects and to submit them for review by IUCN before any approvals are given.

The projects in question include the Nimba Iron Ore Project of the Société des mines de fer de Guinée, adjacent to the property, the Zali Mining iron ore project, and the Nimba Development Company project, which was at an early stage. In 2024 the Committee regretted that Guinea had not provided information on the approved terms of reference for the Zali impact assessment, and reiterated that assessments must meet the highest international standards.

The 2026 Decision

At its 48th session in Busan in 2026, the Committee again decided to maintain Mount Nimba on the List of World Heritage in Danger. It emphasised that, given the property’s Danger List status, the major impacts identified in the environmental assessment for the first phase of the Société des mines de fer de Guinée project raise serious concerns about the prospects of removing the property from the Danger List.

It asked Guinea not to issue any operating permits or environmental compliance certificates for that project until the Committee’s earlier requests have been met: a comprehensive assessment covering all phases of the project and the cumulative effects of other mining projects in the landscape, as well as a strategic environmental assessment of the entire landscape. The message was clear: the future of Nimba cannot be decided one mine at a time.

The Ivorian Side and the Road Out of Danger

On the Ivorian side, conservation work has continued under the Office Ivoirien des Parcs et Réserves. In 2024 the Makoré Project began financing the management costs of five protected areas, including the Mount Nimba Reserve, for two years. In December 2023 a workshop held in Conakry with UNESCO’s support assessed the desired state of conservation needed for the property to be removed from the Danger List and produced a costed action plan.

A joint mission by the World Heritage Centre and IUCN visited both countries in 2019, and donors including the European Commission, the Government of China and the German Commission for UNESCO have supported conservation in the region. The challenge is to turn these efforts into a clear path out of danger, one that requires cooperation between two governments, local communities and the mining industry.

People, Villages and Visiting

The mountain is surrounded by farming communities that depend on the forest for land, water and fuel, and conflicts between local needs and strict protection have been a long-standing difficulty. Successful conservation depends on involving communities, offering alternatives to forest clearing and ensuring that the benefits of protection reach the people who live beside it.

Tourism is formally prohibited within the strict nature reserve itself, but limited ecotourism takes place around the Bossou hills, where visits to the chimpanzees and excursions toward the summit have been organised with local research and conservation bodies. Access is difficult, facilities are basic and conditions change, so anyone considering a visit should seek current advice from authorised organisations and not attempt to enter the reserve independently.

Reflection — What a Mountain Is Worth

Nimba poses a question that every generation of conservationists has to answer: what is a mountain worth? The iron beneath it has a price that can be calculated in jobs, revenue and steel. The toad, the otter shrew and the chimpanzees, the springs and the cloud forest, have a value that is harder to count but no less real. The World Heritage Committee has insisted that some places are too important to be traded for a mine, but the decision rests with governments and the pressures they face.

The same tension runs through the story of many natural heritage sites, from the unique wildlife of the Galapagos Islands, Ecuador to the great wildernesses of Yellowstone National Park, USA. In each, the challenge is to find ways for people to prosper without destroying the very things that make a place irreplaceable.

✦ Conclusion

Mount Nimba is a mountain that matters far beyond its size. Its springs water the lowlands, its forests and grasslands shelter species found nowhere else, and its stone-tool chimpanzees show the depth of animal culture. Protected since the 1940s and inscribed on the World Heritage List in 1981, it has also been on the Danger List since 1992 because of the threat of iron-ore mining.

Its future is still open. The Committee’s 2026 decision shows that the international community will not accept mining in a World Heritage property without the most rigorous scrutiny, and the efforts of the two governments, rangers and local communities offer a basis for hope. Mount Nimba deserves to be remembered as a place where conservation prevailed.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

Why is Mount Nimba a World Heritage Site?

It was inscribed in 1981 (Guinea), with an extension in 1982 (Côte d’Ivoire), under criteria (ix) and (x) for its outstanding biodiversity and mosaic of tropical habitats. It protects forest and highland grassland, about fifty springs and rare species such as the viviparous toad, the Nimba otter shrew and chimpanzees that use stones as tools.

Why is it on the List of World Heritage in Danger?

It has been on the Danger List since 1992 because of the threat of iron-ore mining in the Guinean part of the property and, at the time of listing, the arrival of large numbers of refugees. The Committee has repeatedly stated that mining is incompatible with World Heritage status.

What is the viviparous toad?

The Nimba viviparous toad, Nimbaphrynoides occidentalis, is a tiny species whose females give birth to live young. It lives only in high-altitude habitats on Mount Nimba and is critically threatened with extinction.

How big is the reserve?

The property covers 17,540 hectares, of which 12,540 hectares lie in Guinea and 5,000 hectares in Côte d’Ivoire. The highest peak is Mount Richard-Molard at 1,752 metres.

Can I visit Mount Nimba?

Tourism is formally prohibited inside the strict nature reserve. Limited ecotourism, including visits to the chimpanzees of Bossou, has been organised in the surrounding area. Access is difficult, so seek current advice from authorised organisations before planning any visit.
