A Temple Cut Into the Earth

On the outskirts of Valletta, in the ordinary streets of the town of Paola, a modest entrance leads to one of the most extraordinary monuments of prehistoric Europe. Below the pavement, on three levels reaching about ten metres underground, lies a maze of chambers, passages and halls carved entirely from soft limestone. This is the Ħal Saflieni Hypogeum, a complex that functioned for centuries as a sanctuary and a burial place, and which is widely described as the only known prehistoric underground temple in the world.

UNESCO inscribed the Hypogeum in 1980 under criterion (iii), recognising it as an exceptional testimony to a vanished civilisation. Its carved chambers date from roughly 3600 to 2500 BCE, making it contemporary with the great temples of Malta and older than the pyramids of Giza. The name comes from the Greek hypogeion, meaning underground, and the Maltese place name Ħal Saflieni refers to the village that once stood nearby.

To enter is to cross a threshold in time as well as in space. The air is cool and still, the walls are smooth and curved, and the only sounds are the hushed voices of visitors. It is a place that seems built for silence, and its atmosphere has moved those who have experienced it for more than a century.

Neolithic Malta — An Island of Temple Builders

The first settlers reached the Maltese islands from Sicily around 5900 BCE, bringing domesticated animals, cereals and pottery. Over the following millennia their descendants developed an unusual culture, farming the thin soils, raising livestock and, from around 3600 BCE, building monumental stone temples. Sites such as Ġgantija on Gozo and Tarxien, Ħaġar Qim and Mnajdra on Malta are among the oldest freestanding stone structures in the world, older than Stonehenge by more than a thousand years.

The temples share a distinctive plan of curving apses, or lobes, arranged around a central axis, built of huge limestone blocks and decorated with spiral reliefs and carved animals. The people who built them apparently had no metal and no written language, yet organised the labour to cut, move and raise stones weighing many tonnes. Figurines of broad-hipped, seated or standing figures, often called fat ladies, suggest a ritual world in which fertility, the body and the cycle of life and death were central.

The Hypogeum belongs to the same culture and the same beliefs. It is, in effect, the temple turned inside out: instead of raising stone upward, the builders dug downward, using the soft globigerina limestone of the island to create spaces that mimic the forms of the above-ground temples. In doing so they made something that is without close parallel in the prehistoric world.

Discovery in 1902 and the Work of Father Magri

For nearly five thousand years the Hypogeum was forgotten. In 1902, builders digging a cistern for new houses in Paola broke through the roof of an upper chamber, and found themselves looking into a vast underground space. They seem to have sold some of the finds and carried on building, even incorporating the entrance into a house, and it was only gradually that the importance of the discovery was recognised.

The excavation was begun in 1903 by Father Manuel Magri, a Jesuit priest and pioneering Maltese archaeologist, who spent several years clearing the chambers of earth, bones and debris. After his death in 1907, the work was carried on by Themistocles Zammit, the founder of Maltese archaeology, who completed the clearance and published the results. Their work produced a wealth of finds and a surprising discovery: that the chambers had held the remains of thousands of people.

Unfortunately, early excavations were not conducted by modern standards, and much information about the context of the finds was lost. Many of the bones were handled carelessly or discarded, and a significant part of the contents was never properly recorded. Even so, the Hypogeum was recognised as a prehistoric monument of the first importance, and it has been studied and protected ever since.

Three Levels, Thirty Chambers — The Architecture of the Underworld

The Hypogeum extends over three levels, each carved at a different period and connected by passages and steps. The upper level, the oldest, is closest to the surface and consists of a series of rock-cut chambers that were probably used for burial. The middle level, the most elaborate, contains the great halls, decorated rooms and false façades that give the monument its fame. The lower level, reached by a narrow stair, extends about ten metres below the street and includes small storage and burial chambers.

Imitating the Temples in Living Rock

The builders did not merely excavate rooms; they sculpted architecture. In the main chambers they carved the soft rock to imitate the megalithic temples above ground, with a trilithon doorway of two uprights and a lintel, false corbelled ceilings of overlapping courses, and pillar-like forms. All of this was done with tools of flint, stone and antler, since metal was not yet available, a feat that required immense patience and skill. Nowhere else can the visitor so clearly see how Maltese temple architecture was conceived and how it related to the beliefs of its builders.

Painted Spirals and the Oracle Room

The most beautiful chamber of the Hypogeum is the so-called Decorated Room, whose ceiling and walls are covered with spirals and circles painted in red ochre. The designs flow across the stone in curving, organic patterns, some resembling honeycomb and others branching like trees. They are among the earliest known paintings in Malta, and rank among the finest surviving examples of Neolithic art in the Mediterranean.

Another of the Hypogeum’s marvels is the Oracle Room, a small chamber with a niche in the wall that seems to amplify sound dramatically. A low voice speaking into the niche resonates throughout the complex, a deep, booming echo that has led archaeologists to suggest that the room was used for ritual pronouncements, perhaps by a priest or priestess speaking to the assembled community. Acoustic studies have found that the room resonates at frequencies in the range of the human male voice, and some researchers have proposed that the effects of sound and darkness may have played a part in ritual experience.

Whether or not these interpretations are right, the combination of painted ceilings, carved architecture and extraordinary acoustics suggests a sophisticated conception of sacred space. The builders understood that a ritual place is not only seen but heard and felt, and they made the Hypogeum an environment in which sound, light and darkness worked together.

The Dead, the Sleeping Lady and the Meaning of the Place

The Hypogeum was not only a temple but a necropolis. Excavations revealed the remains of thousands of individuals, usually estimated at more than seven thousand, deposited over many centuries, often disarticulated and heaped in chambers and niches. The dead were probably placed in the Hypogeum after the flesh had decayed elsewhere, and their bones were gathered together, suggesting a communal approach to burial in which the ancestors became a collective presence.

Among the finds, the most famous is the Sleeping Lady, a small terracotta figurine only about twelve centimetres long, depicting a woman lying on her side on a couch, her large hips and thighs rendered with great softness. Whether she represents a goddess, a priestess or a worshipper in a state of sleep or trance remains unknown, but she has become a symbol of Malta’s prehistoric culture. The original is displayed in the National Museum of Archaeology in Valletta.

Other finds include pottery, beads, amulets and small carved figures, and the details of the rock-cut chambers themselves. Together they suggest that the Hypogeum was a place where the living and the dead were closely connected, and where rites of burial, memory and renewal were conducted. It was a threshold between worlds, and perhaps between sleep and death.

Fragile Beauty — Conservation and Visiting

The Hypogeum is extremely fragile. The soft limestone is sensitive to humidity and temperature, and the breath and body heat of visitors raise levels of moisture and carbon dioxide that can damage the paintings and encourage the growth of micro-organisms. After decades of increasing visitor numbers, the monument was closed in the 1990s for conservation work and reopened in 2000 with a strictly controlled system of climate management and limited access.

Today only a small number of visitors are allowed in each day, in carefully organised groups, and tickets must be booked well in advance, often weeks or months ahead. Photography is not permitted inside, and visitors are asked to follow the guide’s instructions closely. These restrictions can seem frustrating, but they are the price of preserving a monument that would otherwise deteriorate rapidly.

Heritage Malta, the national agency responsible for the site, has also developed a visitor centre and interpretation facilities, helping visitors understand the Hypogeum’s context before and after their descent. Those who are unable to secure a ticket can gain some sense of the place from the exhibits in the National Museum of Archaeology and from the nearby Tarxien Temples, which belong to the same culture.

Reflection — Descending to the Roots of Belief

There are monuments that impress by height and others by depth, and the Hypogeum belongs firmly to the second kind. Its builders chose to dig down rather than build up, and in doing so they created a space that feels like an answer to a very old human question: where do the dead go, and how do the living remain in contact with them? The answer they gave, in the soft glow of a painted ceiling and the deep resonance of a voice in the dark, is one of the earliest and most moving that we possess.

It is also a reminder of how much human creativity has been lost with the passing of time. The people who carved these chambers left no names and no written word, yet their work speaks to us with surprising directness. To stand in the Oracle Room and hear one’s own voice transformed into a deep echo is to share, for a moment, in a ritual that was old when Egypt was young.

✦ Conclusion

The Ħal Saflieni Hypogeum is one of the most remarkable monuments of prehistoric Europe: an underground temple and necropolis, carved without metal tools, painted with red ochre spirals and shaped to resonate with the human voice. It is a unique survival from a culture that produced the oldest freestanding stone architecture in the world.

Visiting it requires planning and patience, but the reward is an experience that is difficult to forget. In the cool darkness beneath a busy Maltese town, one meets the beliefs, skills and imagination of people who lived five thousand years ago, and who made of the earth itself a place of ritual and memory.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

Why is the Ħal Saflieni Hypogeum a UNESCO World Heritage Site?

UNESCO inscribed it in 1980 under criterion (iii) as an exceptional testimony to the prehistoric culture of Malta. It is the only known prehistoric underground temple, a masterpiece of rock-cut architecture and ritual art.

How old is the Hypogeum?

The chambers were carved over many centuries, from about 3600 BCE to about 2500 BCE, during Malta’s Temple Period. This makes the complex roughly five thousand years old, older than the pyramids of Giza.

What is the Oracle Room?

It is a chamber with a niche in the wall that amplifies and resonates low-pitched sounds, so that a voice speaking into it echoes through the Hypogeum. Archaeologists suggest it may have been used for ritual speech or ceremonies.

Can visitors take photographs inside?

No. Photography is not allowed inside the Hypogeum, to protect the fragile paintings and to keep conditions stable. Visits are guided, in small groups, and visitors must follow instructions closely.

How do I get tickets?

Tickets are managed by Heritage Malta and are limited in number, so they should be booked online well in advance, often weeks or months ahead, especially in the high season. Check the official website for current availability and rules.

🧳 Visitor's Guide

Getting There — The Hypogeum lies in Paola, about 5 kilometres south-east of Valletta, and is reached by bus or taxi; it is close to the Tarxien Temples

Booking — Tickets are limited and must be reserved in advance through Heritage Malta; book as early as possible, particularly in summer

Group Size — Visits are in small guided groups for a limited time

Photography — Not permitted inside the Hypogeum; photographs are allowed in the visitor centre where indicated

What to Wear — Comfortable shoes and a light layer; the interior is cool, and the steps are steep and narrow

Accessibility — The stairs and low passages make the site difficult for some visitors; check access information before booking

Combine With — The National Museum of Archaeology in Valletta, the Tarxien Temples and the temples of Ħaġar Qim and Mnajdra
