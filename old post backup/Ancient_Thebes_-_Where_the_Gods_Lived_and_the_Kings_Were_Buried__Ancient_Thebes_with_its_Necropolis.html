The City the Greeks Called the City of a Hundred Gates

Homer called it the city of a hundred gates — Thebes, the greatest city of ancient Egypt, its name resonating through the literature of the ancient world as a byword for wealth, for power, and for the accumulated grandeur of a civilisation that had been building temples and tombs and palaces on the banks of the Nile for more than a thousand years before the Greeks arrived to be astonished by what they found. The ancient Egyptians called it Waset, or simply Niut — the City. At its height, in the period of the New Kingdom (approximately 1550 to 1070 BCE), it was the capital of the most powerful empire in the ancient world, the religious centre of the Egyptian state and the burial ground of the pharaohs whose names — Tutankhamun, Ramesses II, Seti I, Hatshepsut, Thutmose III — have become, through the archaeology of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, among the most recognisable in human history.

The modern city of Luxor occupies the site of ancient Thebes, on both banks of the Nile in Upper Egypt approximately 670 kilometres south of Cairo. The east bank — the side of the living, the side of the rising sun — was the side of the city, the temples, and the palaces. The west bank — the side of the dead, the side of the setting sun — was the side of the necropolis: the Valley of the Kings, the Valley of the Queens, the mortuary temples of the pharaohs, and the villages of the craftsmen who spent their lives cutting and decorating the royal tombs. Together, the east and west bank monuments of ancient Thebes constitute the greatest concentration of ancient Egyptian heritage anywhere on Earth — a density of temples, tombs, and sculptural achievement unmatched in the entire ancient world.

UNESCO inscribed Ancient Thebes with its Necropolis on the World Heritage List in 1979, under criteria (i), (iii), and (vi), recognising it as a masterpiece of human creative genius, as an outstanding testimony to an ancient civilisation, and as a site directly associated with events, ideas, and beliefs of outstanding universal significance. The inscription was entirely justified, and it understates, if anything, the significance of what stands here on both banks of the Nile. Readers who have explored our post on Babylon, Iraq will recognise in ancient Thebes a parallel ambition — both were the capitals of great ancient empires, both left behind architectural legacies of extraordinary scale, and both have been transformed by archaeology into places of direct encounter with the ancient world at its most powerful and its most creative.

Karnak — The Temple That Grew for Two Thousand Years

The Temple Complex of Karnak is the largest religious building ever constructed. Its precinct — dedicated primarily to the god Amun-Ra, the king of the Egyptian pantheon, but containing temples to Mut, Khonsu, and other deities within its enclosing walls — covers approximately 100 hectares and was built and expanded by successive pharaohs over a period of approximately 2,000 years, from the Middle Kingdom (approximately 2055 BCE) to the Roman period. Every pharaoh who wished to demonstrate his piety, his power, and his connection to the divine added to the complex — a new pylon (monumental gateway), a new hall, a new sanctuary, a new obelisk — until the accumulated construction of two millennia had produced an architectural landscape of almost incomprehensible complexity and scale.

The Great Hypostyle Hall of Karnak — built primarily under the reigns of Seti I and Ramesses II in the thirteenth century BCE — is the most ambitious single architectural space in the history of ancient Egyptian building. It covers an area of approximately 5,000 square metres and contains 134 massive columns arranged in 16 rows, the central 12 columns reaching a height of approximately 21 metres and a circumference of approximately 10 metres — large enough for 100 people to stand on their capitals. The columns are covered from base to capital with hieroglyphic inscriptions and painted relief carvings of extraordinary quality and extraordinary density, depicting scenes of royal ritual and divine mythology in a visual programme so complex and so complete that Egyptologists have been studying it for more than two centuries without exhausting its content.

The Sacred Lake of Karnak — a large rectangular pool used for ritual purification and the floating of sacred barques — is one of the most evocative spaces in the complex. At its shores, the famous stone scarab beetle of Amenhotep III stands on its plinth, and Egyptian tradition holds that walking around it seven times counterclockwise brings good fortune. The sound and light show performed at the lake each evening — in which the story of Karnak is narrated across the illuminated temple columns — is one of the most atmospheric cultural experiences available in Egypt.

Luxor Temple — The Southern Sanctuary

On the east bank of the Nile, approximately three kilometres south of Karnak, the Luxor Temple stands in the heart of the modern city — its columns and pylons rising directly from the middle of Luxor's tourist district, the modern street level several metres above the ancient floor, the temple a presence in the daily life of the city in a way that makes it simultaneously an archaeological site and an urban monument. The Luxor Temple was built primarily by Amenhotep III (approximately 1390–1352 BCE) and completed and expanded by Ramesses II, and was dedicated to the celebration of the Opet festival — the annual procession in which the cult statue of Amun was carried in a sacred barque from Karnak to Luxor, symbolising the renewal of divine kingship and the fertilising power of the Nile flood.

The entrance to the Luxor Temple is dominated by the pylon of Ramesses II — a monumental gateway originally flanked by six colossal statues of the pharaoh and two obelisks. One of the obelisks still stands before the pylon. The other was removed in 1833 by the French government and now stands in the Place de la Concorde in Paris, where it has been since 1836. The standing obelisk of Luxor is covered with hieroglyphic inscriptions recording the military victories of Ramesses II in a programme of royal self-glorification that is characteristic of the most powerful pharaoh in Egyptian history — a man who reigned for sixty-seven years and left his name on more temples and monuments than any other ruler of ancient Egypt.

The Avenue of Sphinxes — The Sacred Road

Between the Luxor Temple and the Karnak complex, a road approximately three kilometres long was lined with sphinx statues — human-headed sphinxes in the section nearest Luxor, ram-headed sphinxes in the section nearest Karnak — creating the ceremonial processional route along which the Opet festival procession moved each year, the sacred barques of Amun carried on the shoulders of priests in a ceremony of great religious and political significance. The Avenue of Sphinxes was excavated and restored in the early twenty-first century, and the processional route — the ancient road of the gods, the spine of ancient Thebes — has been partially restored to visibility after centuries of being buried beneath the modern city. The restoration of the Avenue of Sphinxes, completed in 2021, is one of the most ambitious archaeological urban projects of recent years, revealing the full processional route between the two great temple complexes for the first time in more than a thousand years.

The Valley of the Kings — The City of the Dead

On the west bank of the Nile, behind the line of funerary temples that face the river, the barren limestone cliffs of the Theban hills conceal one of the most extraordinary archaeological sites in the world: the Valley of the Kings, the royal necropolis of the New Kingdom pharaohs, where sixty-three tombs — cut deep into the limestone and decorated with paintings and inscriptions of extraordinary quality and extraordinary religious complexity — were prepared for the pharaohs and their consorts between approximately 1550 and 1070 BCE. The valley was chosen for its remote and defensible position, its natural peak — el-Qurn, the horn — which resembled a natural pyramid above the royal tombs, and its distance from the inhabited Nile valley, where the tombs would have been too easily visible and too easily accessible to robbers.

The most famous of the Valley of the Kings tombs is, of course, the tomb of Tutankhamun — not because it is the largest or the most elaborately decorated (it is, by the standards of the valley, relatively modest in both respects) but because it is the only royal tomb in the valley to have been discovered substantially intact, with its burial equipment and its treasures still in place, when Howard Carter opened it in November 1922. The treasures of Tutankhamun — the golden death mask, the innermost coffin of solid gold, the canopic shrine, the ushabti figures, the golden throne, and the thousands of other objects packed into the four small chambers of the tomb — constitute the most complete surviving royal burial assemblage from ancient Egypt and the most celebrated single archaeological discovery in the history of the discipline.

The largest and most elaborately decorated tomb in the Valley of the Kings is the tomb of Ramesses VI — its ceiling covered with astronomical texts and images depicting the journey of the sun through the hours of the night, its walls lined with texts from the Book of the Dead, the Book of Gates, and the Book of Caverns in a programme of funerary literature of extraordinary density and theological complexity. The decoration of the New Kingdom royal tombs is not merely ornamental — it is functional, in the Egyptian understanding: the images and texts on the walls of the tomb were the means by which the dead king navigated the perilous journey through the underworld and achieved union with the sun god Ra in the sky above.

The Valley of the Queens and the Mortuary Temples

Adjacent to the Valley of the Kings, the Valley of the Queens — a smaller and less dramatic but equally significant necropolis — contains the tombs of royal consorts and princes of the New Kingdom period. The most celebrated of the Valley of the Queens tombs is the tomb of Nefertari — the principal wife of Ramesses II and the most beloved consort in the history of the Egyptian royal family — whose tomb paintings, executed in the finest style of the Ramesside period, are regarded as the most beautiful paintings in all of Egyptian art. The paintings of Nefertari's tomb — their colours as vivid as when they were applied three thousand years ago, their draughtsmanship and their spatial composition representing the highest achievement of ancient Egyptian two-dimensional art — were severely damaged by the salt crystallisation that had been gradually destroying the painted plaster since antiquity, and were the subject of a major international conservation project in the 1980s and 1990s that stabilised them for future generations.

The west bank is also dotted with the mortuary temples of the New Kingdom pharaohs — large freestanding temple complexes built for the celebration of the royal funerary cult, their decoration and architectural ambition varying with the wealth and the reign length of the pharaoh who commissioned them. The Temple of Hatshepsut at Deir el-Bahri — built by the female pharaoh who ruled Egypt for approximately twenty years in the fifteenth century BCE, the most successful female ruler in Egyptian history — is the most architecturally accomplished of the mortuary temples: a three-terraced structure of extraordinary elegance built against the cliff face of the Theban hills, its colonnaded terraces connected by ramps in a composition that is simultaneously bold in its overall conception and refined in its detail.

Reflection — The City That Defined Eternity

Ancient Thebes is, among other things, a meditation on the ancient Egyptian preoccupation with eternity — the most sustained and the most architecturally elaborate attempt in human history to defeat the impermanence of individual human life through the construction of monuments designed to last forever. The temples of Karnak and Luxor were built to house the gods in perpetuity. The tombs of the Valley of the Kings were built to preserve the royal body and to provide everything needed for the king's journey through the afterlife and his resurrection as a divine being. The Avenue of Sphinxes was built to channel the power of the divine procession across the sacred geography of the city. Everything in ancient Thebes was built in the service of a single, extraordinary, and ultimately unanswerable question: what happens after we die?

The answer that ancient Thebes gives to this question is not a verbal one but an architectural one — a demonstration, in stone and in paint and in gold, that the answer to the fear of death is not the suppression of the fear but its transformation into the most ambitious building programme in the history of the ancient world. The temples and the tombs of Thebes were not built in spite of the knowledge that all things pass. They were built because of it — because the pharaohs and the priests and the craftsmen who created them understood, with full clarity, that the stone they carved would outlast them all, and that in the outlasting was the nearest thing to eternity that human hands could achieve.

They were right. The temples stand. The tombs endure. The paintings of Nefertari are still as vivid as the day they were applied. And the name of Ramesses II — carved on more surfaces in more places across the ancient world than any other human name before the invention of mass media — is known to every schoolchild on Earth, three and a half thousand years after the death of the man who ordered it inscribed.

Conclusion

Ancient Thebes is the supreme archaeological site of the ancient world — a place where the full range of ancient Egyptian civilisation's architectural, artistic, and religious achievement is concentrated in a single landscape of extraordinary density and extraordinary beauty. The temples of Karnak and Luxor, the Valley of the Kings and the Valley of the Queens, the mortuary temples of the west bank, the Avenue of Sphinxes, and the painted tombs of the Theban necropolis together constitute a heritage of outstanding universal value that UNESCO rightly inscribed in 1979 and that every visitor who has stood in the Hypostyle Hall of Karnak or descended into the painted corridors of a royal tomb has verified in the most direct and most powerful possible way. It is one of the greatest concentrations of human cultural achievement anywhere on Earth, and one of the most affecting places the ancient world has left behind.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: What is the difference between Thebes, Luxor, and Karnak?

Thebes is the ancient Greek name for the city that the ancient Egyptians called Waset, the capital of the New Kingdom empire. Luxor is the modern city built on the site of ancient Thebes, on both banks of the Nile. Karnak is the name of the great temple complex — the largest religious building ever constructed — located on the east bank of the Nile approximately three kilometres north of the Luxor Temple.

Q2: How many tombs are in the Valley of the Kings?

The Valley of the Kings contains sixty-three known tombs, cut into the limestone of the Theban hills between approximately 1550 and 1070 BCE. Not all are royal tombs — some were prepared for royal consorts, princes, and high officials. Most were robbed in antiquity; the tomb of Tutankhamun, discovered by Howard Carter in 1922, is the only royal tomb found substantially intact.

Q3: Can visitors enter Tutankhamun's tomb?

Yes. The tomb of Tutankhamun is open to visitors in the Valley of the Kings, though a separate ticket is required beyond the standard Valley of the Kings entry. The mummy of Tutankhamun remains in the tomb in its outermost coffin. The treasures from the tomb are displayed in the Egyptian Museum in Cairo and the new Grand Egyptian Museum in Giza.

Q4: Is Ancient Thebes a UNESCO World Heritage Site?

Yes. Ancient Thebes with its Necropolis was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1979, under criteria (i), (iii), and (vi), as the greatest concentration of ancient Egyptian monuments on Earth and one of the outstanding testimonies to an ancient civilisation of outstanding universal significance.

Q5: What is the best way to visit Ancient Thebes?

Luxor is the base for visiting all the monuments of ancient Thebes. A minimum of two full days is needed — one for the east bank (Karnak and Luxor Temple) and one for the west bank (Valley of the Kings, Temple of Hatshepsut, Colossi of Memnon). Three or four days allows a comprehensive visit. A private guide is strongly recommended for the Valley of the Kings to understand the tomb decoration. Karnak is best visited early morning before the heat and the crowds.
