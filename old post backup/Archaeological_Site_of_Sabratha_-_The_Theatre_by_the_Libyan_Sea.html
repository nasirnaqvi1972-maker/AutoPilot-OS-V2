A Roman City on the Edge of the Sahara

The North African coast of the Mediterranean was once among the richest regions of the Roman world, studded with cities whose names are now known mostly to archaeologists. On the western coast of Libya, about seventy kilometres from the present capital Tripoli, lies one of the loveliest of them: Sabratha. Its ruins spread along a low promontory facing the sea, golden stone and white marble against deep blue water, and their centrepiece is a theatre whose three-storey stage building rises like a vast stone curtain behind the semicircle of seats.

Sabratha was one of the three cities that gave the Roman region of Tripolitania its name, the others being Oea, which became Tripoli, and the spectacular Leptis Magna to the east. Together they formed a Tripolis, a three-city league, whose prosperity rested on the trade of the Sahara and the produce of the coastal plains. Sabratha’s wealth came from olive oil, grain, ivory, exotic animals and the commerce of caravans that arrived from deep in the interior.

UNESCO inscribed the Archaeological Site of Sabratha on the World Heritage List in 1982, under criterion (iii), as an exceptional testimony to a vanished civilisation, in this case the interwoven Phoenician, Berber and Roman cultures of the Libyan coast. Since 2016 it has been on the List of World Heritage in Danger, a reflection of the conflict and instability that have affected Libya in recent years.

From Phoenician Port to Punic Trading Town

The earliest settlement at Sabratha was a Phoenician trading post, founded probably between the sixth and fifth centuries BCE, in the age when merchants from Tyre and later Carthage established harbours along the coast of North Africa. The site offered a sheltered landing place and access to the fertile hinterland, and the settlers traded with the Libyan peoples of the interior, exchanging cloth, pottery and metalwork for gold dust, ivory and slaves brought across the desert.

Under the influence of Carthage, the town developed Punic character, with temples, houses and harbour installations, and a language and religion that continued to flourish even after Carthage’s fall in 146 BCE. In the second century BCE, the local kings of Numidia extended their authority over the region, and Sabratha came under the sway of Masinissa and his successors, a period that left its most impressive surviving trace in the monumental tomb known as Mausoleum B.

That tower-like tomb, with its stepped podium, Ionic columns and pyramidal roof, shows a blend of Hellenistic, Punic and Libyan elements that is typical of the culture of the age. It stands as a reminder that Sabratha’s story did not begin with the Romans and that its Roman glory rested on older foundations.

Roman Sabratha — Forum, Streets and Merchants

Sabratha passed under Roman control in the first century BCE, after the Romans defeated the African allies of Pompey in the civil wars, and became part of the province of Africa. It reached its peak in the second and early third centuries CE, in the age of the Antonine emperors, when a flourishing trade brought prosperity and monumental building. The city was laid out with a regular grid of streets, a forum at its centre and a harbour on the sea front, and many of its public buildings date from this period.

The Forum, a large paved square, was lined with temples and administrative buildings. At its northern end stood the Capitolium, the temple of Jupiter, Juno and Minerva, while the sides held the Curia, where the town council met, and a basilica, which served as a court and exchange. Inscriptions found in the Forum record the names of the city’s leading citizens, many of whom had Punic or Libyan names, demonstrating how thoroughly the local elite adopted Roman culture while preserving their identity.

Merchants from across the Mediterranean visited Sabratha, and the remains of an association of traders in the Forum area reveal the connections of the city with ports in Italy, Spain and the eastern Mediterranean. The harbour, now silted up, handled exports of olive oil, grain and wild animals for the gladiatorial games, as well as the produce of the Saharan caravan routes.

The Theatre — A Stage Rebuilt from Stone

The greatest monument of Sabratha is its Roman theatre, built in the later second century CE and capable of seating about five thousand spectators. The semicircular auditorium, or cavea, is built of local sandstone and rises in three tiers, while the orchestra, a flat space in front of the stage, is paved with marble. Behind the stage, the scaenae frons, a three-storey architectural façade, is decorated with more than a hundred columns of coloured stone, in a rhythm of niches and statues that formed a spectacular backdrop to the plays.

The theatre had fallen into ruin after earthquakes and neglect, but between the 1920s and 1930s Italian archaeologists, working during the period of Italian colonial rule, reassembled the stage building from its fragments, using a technique called anastylosis, in which original stones are returned to their place. The result is one of the most complete Roman theatres in Africa, and it is still sometimes used for performances.

The decoration of the stage front includes panel reliefs showing Muses, comic and tragic masks and mythological scenes, whose carving reveals the sophistication of the sculptors who worked here. Sitting in the upper rows and looking over the sea, one can imagine an audience of Roman Africans watching a comedy or a mime, in a Latin that was spoken here alongside Punic for centuries.

Temples, Baths and Mosaics — Faith and Leisure on the Coast

Sabratha’s religious life was varied. Alongside the official cults of Rome, such as the Capitoline triad and the imperial cult, there were temples to the Punic gods in their Roman guise, to Liber Pater, the Roman Bacchus, to Hercules, to Serapis and to Isis. The Temple of Isis, on the sea front, is one of the most evocative ruins, its podium and columns set against the waves, and speaks to the importance of Egyptian cults in the port cities of the Roman Mediterranean. The Antonine Temple, built in the second century CE, shows the Roman architectural style at its most confident.

The city also had splendid baths, essential to Roman urban life. The Seaward Baths, near the shore, include a series of rooms with fine mosaic floors depicting marine creatures, geometric patterns and figures, and were probably used by wealthy citizens and visitors. Elsewhere in the city, the floors of private houses and public buildings were adorned with mosaics in black and white or with vivid colours, and many of these are displayed in the museum on the site and in the National Museum in Tripoli.

These buildings reveal a society that combined piety, sociability and display. Temples, baths, theatre and forum together formed the framework of urban life, and Sabratha, though smaller than Leptis Magna, possessed all the elements of a complete Roman city.

A Trial for Magic, an Earthquake and a Byzantine Revival

One of the most vivid episodes in Sabratha’s history is a trial. Around 158 or 159 CE, the philosopher and writer Apuleius, author of the novel The Golden Ass, was brought before the Roman governor Claudius Maximus at Sabratha, accused of using magic to win the hand of a rich widow. His defence speech, the Apologia, a brilliant and witty text, survives, and gives a rare glimpse of the legal and social life of a provincial Roman city, as well as of the educated culture of the African elite.

In the fourth century, Christianity became established in Sabratha, which became the seat of a bishop, and churches were built within the old city. A disaster struck on 21 July 365 CE, when a powerful earthquake, followed by a tsunami, devastated much of the eastern Mediterranean and caused severe damage at Sabratha, destroying part of the theatre and other buildings. The city recovered only partially, and in later centuries was occupied by the Vandals and then, in 533, reconquered by the Byzantine general Belisarius.

Under Byzantine rule, a smaller city was rebuilt within reduced walls, and the Basilica of Justinian, with its mosaics and apse, was erected, partly using stones from earlier buildings. This last flowering did not last, and after the Arab conquest of the seventh century, the city declined and was gradually abandoned, as trade shifted to other ports and the sea silted the harbour.

Rediscovery and the Work of Archaeologists

For centuries, Sabratha lay half-buried in sand and scrub, its stones quarried by local builders and its temples gradually covered by dunes. European travellers began to visit in the nineteenth century, and systematic excavation began after the Italian occupation of Libya in 1911, when teams of Italian archaeologists cleared the Forum, the theatre and the main temples. Their reconstruction of the theatre was a landmark of twentieth-century conservation, albeit shaped by colonial ambitions to link the Italians with the Roman past.

After the Second World War, British archaeologists, including the distinguished Kathleen Kenyon, carried out excavations at Sabratha, uncovering evidence of the earliest Punic occupation and illuminating the city’s long history beneath the Roman layers. Later Libyan authorities took charge of the site, and international teams, including those working with the Society for Libyan Studies, continued to study the area.

These excavations have shown that Sabratha was a more complex place than a simple Roman provincial town. Its architecture, language and religion reveal a blend of Phoenician, Libyan and Roman elements, a remarkable example of how cultures merged in North Africa, and a corrective to older views that treated the region as a mere extension of Rome.

A Site in Danger

The upheavals that have affected Libya since 2011 have placed its great archaeological sites at risk. Armed conflict, the weakening of state authority and economic hardship have all created conditions in which looting, illegal construction and neglect can flourish. In 2016, UNESCO placed all five of Libya’s World Heritage Sites, including Sabratha, Leptis Magna, Cyrene, the Old Town of Ghadamès and the rock art sites of Tadrart Acacus, on the List of World Heritage in Danger.

Sabratha itself has been affected by the instability of the surrounding region. Armed groups have operated in the area, and there were reports in 2016 of attempts by extremists to attack sites and of fighting nearby. Local people and heritage officials, often at considerable personal risk, have worked to guard the site, and the response of residents who protected the ruins has been widely praised.

The challenge for the future is to ensure that when stability returns, the knowledge, resources and infrastructure exist to conserve Sabratha properly. That means training Libyan archaeologists, documenting the site thoroughly and supporting local communities, whose pride in the ancient city is one of its greatest assets. International cooperation will be essential, but the ownership of the heritage rightly belongs to Libyans.

Reflection — The Quiet of a Stage Without Actors

There is a particular melancholy to an empty theatre. The stage has been silent for sixteen centuries, the audiences long gone, and yet the architecture still seems to wait for the performance to begin. At Sabratha the effect is intensified by the sea behind the stage and by the knowledge that this city, once thronged with merchants, sailors and actors, has been quiet for so long.

But the silence is not empty. It is filled by the memory of the many peoples who lived here, Phoenician traders, Libyan farmers, Roman officials and Byzantine bishops, and by the hope that one day the site will once again be open to all who wish to see it. Sabratha reminds us that heritage is never secure, and that protecting it requires peace, care and cooperation across borders and generations.

✦ Conclusion

The Archaeological Site of Sabratha is one of the most beautiful ruins of the Mediterranean, a city that grew from a Phoenician trading post into a prosperous Roman port, with a theatre, temples and baths that still evoke the sophistication of its inhabitants. Its history, encompassing Punic, Numidian, Roman and Byzantine phases, is a vivid illustration of the cultural richness of ancient North Africa.

Today it stands in danger, vulnerable to the effects of conflict and neglect. Its survival depends on the commitment of Libyans and the support of the international community, and its story deserves to be known. For those who cannot visit, learning about Sabratha is a way of honouring a heritage that belongs to all humanity.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

Why is Sabratha a UNESCO World Heritage Site?

UNESCO inscribed it in 1982 under criterion (iii) as an exceptional testimony to the Phoenician, Berber and Roman civilisations of the Libyan coast. Its theatre, temples and mosaics are outstanding examples of Roman provincial architecture and art.

Is Sabratha on the List of World Heritage in Danger?

Yes. UNESCO placed all five of Libya’s World Heritage Sites, including Sabratha, on the Danger List in 2016 because of the impact of conflict and instability. Check UNESCO for the current status.

How old is the Roman theatre?

The theatre was built in the later second century CE. Its stage building was largely reconstructed by Italian archaeologists in the 1920s and 1930s from fallen fragments, a process called anastylosis.

What happened to Sabratha?

The city declined after a major earthquake in 365 CE, then suffered during the Vandal period and the Byzantine and Arab conquests. By the seventh century it had been largely abandoned, and its ruins were gradually covered by sand.

Can tourists visit Sabratha?

Travel to Libya has been strongly discouraged by many governments because of security concerns, and access to the site has been limited. Anyone interested in visiting should consult current official travel advice and local authorities, and should treat this post as background information, not travel guidance.
