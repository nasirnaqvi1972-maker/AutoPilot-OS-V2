Halab — A City That Has Been Everything

Few cities on earth have been inhabited as long, as continuously and as densely as Aleppo. Called Halab in Arabic, and known in the cuneiform tablets of the Bronze Age as a city of consequence, it sits in the dry plains of northern Syria between the Euphrates and the Mediterranean, at the point where the caravan routes from Mesopotamia, Anatolia, Persia and the Levant came together. For millennia, anyone who wished to trade between east and west had reason to pass through it, and the city grew accordingly rich, crowded and cosmopolitan.

The old city that UNESCO inscribed in 1986 is the product of that long history. Within its walls and around its central citadel are mosques, madrasas, churches, synagogues, caravanserais, hammams and covered markets from almost every period, from the Hellenistic and Roman layout visible in the grid of the central streets to the Mamluk and Ottoman monuments that give the city its distinctive stone-built character. The sheer density of heritage, packed into only a few square kilometres, is almost without parallel in the Middle East.

It is also impossible to speak of Aleppo today without speaking of loss. Between 2012 and 2016 the old city became a front line in the Syrian civil war, and large parts of it were badly damaged or destroyed. UNESCO placed the property on the List of World Heritage in Danger in 2013, and it remains there. To write of Aleppo is therefore to write of a living city and a wounded one, and to honour what survives while acknowledging what has been lost.

From the Storm God to the Seleucids

The deep history of Aleppo is written in the mound on which the Citadel stands. Excavations have revealed that the summit was already a sacred place in the third millennium BC, and the site later housed a temple of the Storm God, Hadad, whose cult was central to the religious life of northern Syria and which survives in fragments, including carved reliefs, within the Citadel’s precinct. Aleppo was the capital of the kingdom of Yamhad in the early second millennium BC, a power that rivalled its neighbours, and was later absorbed by the Hittites and a succession of Near Eastern empires.

The Seleucids, the Macedonian dynasty that inherited the eastern portion of Alexander’s empire, refounded the city around 300 BC as Beroea, laying out a regular grid of streets whose line is still traceable in the old quarter today. Under Rome and then Byzantium the city flourished as a regional centre, a place of churches and walled markets, and the Christian community that took root here has survived into modern times.

The Arab conquest of 637–638 brought Aleppo into the early caliphate, and the following centuries saw the city pass through the hands of the Umayyads, the Abbasids and local dynasties such as the Hamdanids, under whose ruler Sayf al-Dawla in the tenth century Aleppo became a brilliant court city, a magnet for poets and scholars. Its character was formed in this long period of Arab and Islamic rule, even as older layers of Aramean, Hittite and Greco-Roman life remained embedded in its streets.

The Citadel and the Warrior Princes

The Citadel as it appears today is largely the work of the Ayyubid and Mamluk eras, and above all of al-Zahir Ghazi, the son of Saladin, who ruled Aleppo from 1186 to 1216 and rebuilt its defences on a monumental scale. The mound was ringed with a steep stone glacis, a smooth sloping apron meant to deny access to attackers, and the single entrance was approached by a long bridge on arches leading to a heavily protected gate with a series of right-angled turns, designed to slow and expose any assailant to fire from above.

Palaces, Mosques and a Throne Hall

Inside the walls lay a small city of its own: mosques, bathhouses, storerooms, cisterns and a palace complex with a ceremonial throne hall whose richly decorated interior survived to be admired by visitors. The Citadel was a place of both defence and display, a statement of the power of the ruler who commanded the whole region. In the Middle Ages it withstood sieges by Crusaders, was captured and sacked by the Mongols under Hulagu in 1260, and was rebuilt by the Mamluks, who adapted it for their own purposes.

Anyone climbing to the summit gains a sense of the sweep of the city below. The Citadel has long served as the lens through which Aleppo is viewed, and its image, a pale mass crowned by towers, is the city’s emblem. Parts of it were damaged during the recent conflict and in the earthquake of February 2023, and work to stabilise and restore the monument has been an urgent priority.

The Souqs and the Khans — Aleppo as Marketplace of the World

If the Citadel was the city’s head, its markets were its heart. The covered souqs of Aleppo, collectively one of the largest and most celebrated historic bazaars in the world, run for several kilometres in a labyrinth of stone-vaulted passages, each specialising in a particular trade. Here were sold wool and cotton cloth, leather, spices, gold, perfume, and the famous olive-oil and laurel soap that still bears the city’s name. Walking the main souq, Souq al-Madina, the visitor entered a world of low light, echoing voices and a thousand smells, which was for many centuries the commercial centre of northern Syria.

Attached to the souqs were the khans, the caravanserais that served as warehouses, hostels and trading offices for merchants from near and far. The Khan al-Gumruk, or customs khan, built in the late sixteenth century, and the Khan al-Wazir, with their arcaded courtyards and rows of storerooms, housed European consuls and traders, including English, French, Dutch and Venetian merchants, who established permanent colonies in Aleppo in the Ottoman period. The city thus became a place where Europe and Asia met in daily commerce.

The importance of Aleppo as a trading centre began to decline in the nineteenth century, as the opening of the Suez Canal in 1869 diverted trade away from the overland routes, and as the political map of the region changed in the twentieth century. Yet the souqs continued as living markets until the recent war, when large parts were burned or destroyed, a particularly poignant loss because their value lay not only in the buildings but in the trades and communities they sustained.

Mosques, Madrasas and a Medieval Hospital

Aleppo’s religious and educational buildings are among the glories of Islamic architecture in Syria. The Great Mosque, traditionally attributed to the Umayyad period, was rebuilt and expanded over the centuries, and its square minaret, completed in the 1090s under the Seljuks, was celebrated for its elegantly carved decoration and slender proportions. Alongside it stood dozens of smaller mosques and madrasas, each a miniature masterpiece of stone carving, with inscriptions, geometric ornament and muqarnas vaulting.

The Madrasa al-Firdaws, founded in the 1230s by Dayfa Khatun, the widow of al-Zahir Ghazi, is often cited as one of the most perfect Ayyubid buildings, a calm courtyard of pale stone arranged around a central pool and a prayer hall with a richly decorated portal. In the following century the Bimaristan Arghun, a hospital built in 1354, offered medical care and also, according to tradition, treatment for the mentally ill through the use of music and water, an early example of humane therapy.

These buildings were not only places of worship and learning but civic institutions, paid for by endowments, serving the people of the city, and linked to the markets and neighbourhoods around them. Their architecture expresses a society that valued both piety and public service, and their survival, even in damaged form, testifies to the continuity of that civic tradition.

A City of Many Faiths and Neighbourhoods

One of the features that distinguished Aleppo was the diversity of its population. Muslims, Christians of several denominations, and Jews lived side by side for centuries, each community occupying its own quarters yet taking part in a common economic and civic life. The Christian quarter of Jdeideh, with its narrow lanes and courtyard houses built in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, contained churches of the Armenian, Greek Orthodox, Maronite and other traditions; the Jewish community had its own synagogues and neighbourhood, famed in particular as the home of the medieval manuscript known as the Aleppo Codex, the oldest nearly complete text of the Hebrew Bible.

The courtyard houses of Aleppo, built around inner courtyards with fountains, stone-paved floors and richly carved reception rooms, were a distinctive expression of domestic architecture, combining privacy with elegance. Many were converted in recent decades into hotels, restaurants and cultural spaces, reflecting a growing appreciation of the city’s heritage before the war.

This religious and cultural plurality is central to Aleppo’s significance. The city has historically been a place where difference was lived with rather than erased, and its architecture preserves that pluralism in stone. The loss and displacement of many of its communities in recent years have made the survival of these spaces all the more important as a record of what coexistence once looked like.

War, Earthquake and the Long Work of Restoration

The Syrian conflict that began in 2011 reached Aleppo in 2012, and the battle for the city lasted until late 2016. The old city, with its dense fabric of stone buildings and its strategic location, became one of the principal fronts. A fire in the souqs in 2012 destroyed large sections of the covered market, the Great Mosque was badly damaged and its celebrated minaret collapsed in 2013, and the Citadel and numerous other historic buildings were hit by shelling, bombing and the digging of tunnels. By the end of the fighting, a substantial portion of the old city lay in ruins.

In February 2023, a devastating earthquake in southern Turkey and northern Syria brought further damage to already weakened structures in Aleppo, including parts of the Citadel and several historic houses. The combination of conflict and natural disaster has left the heritage of the old city profoundly fragile, and has created an enormous task for those responsible for its recovery.

Since the end of the main fighting, restoration has been undertaken by a range of actors, including Syrian authorities and organisations, the Aga Khan Trust for Culture, UNESCO and other international bodies. Work has included clearing rubble, documenting and numbering fallen stones for possible reassembly, stabilising damaged structures and rebuilding some souq sections. Restoration in a city that has seen so much destruction raises difficult questions about authenticity, scale and priority, but it also embodies a determination that Aleppo should not be lost. Readers should note that the situation in Syria continues to evolve, and that conditions on the ground and the status of individual projects may have changed since this post was written.

Reflection — What a City Is For

It is difficult to look at Aleppo without thinking about what a city actually is. We tend to define it by its monuments, and Aleppo has many, but the old city was never simply a collection of monuments. It was a web of trades, families, faiths and friendships, held together by habit and by the shared use of streets, markets and courtyards. The stones that survive are only the container of that life, and the true loss of war is what it does to the people who made the city, scattering them and breaking the continuity of their crafts and customs.

Yet Aleppo’s history is also a history of resilience. The city has been sacked by Mongols, shaken by earthquakes, and bypassed by changes in trade routes, and it has each time recovered. The task of understanding and restoring it today is one of respect, for what was, and for those who will return to it. In the careful numbering of fallen stones, in the patient work of masons and conservators, in the memories of those who know the alleys by heart, the old city survives as an idea as well as a place. That idea, of a city built by many hands from many faiths, is worth protecting for its own sake.

✦ Conclusion

The Ancient City of Aleppo is one of the great historic cities of the world, a place where Hittite temples, Greek street grids, Arab mosques, Mamluk fortifications and Ottoman caravanserais are layered one upon another within a few square kilometres. Its Citadel, souqs, khans and mosques tell the story of a city that was for millennia a crossroads of civilisations, and its religious and cultural plurality remains one of its most precious legacies.

It is also a city in recovery, bearing the marks of war and earthquake yet sustained by the people and organisations determined to rebuild it. To learn about Aleppo is to engage with both its splendour and its suffering, and to appreciate how much the survival of its heritage matters, not only for Syria but for everyone who values the shared human story. Its inscription on the World Heritage List, and its place on the List in Danger, are reminders that remembering and protecting such a city is a common responsibility.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

Why is the Ancient City of Aleppo a UNESCO World Heritage Site?

UNESCO inscribed Aleppo in 1986 under criteria (iii) and (iv). It was recognised as a city that bears exceptional testimony to successive civilisations and as an outstanding ensemble of architecture, including its Citadel, Great Mosque, madrasas, caravanserais and covered souqs, shaped by its role as a crossroads of trade between east and west.

Is Aleppo on the List of World Heritage in Danger?

Yes. UNESCO placed the Ancient City of Aleppo on the List of World Heritage in Danger in 2013, together with the other Syrian World Heritage Sites, because of the damage caused by the armed conflict. It remains there while recovery and protection efforts continue.

What was damaged in Aleppo during the war?

The old city suffered extensive damage between 2012 and 2016. Large parts of the covered souqs were destroyed by fire, the Great Mosque was badly damaged and its minaret collapsed in 2013, and the Citadel and many historic houses, khans and madrasas were hit. The February 2023 earthquake caused further damage to weakened buildings.

What is the Citadel of Aleppo?

The Citadel is a vast medieval fortress on an artificial mound at the centre of the old city. Much of its present form dates from the Ayyubid ruler al-Zahir Ghazi, who rebuilt it in the late twelfth and early thirteenth centuries, though the mound itself has been occupied and fortified for thousands of years and includes remains of a temple of the Storm God.

Can tourists visit Aleppo?

Travel conditions in Syria have changed repeatedly and many governments have advised against travel to the country. Anyone considering a visit should check current official travel advice, security conditions and entry requirements immediately before planning, and should treat any information in a blog post, including this one, as background rather than a substitute for up-to-date guidance.
