A City in the Green Ocean

There is no more dramatic way to arrive at a Maya city than at Tikal. A dirt path leads through dense forest loud with the calls of birds and howler monkeys, and then, with no warning, the trees open onto a vast plaza flanked by two steep pyramids, their stepped limestone flanks climbing into the sky. It feels less like a ruin than a place that has been waiting in the rainforest, patient and a little amused, for the visitor to catch up.

UNESCO inscribed Tikal National Park in 1979, in the early days of the World Heritage List, and it remains one of the few properties recognised under both cultural and natural criteria. The cultural case rests on the architecture, art and political history of one of the greatest cities of the Classic Maya world; the natural case rests on the surrounding tropical forest, part of the largest block of rainforest north of the Amazon.

The two cannot really be separated. The city was built from the forest and, after its abandonment, was reclaimed by it, and the preservation of the temples today depends on protecting the living ecosystem that surrounds them. To visit Tikal is to experience the fusion of human achievement and wild nature in a single, unforgettable landscape.

Mutal — The Rise of a Maya Kingdom

The Maya knew the city as Mutal, a name that appears in the glyphs carved on its monuments and identifies the royal lineage that ruled from here. The first settlers arrived as early as the middle of the first millennium BCE, and by the Late Preclassic period a substantial ceremonial centre had developed, including the monumental complex known today as the Lost World. Tikal’s founding dynasty is traced by its inscriptions to a ruler called Yax Ehb Xook, who is thought to have lived around the end of the first century CE.

The position of the city was both fortunate and demanding. Tikal lies in an area without rivers or lakes, and its inhabitants relied on a system of reservoirs and canals to capture and store rainwater through the dry season. The city’s great plazas were carefully sloped to channel water into these reservoirs, a feat of hydraulic engineering that allowed the population to grow to tens of thousands.

By the fourth century CE, Tikal was a major regional power, with trade connections stretching across Mesoamerica. Its rulers commissioned carved stelae to record their deeds, and these inscriptions, now partly deciphered, form the backbone of our understanding of the city’s political history, from alliances and marriages to wars and sacrifices.

Teotihuacan, Calakmul and the Wars of the Maya Lowlands

In 378 CE, according to Tikal’s inscriptions, a foreign warrior named Siyaj K’ak’, whose name means Fire Is Born, arrived at the city, and its reigning king died on the same day. The newcomer is linked to Teotihuacan, the great metropolis of central Mexico, and within a few years a new ruler, Yax Nuun Ayiin, was installed at Tikal with strong Teotihuacan iconography. The event shows how far the networks of power and trade of the Classic world extended, and how deeply the Maya cities were bound up with the politics of the wider region.

The sixth and seventh centuries brought a long rivalry between Tikal and the powerful kingdom centred at Calakmul, which built an alliance system around the Kaan dynasty. In 562 CE Tikal was defeated in a war involving its rival and its allies, and entered a period of decline known as the Hiatus, in which few monuments were erected. The humiliation was long-lasting, and recovery was slow.

Revival came with Jasaw Chan K’awiil I, who ruled from 682 CE and who in 695 defeated Calakmul in a decisive battle. His victory restored Tikal’s fortunes and inaugurated a golden age of building and artistic achievement, which saw the construction of many of the temples that visitors see today. It is a story of fall and recovery worthy of any epic.

The Great Temples and the Architecture of Power

Tikal’s architecture is distinguished by the steepness and height of its pyramids. Temple I, the Temple of the Great Jaguar, built around 730 CE as the tomb of Jasaw Chan K’awiil I, rises in nine terraces to a small temple chamber crowned by a decorative roof comb. Across the plaza stands Temple II, the Temple of the Masks, which faces it at the opposite side and is thought to commemorate the king’s wife, creating a composition of balanced mass that frames the Great Plaza.

Temple IV, the Tallest of All

Temple IV, built around 741 CE for the king Yik’in Chan K’awiil, is the tallest structure at Tikal and one of the tallest pre-Columbian buildings in the Americas, about seventy metres high. Visitors can climb a wooden stairway on its flank to the summit, from which the view across the forest canopy, with the roof combs of other temples breaking through the green, is among the most celebrated panoramas in Central America. Beyond these great pyramids, the North Acropolis, a dense cluster of funerary temples built over centuries, the Central Acropolis with its warren of palace rooms, and the twin-pyramid complexes used to mark the end of calendar cycles all contribute to the city’s extraordinary architectural range.

Wildlife and the Rainforest — The Natural Heritage of Tikal

Tikal National Park protects some 57,600 hectares of tropical forest, part of the Maya Biosphere Reserve, which is one of the most important areas of rainforest in Central America. The forest is home to jaguars, pumas, ocelots, tapirs, spider monkeys and howler monkeys, as well as hundreds of bird species, from toucans and parrots to the spectacular ocellated turkey. Coatimundis scuttle across the paths, and the stillness of the early morning is broken by the roaring of howlers and the calls of birds.

The trees are also part of the story. Mahogany, sapodilla, ceiba and ramón trees grow among the ruins; the ceiba, with its towering trunk, was sacred to the Maya as the world tree linking earth, sky and underworld. The ramón, or breadnut, whose seeds can be ground into flour, may have been an important food source, and archaeologists have suggested that the forest around Tikal was partly managed and enriched by its builders.

This interdependence is why UNESCO recognised both cultural and natural values. The forest protects the monuments from erosion and development, and the monuments, in turn, have given the forest a powerful claim to protection. Maintaining that balance, in the face of pressures from agriculture, logging and illegal activity in the wider region, is a continuing challenge for Guatemala.

Collapse, Rediscovery and the Work of Archaeology

The last dated monument at Tikal was erected in 869 CE, and by about 900 the city had been largely abandoned, part of the broader phenomenon known as the Classic Maya collapse. The causes were many and interacting: prolonged droughts, environmental stress, warfare and political fragmentation seem all to have played a part. Population declined, rulers lost authority, and the great ceremonial centres of the southern lowlands fell silent one by one.

The forest gradually swallowed the city, and Tikal passed into local memory. It was brought to outside attention in the middle of the nineteenth century, when a Guatemalan expedition led by Modesto Méndez and Ambrosio Tut visited the site in 1848, and later by explorers and scholars such as Alfred Maudslay and Teoberto Maler. Large-scale excavation began in the 1950s with a University of Pennsylvania project that continued for about fifteen years, clearing and recording much of the central area.

Today, archaeological work continues under Guatemalan direction, with international collaboration. Laser mapping from the air, known as lidar, has revealed that the settlement was far larger than previously thought, with extensive networks of structures, causeways and fortifications hidden beneath the forest. Each new discovery deepens the sense that Tikal was not merely a ceremonial centre but a dense, sophisticated urban landscape.

Visiting Tikal Today — Sunrise, Silence and Steep Stairs

A visit to Tikal begins long before the ruins. Most travellers base themselves in Flores or in the lodges close to the park, and many choose to enter before dawn, walking through darkness with a guide and a torch to reach the summit of a temple in time for sunrise. The experience is unforgettable: first the forest’s sounds, then a pale glow, and finally the tops of the pyramids emerging from the mist while the howlers roar in welcome.

The central area can be explored on foot, with well-marked trails between the Great Plaza, the acropolises and the outlying groups. Distances are greater than they seem, and the heat and humidity can be tiring, so an early start and frequent water breaks are essential. A good guide, who can point out birds and monkeys as well as explain inscriptions and architecture, adds enormously to the visit.

Respect for the site is vital. Many structures are fragile, and climbing is permitted only on designated stairways. Visitors are asked to keep to the paths, not to touch carved surfaces and not to disturb wildlife. Those who follow these rules help ensure that Tikal remains as awe-inspiring for future generations as it is today.

Reflection — A Place Where Time Is Layered

Standing atop Temple IV as the mist lifts, one feels the layers of time. Below, the forest shimmers; the roof combs of temples emerge like the masts of drowned ships; and in the distance a howler monkey roars. The city that was once among the largest in the Americas now exists in a kind of suspended state, neither fully lost nor fully recovered.

What Tikal teaches, beyond the achievements of its builders, is a humility before the cycles of civilisation and nature. Great cities rise and fall, forests advance and retreat, and the work of preserving both is a task of patience and care. That the Maya descendants who live in Guatemala today continue to honour the site as part of their heritage adds a further dimension: Tikal is not only a monument to the past but a link to a living culture.

✦ Conclusion

Tikal National Park is one of the world’s great archaeological and natural landscapes: a Maya city of soaring pyramids, royal tombs and hieroglyphic monuments set within an immense and living rainforest. Its history, from its rise in the Preclassic period through its wars with Calakmul to its mysterious abandonment, is a narrative of ambition, rivalry and resilience.

To visit it is to climb ancient stairways among howling monkeys and calling toucans, and to watch the sun rise over a canopy that conceals still more secrets. Tikal rewards the patient traveller with a sense of both the grandeur of the Maya achievement and the enduring power of the natural world.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

Why is Tikal a UNESCO World Heritage Site?

UNESCO inscribed Tikal National Park in 1979 as a mixed site, recognising its outstanding Maya architecture and art under cultural criteria (i), (iii) and (iv), and the significance of its tropical rainforest ecosystem under natural criteria (ix) and (x).

Who built Tikal?

Tikal was built by the Maya over many centuries, with the greatest monuments raised during the Classic period, between about 200 and 900 CE. Its royal dynasty, known as the Mutal lineage, is recorded in hieroglyphic inscriptions on stelae and temples.

What is the tallest temple at Tikal?

Temple IV, built around 741 CE, is the tallest, at roughly seventy metres. Visitors can climb a wooden staircase to its summit for panoramic views over the forest canopy.

Why was Tikal abandoned?

By about 900 CE, Tikal had been largely abandoned, probably due to a combination of drought, environmental stress, warfare and political disruption, part of the wider Classic Maya collapse. The reasons remain a subject of active research.

How do I get to Tikal?

Most visitors travel from Flores, in northern Guatemala, about an hour by road from the park, with flights from Guatemala City to Mundo Maya International Airport. Organised tours from Flores and El Remate are common, including sunrise and sunset visits.

🧳 Visitor's Guide

Getting There — Fly to Flores (Mundo Maya International Airport) and travel about an hour by road to the park; buses and shuttles run from Flores and El Remate

Best Time to Visit — The dry season from December to April is most comfortable for walking, though it is hot; the wet season brings rain and lush vegetation

Tickets — Entrance fees apply at the park gate, and sunrise and sunset visits require special arrangements; check current rules before travelling

Time Needed — At least a full day to see the main plazas and temples; two days allows a slower pace

What to Bring — Insect repellent, water, sun protection, sturdy shoes and a light rain jacket

Climbing — Only some structures can be climbed, and staircases are often steep; follow signs and guide advice

Wildlife — Keep a respectful distance from animals and do not feed them

Safety — Check current regional travel advice and use reputable guides and transport
