An Island Too Small for Its Own Fame

There is a particular moment in the crossing from Dakar when the harbour noise falls away and Gorée comes into view, a low island the colour of terracotta and bougainvillea, no more than a few hundred metres across, afloat on a restless Atlantic. It looks, at first, like a postcard of a Mediterranean fishing village that has lost its way and drifted south, with balconies leaning over narrow lanes, bougainvillea spilling over garden walls and a pair of old bastions keeping watch over the water. Nothing in that gentle first impression announces that the island is among the most argued-over places in the African memory of the Atlantic slave trade.

Yet argued over it is, and also deeply loved. In 1978, when UNESCO's World Heritage Committee compiled its very first list, the Island of Gorée was among the twelve properties it chose, and the committee justified the choice on a single criterion, the sixth, which concerns places directly associated with events or ideas of outstanding universal significance. The island, in UNESCO's words, is an exceptional testimony to one of the greatest tragedies in the history of human societies, and its fortresses, buildings, streets and squares were presented as the evidence of its role as the largest slave-trading centre on the African coast between the fifteenth and nineteenth centuries.

That last claim is where this post must proceed with care. Gorée is real, its architecture is real, and the suffering of the millions of Africans who were carried across the Atlantic is beyond dispute. What historians have contested, sometimes bitterly, is how many captives actually passed through this particular island, and how much of the island's emotional authority rests on numbers that the surviving records do not support. In the pages that follow I shall try to keep both truths in view: the island as a place of genuine historical importance and as a symbol whose scale has been disputed by scholars. The two do not cancel each other, and the more patiently one reads the debate, the richer the island becomes.

A Rock on the Edge of Africa

The island lies roughly three kilometres off the coast of Dakar, close to the westernmost point of the African continent, and that position explains almost everything that followed. Ships sailing along the Senegambian coast found in Gorée a sheltered anchorage, defensible by its cliffs and its insularity, and far enough from the mainland to be safe from sudden attack yet near enough to trade with the peoples of the coast. A small rocky island with a sheltered harbour and a few sandy lanes, it was an ideal trading post for any European power willing to garrison it.

Today Gorée forms one of the nineteen communes d'arrondissement of the city of Dakar, and it is the smallest and the least populated of them, home to only a couple of thousand residents. There are no cars; the lanes are too narrow and the island too small, and so life is conducted on foot, to the sound of the sea, the voices of residents and, now and then, the arrival of the ferry at the quay. This quiet is part of the island's character and also part of its protection. UNESCO's own assessment of integrity notes that the insular nature of Gorée and a body of legal protections contribute to the preservation of the site, and that the surrounding Atlantic provides a natural buffer of nearly four kilometres.

Four Flags Over One Harbour

The Portuguese were the first Europeans to establish themselves at Gorée, in the mid-fifteenth century, in the age when their caravels were feeling their way down the African coast. After them the island passed through a succession of hands, a sequence that reads like a catalogue of the Atlantic powers. The Dutch occupied it in the early seventeenth century and gave it the name Goeree, probably after an island in their own country, and built a fort there. Then the English and the French contended for it, and it changed hands repeatedly during the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. The French held it from 1677, and, after a final period of English control, again from 1815 until Senegal became independent in 1960.

For the history of the slave trade, what matters is that Gorée served each of these powers as a trading station, a strongpoint and a place from which goods, and people, could be gathered and shipped. Its warehouses and fortifications are the physical residue of that commerce. They also testify to the long contest among European states for the African coast, a contest in which the island's tiny size was no obstacle, for in the economics of the Atlantic a good harbour mattered more than acreage. It is this layering of Portuguese, Dutch, English and French presence that gives the island its architecture, a hybrid of stone bastions, tiled roofs and the characteristic two-storey houses with open galleries.

Slavery itself was formally outlawed in France's territories in 1794, but the practice and the commerce continued, and the definitive abolition of the slave trade came under Napoleon in 1815. After that the island's merchants turned, with varying enthusiasm, to what contemporaries called legitimate commerce in groundnuts, groundnut oil, gum arabic and ivory. Gorée entered the nineteenth century as a trading town with a changing economy and an enormous moral inheritance.

The Signares and the Society They Built

No account of Gorée can be honest without the signares, the women of mixed African and European descent who became the island's commercial and social aristocracy. Many of them formed unions with European traders and officers, and the families that resulted combined African and European kinship, property and influence. Signares owned houses, ships and goods, supervised male clerks and merchants and set the fashions in dress, jewellery and entertainment, creating a distinctive urban culture of which echoes survive in the island's festivals and in the elegance of the older houses.

It would be a mistake to romanticise them. Their wealth derived from the same commerce that the island commemorates, and some of the great houses were built on the proceeds of trade in human beings. But they also illustrate a truth that the simple image of the island as a dungeon sometimes obscures: Gorée was not only a prison or a depot but a town, a society with its own families, rituals and ambitions, shaped by Africans as well as by Europeans. That complexity is one of the reasons historians have urged visitors to see the island as a whole rather than as a single building.

The House of Slaves and the Door of No Return

At the heart of the island's modern fame stands a single house. The Maison des Esclaves, a handsome two-storey building of ochre and rose with a graceful double staircase curling into its courtyard, is generally dated to the late eighteenth century, with sources placing its construction around the 1770s or early 1780s, and it is associated with a family of mixed-race merchants, the Pépins, one of the great signare dynasties. On the ground floor are small vaulted rooms that the museum presents as the cells in which captives were held, and at the end of a passage a narrow doorway opens straight onto the water. This is the Door of No Return, the portal through which, according to the traditional account, captives were led onto the ships.

The house was reconstructed and opened as a museum in 1962, and its first and most celebrated curator, Boubacar Joseph Ndiaye, devoted decades to telling its story to visitors from every continent. His guided talks, delivered with great feeling, made the house an international site of memory. Nelson Mandela is recorded as having visited and sat alone in one of the cells; Pope John Paul II came; and in 2013 Barack Obama stood at the Door of No Return and spoke of the cruelty of the slave trade. Some two hundred thousand people visit the museum in a year, many of them from the African diaspora, for whom the doorway is a threshold of identity as much as of history.

And yet, among historians, the House of Slaves has also been the subject of sustained doubt. Critics point out that the house was built relatively late in the history of the Atlantic trade, and that the evidence for the famous doorway functioning as a point of embarkation for large numbers of captives is thin. Curtin himself, the pioneering historian of the Atlantic slave trade, argued that the doorway was unlikely to have had much importance in this respect. It is a sobering exercise to stand in the cool of the cells and to know that the story told there is, in its details, disputed. The right response is not cynicism but attentiveness.

The Numbers Debate: What Historians Have Said

The dispute over Gorée is one of the most instructive in the modern study of the slave trade, and it deserves to be set out carefully, because the figures vary enormously and because they come from sources of very different kinds. The museum's own literature, associated with the work of the late curator Ndiaye, has at times spoken of millions of captives, and figures as large as fifteen to twenty million have been attributed to the house's published material, though Ndiaye himself, according to press accounts, said that the real number could not be known. These totals, it must be said plainly, cannot be reconciled with the overall estimates of the Atlantic trade, in which twelve to fifteen million Africans are generally thought to have reached the Americas alive, and in which Gorée was one of many embarkation points along a coast that stretched for thousands of kilometres.

At the other end of the debate stand quantitative historians. Philip Curtin, in his foundational 1969 census of the Atlantic slave trade, concluded that the shipments from Gorée were small, no more than a few hundred people a year even in significant years, with a total reported in one press account as about thirty thousand. David Eltis, whose database of slaving voyages has revised and refined these estimates, has been quoted as suggesting that fewer than one per cent of those taken across the Atlantic, perhaps about a hundred and twenty thousand people, left through Gorée. Other writers have given still other figures: a widely repeated claim that more than a hundred thousand people were shipped from the island to Nantes between 1763 and 1775 has itself been challenged, since the source document is said to refer to the whole region of Upper Guinea rather than to Gorée alone. The fair summary is that the estimates range across orders of magnitude, from tens of thousands to the millions, and that the better-documented ones lie at the lower end.

The disagreement came to a head in the 1990s, when a French newspaper described the House of Slaves as a myth. A conference at the Institut Fondamental d'Afrique Noire in Dakar brought together about a hundred scholars, and its conclusions were measured: most participants affirmed that Gorée had played a significant role as a slave port, while acknowledging the limits of the statistics, calling for further research and paying tribute to Ndiaye's work in keeping remembrance alive. Critics, however, continue to accuse the museum tradition of exaggeration to attract tourists, and scholars such as Ana Lucia Araujo have argued that the island is not a place from which real people left in the numbers that are claimed.

Memory Against Archive

What should the reader make of this? I would suggest that two different questions are being asked, and that each has its own answer. If the question is quantitative, how many people were embarked at Gorée, the weight of the evidence favours the lower estimates, and UNESCO's formulation of the island as the largest slave-trading centre on the African coast is a claim that many specialists would not accept without qualification. If the question is commemorative, whether Gorée can legitimately stand for the grief of the entire diaspora, there is a different and also reasonable answer, offered by the scholars who have defended the island's symbolic role. Hamady Bocoum has called the debate a false one and said that every African village has the right to develop a memory of slavery, while Achille Mbembe has remarked that the exact number does not really matter because it can never be known. Ibrahima Thioub, for his part, has voiced the worry that African nations might compete for tourist revenue by claiming the larger share of this sorrowful history.

I find the most honest reading to lie between these positions. A memorial does not require the numbers to be wrong to be moving, and an archive does not diminish the sorrow of the people it records. What a careful visitor can do is to hold the island's symbolism and its documented history together, and to remember that this is the way that all difficult sites, from Robben Island to the great fortified ports of the Caribbean, ultimately teach.

Why Gorée Still Matters

It would be perverse to conclude that because the numbers are uncertain the island is unimportant. The slave trade along the Senegambian coast was real and large, and the French establishments there, centred on Saint-Louis and Gorée, were integral to it. Curtin himself estimated that about a hundred and eighty thousand enslaved Africans were shipped from the French posts of Senegambia between 1711 and 1810, most of them from Saint-Louis and from the English fort at James Island, which reminds us that the wider region was a theatre of trade even if the island itself was a smaller part of it. What Gorée preserves is the architecture of this world, the fortresses, the merchants' houses, the squares, and the social fabric of the signares, in a state that UNESCO describes as almost intact: the island was listed as a historic site by the colonial administration in 1944, and UNESCO records no major construction since then that might harm its authenticity.

There is another reason for its prominence, and it has to do with what happens when memory finds a physical home. For African-Americans, Afro-Caribbean people and many Brazilians seeking a point of contact with their ancestors' origins, Gorée has offered a place to stand. The island's status as a pilgrimage site, a sanctuary for reconciliation in UNESCO's phrase, has little to do with a precise tally and much to do with the human need to locate grief in the world. A tragedy of such scale demands places where it can be mourned, and Gorée, whatever the quibbles of the archive, has become one of them.

After the Trade: Teachers, Deputies and Swimmers

The island's story did not end in 1815. In the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries Gorée became a place of administration, education and political ambition. It was made a French commune with an elected mayor in 1872, and it gave to Senegal and to France Blaise Diagne, born on the island, who in 1914 became the first African elected to the French National Assembly and sat there until 1934. From 1913 to 1938 the island housed the William Ponty School, a teachers' college that trained a generation of West African intellectuals and administrators, and W. E. B. Du Bois, visiting in the 1920s, praised it.

Some of the older buildings still serve these later histories. The Historical Museum of Senegal occupies Fort d'Estrées, a fortress built in the middle of the nineteenth century, while the former government palace, constructed in 1864, recalls the days when the island was the seat of a governor. In 1985 the Dakar-Gorée swim began, an annual race across the strait that honours, in its own athletic way, those who may have tried to swim toward freedom. These layers matter, because they remind us that Gorée has been, through all its phases, a place where Africans have made history and not simply endured it.

Walking the Island Today

To visit Gorée now is to participate in a kind of slow, thoughtful tourism. The ferry from Dakar departs at intervals through the day, the crossing taking about half an hour, and the passengers, a mix of schoolchildren, families, diaspora pilgrims and day-trippers, file off at the quay into a world of sun-warmed walls and sandy lanes. One may wander at will, stopping at the small shops and studios, and at the restaurants and guesthouses that now occupy many of the old merchants' houses.

The principal sights are quickly seen: the House of Slaves, the Historical Museum in the fort, the church of Saint Charles Borromeo, and the hilltop fortifications with their view of the Dakar skyline. But the best advice is to be unhurried. Come on a quiet weekday, spend the morning in the lanes before the heat of noon, sit in the shade, and watch the light change on the bay. A licensed local guide can be of great value in setting the House of Slaves in its fuller historical context, and one who is willing to explain the debate described above will give the visit an honesty that no slogan could.

Reflection

I have come to think of Gorée as a lesson in how to hold a difficult memory. The island invites us to do two things at once that seem to pull in opposite directions: to feel, and to question. To stand at the Door of No Return and feel something of the weight of the Atlantic trade is not in itself a mistake; to go on and ask what the archives say about how many people actually passed through that doorway is not a betrayal. The mature response to a place like this is to let the grief and the scrutiny coexist, and to trust that a history as terrible as this one can bear the weight of rigorous examination.

The scholars who doubted the old numbers did the island a service, as did the curators who kept the story alive for decades when almost no one else was telling it. Between their work lies a more truthful Gorée: a small island that was a real colonial trading post, a real participant in the slave trade, a real home to a vibrant Afro-European society, and a real symbol of the larger tragedy of which it was a part. We need not choose among these. The island is large enough to hold them all.

If Gorée's mix of memorial and contested memory draws you, continue to Robben Island — South Africa, another small island that stands off the coast of a great city and has been transformed from a place of confinement into a place of conscience. For a very different Atlantic port, shaped by the same maritime empires, try Old Havana and its Fortification System — Cuba, where the walls and the harbour speak of the commerce that once linked the Americas, Europe and Africa.

✦ Conclusion

The Island of Gorée is one of the smallest of the world's great heritage sites and one of the heaviest in symbolic weight. Inscribed in 1978 among the first twelve properties on the World Heritage List, it holds up an image of the Atlantic slave trade as a tragedy that can be seen, walked through and mourned, and it has done so for generations of visitors from Africa and its diaspora. Its architecture, the signares' houses, the fortifications and the Door of No Return, forms a landscape in which history and memory are interwoven.

At the same time, the island demands that its visitors reckon with the limits of what is actually known. The scale of the trade at Gorée has been vigorously contested by serious historians, whose findings suggest that the numbers once attached to the House of Slaves were far too high, even as most scholars gathered in Dakar affirmed the island's importance as a slave port and as a site of remembrance. To visit Gorée well is to carry both conclusions in mind, to walk its lanes with an open heart and a questioning mind, and to leave convinced that honesty and compassion are allies in understanding the past.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

1. When was the Island of Gorée inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site?

Gorée was inscribed in 1978 under cultural criterion (vi), as one of the first twelve properties placed on the World Heritage List. Its UNESCO reference number is 26.

2. Was Gorée really the largest slave-trading centre in Africa?

That is how UNESCO's official description characterises it, but many historians dispute the claim. Quantitative studies by scholars such as Philip Curtin and David Eltis suggest that far fewer captives were embarked at Gorée than at other points along the coast, and that figures in the millions are not supported by records. At the same time, a conference of scholars in Dakar affirmed that Gorée had played a significant role as a slave port while acknowledging the limits of the statistics.

3. What is the Door of No Return?

It is the narrow doorway at the back of the House of Slaves that opens onto the Atlantic and that is traditionally presented as the point through which captives were led onto ships. Historians question how important it was as an embarkation point, but it has become a powerful symbol and a place of pilgrimage for visitors from the African diaspora.

4. How do I get to Gorée from Dakar?

Passenger ferries leave from the port of Dakar, and the crossing takes about thirty minutes. The island is pedestrian-only. Bring your passport to enter the port, and check current ferry times with the port authority, since service has recently been reduced at times.

5. When is the best time to visit?

The pleasantest weather runs from mid-November to mid-June. A weekday visit, preferably an early boat, avoids the crowds, and the House of Slaves is closed on Mondays.
