A Place That Must Be Remembered

Most World Heritage sites are celebrated for beauty or ingenuity. Auschwitz Birkenau is protected for a different reason: because it records, with unbearable clarity, the worst that human beings are capable of doing to one another. It is a place of silence and of evidence. The brick barracks, the watchtowers, the railway tracks, the ruins of the gas chambers and the vast collections of belongings left behind by those who were killed are not monuments to achievement but testimony to a crime.

The camp was built by Nazi Germany in occupied Poland, beginning in 1940, and it grew into the largest of the concentration and extermination camps of the regime. It combined forced labour with systematic mass murder, and it became the principal site of the genocide of the Jews of Europe. Approximately 1.1 million people were murdered here, the great majority of them Jews, together with Poles, Roma and Sinti, Soviet prisoners of war and others. Their deaths were not an accident of war but the deliberate result of an ideology of racial hatred.

UNESCO inscribed the site in 1979 and, from the beginning, treated it as an exception. This post is not a conventional heritage guide. It is an account, as accurate and as restrained as possible, of what happened at Auschwitz, why it is protected, how it is preserved and how visitors can approach it with the seriousness it demands. Many of the facts are painful. They are recorded here because remembering them is the purpose of the site.

Oświęcim Before the Camp

The camp took its name from the nearby town of Oświęcim, which the Germans called Auschwitz. Before the war it was an ordinary Polish provincial town on the Soła and Vistula rivers, a railway junction with a mixed population, and a Jewish community that made up roughly half of its inhabitants. Its synagogues, shops and workshops were part of the same social fabric as the rest of the town, a reminder that the victims of the Holocaust were neighbours, craftsmen, teachers and families before they were statistics.

After the German invasion of Poland in September 1939, the region was annexed to the Reich and the town was renamed. The Germans identified the area as suitable for a camp because of its railway connections and its isolation, and a complex of former Polish army barracks on the edge of the town offered an immediate starting point. The decision to build there would transform the town’s name into a word that stands for genocide.

1940: A Camp for Polish Prisoners

Auschwitz began as a concentration camp for Polish political prisoners. The first transport, of about 700 Poles from the town of Tarnów, arrived in June 1940. The prisoners were set to work building and expanding the camp, and they were subjected to hunger, beatings, disease and arbitrary killing by the SS guards. Over the following months and years the camp received Poles from across the occupied country, and later prisoners from many other nations.

The ironic sign above the main gate, which bore the German words for work that makes one free, became one of the best-known symbols of the Holocaust. The sign now displayed at the gate is a replica, because the original was stolen in 2009; it was recovered and has been conserved by the museum. The sign’s cynicism was characteristic of a system that disguised murder as labour.

Birkenau: The Machinery of Extermination

As the camp population grew, the SS began construction in October 1941 of a much larger camp at Birkenau, about three kilometres away. Originally intended to house Soviet prisoners of war, it became in 1942 the main site of the mass murder of Jews. Trains arrived from across German-occupied and allied Europe, from France, the Netherlands, Greece, Hungary and many other countries, carrying men, women and children who had been told they were being resettled.

On arrival at the railway ramp, the SS carried out a selection. Those judged fit to work were registered and put to forced labour under brutal conditions. The rest, including nearly all children, the elderly and many women, were sent directly to the gas chambers. The camp was designed on an industrial scale, and the efficiency of the process is a central part of what makes Auschwitz historically significant: it reveals a modern state turning its administrative and technical capabilities to the purpose of murder.

Who Was Murdered

The Auschwitz-Birkenau State Museum estimates that approximately 1.3 million people were deported to the camp and that about 1.1 million of them died. The great majority, in the order of 960,000, were Jews. Other victims included some 70,000 to 75,000 Poles, about 20,000 Roma and Sinti, thousands of Soviet prisoners of war and many people of other nationalities. They came from nearly every country of occupied Europe.

The numbers are so large that they risk becoming abstract. The museum’s work aims to restore individuality, through names, photographs, letters and personal belongings, so that visitors encounter not an anonymous mass but human beings with lives, families and futures that were taken from them.

Resistance and Testimony

Even in the camp, there was resistance. Prisoners organised underground networks to share food, smuggle information and help the weak. Witold Pilecki, a Polish officer, deliberately had himself arrested in 1940 in order to gather intelligence about the camp, and he sent reports to the Polish resistance before escaping in 1943. In August 1944 members of the Sonderkommando, prisoners forced to work in the crematoria, took photographs in secret to document what was happening, and on 7 October 1944 they staged an armed revolt in which one crematorium was destroyed.

Other acts were quieter. Maximilian Kolbe, a Polish Franciscan priest, died in 1941 after volunteering to take the place of another prisoner condemned to a starvation cell; he was canonised in 1982. Survivors such as Primo Levi and Elie Wiesel later gave testimony that shaped the world’s understanding of the Holocaust. Their words, along with those of thousands of others, are the most important legacy of the camp.

Liberation and the Beginning of Memory

As Soviet forces advanced in the winter of 1944 and 1945, the SS evacuated tens of thousands of prisoners on death marches toward the west, and attempted to destroy evidence by demolishing the crematoria. On 27 January 1945 the Red Army reached the camp and found about 7,000 prisoners still alive, most of them ill and exhausted. That date is now observed as International Holocaust Remembrance Day.

Memory began immediately. Survivors and Polish authorities recognised that the camp must be preserved, and in 1947 the Polish parliament established the Auschwitz-Birkenau State Museum on the site. In the decades that followed, the museum created exhibitions, an archive and research programmes, and an International Monument to the Victims of Fascism was unveiled at Birkenau in 1967. The preserved camp became both a place of mourning and a centre of study.

Why a Single Criterion

UNESCO inscribed the site in 1979 under criterion (vi) alone, which concerns places directly or tangibly associated with events, traditions, ideas or beliefs of outstanding universal significance. The Committee recognised Auschwitz Birkenau as a monument to the deliberate genocide of the Jews by the Nazi German regime and to the deaths of countless others, and as evidence of one of the greatest crimes against humanity.

The Committee took the unusual step of deciding that the camp was a unique case and that the inscription of other sites of a similar nature should be restricted. In 2007 it changed the name of the property to the present one, which states plainly that this was a German Nazi concentration and extermination camp. The Polish government has emphasised that the site was inscribed under criterion (vi) alone, a reminder of the particular emotional and historical weight that surrounds it.

The Landscape as Evidence

What makes Auschwitz Birkenau extraordinary as a heritage site is that it survives. At Auschwitz I, rows of brick barracks, now housing exhibitions, stand much as they did in the 1940s, with their watchtowers, double fences and the preserved gas chamber and crematorium. At Birkenau, the surviving barracks, the foundations of hundreds of others, the main gatehouse, the railway ramp and the ruined crematoria give a sense of the vast scale of the site.

The landscape is not a reconstruction but an artefact. The management of the site is guided by a principle of preserving rather than restoring, so that the evidence is kept as it is, including its decay. A visitor walking along the railway tracks at Birkenau stands in the same space where those who arrived were forced to walk, and the plain, open ground is part of what makes the experience so powerful.

The Work of Preservation

Preserving the camp is a continuing and costly task. The brick barracks, wooden structures and ruins deteriorate through weather and time, and the museum holds hundreds of thousands of fragile items, including shoes, spectacles, suitcases, clothes and documents, which need constant conservation. The museum’s conservation laboratories work to stabilise the objects without altering them, using techniques designed to maintain their authenticity.

In 2009 an international endowment, the Auschwitz-Birkenau Foundation, was created to fund long-term conservation, supported by governments and donors from around the world. The foundation reflects a shared understanding that the preservation of Auschwitz is a responsibility not only of Poland but of humanity.

Protecting the Surroundings

The memorial is surrounded by a living town, and protecting the setting has been a long-standing challenge. In the early 2000s UNESCO and its advisers urged Poland to define a buffer zone, to manage development in the area around the camps and to prepare a management plan. Concerns were raised about a planned expressway near the property, and a revised route was later prepared that was expected to have little impact.

The issues are sensitive because local residents have legitimate needs, while the former camps require quiet and dignity. The solutions have involved consultation, local planning rules and cooperation between the museum, the town and national authorities. The principle is that the surroundings should not be allowed to compromise the solemn character of the site.

Visiting With Care

More than two million people have visited the site in peak years, and visiting Auschwitz is a serious undertaking. Entry to the grounds is regulated, and at busy times it is necessary to reserve a timed pass or a guided tour in advance. A full visit to both Auschwitz I and Birkenau takes several hours, and the two sites are connected by a shuttle service. The museum recommends that children under 14 should not visit.

Visitors are asked to behave with dignity: to dress appropriately, to speak quietly, to refrain from taking photographs in places where they are prohibited and to avoid any behaviour that disrespects the dead. Many people find it helpful to take a guided tour, to read the testimonies beforehand and to allow time afterwards to reflect. The aim is not entertainment but understanding.

Education and the Duty of Memory

The museum is also an educational institution. It hosts thousands of students and teachers every year, runs seminars and publishes research, and it works to ensure that the facts of the Holocaust remain widely known at a time when the generation of survivors is passing away. Every year on 27 January, survivors and leaders gather at Birkenau to mark the anniversary of the liberation, and their words carry a warning that antisemitism and hatred have not disappeared.

The ultimate purpose of the site is not only to remember the past but to guide the future. By recording what ideology, indifference and the machinery of the state were capable of producing, Auschwitz Birkenau asks every visitor to consider their own responsibility: to defend the dignity of others, to resist prejudice and to recognise the early signs of hatred before they harden into policy.

Reflection — Memory as a Moral Act

To visit Auschwitz is to discover that understanding is not the same as comprehension. The facts can be learned and the numbers recited, but the mind resists the scale of what happened. What remains is the discipline of memory: to say names, to preserve objects, to keep the ground as it is and to pass on the testimony of those who survived. Memory in this sense is not passive remembrance but a moral act.

It is a task shared with other places that bear witness to suffering, such as Hiroshima Peace Memorial (Genbaku Dome), Japan and Robben Island, South Africa. Each is protected not because it is beautiful, but because it teaches something that humanity cannot afford to forget.

✦ Conclusion

Auschwitz Birkenau stands in the landscape of southern Poland as a monument to the victims of genocide and to the responsibility of memory. It preserves the physical evidence of a crime that must not be denied, minimised or forgotten, and it holds the stories of those who suffered and those who resisted. Its inscription as a World Heritage site recognised that this heritage belongs to all humanity.

Visiting it is not easy, and it is not meant to be. But for those who go with respect and attention, the experience leaves a lasting understanding of what hatred can do and of why the protection of human dignity must never be taken for granted.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

What is Auschwitz Birkenau and where is it?

Auschwitz Birkenau was the largest Nazi German concentration and extermination camp, built in occupied Poland near the town of Oświęcim, about 60 kilometres west of Kraków. It consists of the main camp, Auschwitz I, and the much larger Birkenau camp, Auschwitz II, and is preserved as the Auschwitz-Birkenau State Museum.

How many people died at Auschwitz?

The State Museum estimates that about 1.3 million people were deported to Auschwitz and that approximately 1.1 million died, the great majority of them Jews. Others included Poles, Roma and Sinti, Soviet prisoners of war and people of other nationalities.

Why is Auschwitz a UNESCO World Heritage Site?

It was inscribed in 1979 under criterion (vi) as a monument to the deliberate genocide of the Jews by the Nazi German regime and to the deaths of countless others. The World Heritage Committee treated it as a unique case and restricted the inscription of similar sites.

When was Auschwitz liberated?

Soviet troops reached the camp on 27 January 1945 and found about 7,000 surviving prisoners. The anniversary is observed as International Holocaust Remembrance Day.

How should visitors prepare for a visit?

Reserve a timed pass or guided tour in advance, allow several hours for both camps, dress respectfully, keep quiet and follow the museum’s rules on photography. The museum recommends that children under 14 do not visit. Reading survivors’ testimonies beforehand can help prepare for the experience.
