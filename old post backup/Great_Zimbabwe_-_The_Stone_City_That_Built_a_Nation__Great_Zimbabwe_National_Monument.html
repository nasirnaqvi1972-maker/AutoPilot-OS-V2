The Name and the Nation

Zimbabwe takes its name from this place. Not metaphorically, not as a distant historical reference, but directly and deliberately: when the former Rhodesia became Zimbabwe at independence in 1980, the new nation chose as its name the Shona words dzimba dza mabwe — houses of stone — in honour of the medieval stone city that stands in the southeastern part of the country and that represents, more completely than any other single monument, the evidence of a great and sophisticated African civilisation that flourished here before the European colonial period.

The choice was political, cultural, and profoundly symbolic. For nearly a century, the ruins of Great Zimbabwe had been at the centre of one of the most dishonest debates in the history of archaeology: a debate in which colonial authorities, amateur archaeologists, and white settlers in Rhodesia systematically denied, fabricated alternative explanations for, and suppressed scientific evidence of the African origin of the ruins, because the existence of a great pre-colonial African civilisation capable of building on this scale was incompatible with the ideological framework of racial hierarchy on which colonial rule depended. To name the new nation Zimbabwe was to assert, at the moment of independence, that the story of African civilisation had been told falsely, and that the truth — the truth that Great Zimbabwe was built by the ancestors of the Shona people, without foreign assistance, in the eleventh to fifteenth centuries CE — was now reclaimed.

UNESCO inscribed Great Zimbabwe on the World Heritage List in 1986, under criteria (i), (iii), and (vi), recognising it as a masterpiece of African stone construction, an outstanding testimony to a vanished civilisation, and a monument of outstanding universal cultural significance. It is the most important archaeological site in sub-Saharan Africa and one of the most important in the entire African continent.

The Kingdom of Zimbabwe — Gold, Cattle, and Stone

The civilisation that built Great Zimbabwe — conventionally known to archaeologists as the Zimbabwe Culture or the Great Zimbabwe state — flourished between approximately the eleventh and fifteenth centuries CE in the highlands of what is now Zimbabwe. It was a Shona-speaking society whose wealth was based on three foundations: cattle, which in Shona society as in many African cultures represented both practical wealth and social status; gold and ivory, which were traded with the Swahili coast merchants of the Indian Ocean trade network; and control of the trade routes that connected the interior gold fields of the Zimbabwe plateau with the coastal trading ports of Sofala and Kilwa.

The scale of the gold trade that passed through Great Zimbabwe at the height of its power is attested by the extraordinary range of imported luxury goods found in the archaeological excavations of the site: Chinese celadon ceramics of the Sung and Ming Dynasties, Persian faience, glass beads from India and the Arab world, and other objects that demonstrate a level of connection to the international trade networks of the Indian Ocean that is far greater than most accounts of pre-colonial African history acknowledge. Great Zimbabwe was not a provincial backwater. It was a node in one of the most important long-distance trading networks of the medieval world.

The population of the Great Zimbabwe state at its height is estimated at between 10,000 and 18,000 people — making it, for its time and its region, a substantial urban centre. The stone enclosures that give the site its name were not the homes of ordinary people but the residences of the ruling elite — the mambo (king) and his family and retinue — while the general population lived in more conventional structures of mud, thatch, and timber in the surrounding areas.

The Architecture — Dry Stone Masonry Without Parallel

The construction technique of Great Zimbabwe is one of the most remarkable achievements of pre-colonial African building. The walls are built entirely of dry stone — granite blocks split from the natural rock outcrops of the surrounding area and fitted together without mortar, their stability depending entirely on the precision of the fitting and the skill of the builders in selecting and placing each stone. The quality of the construction varies across the site — the most carefully built sections, with courses of uniformly sized and precisely fitted granite blocks, are among the finest examples of dry stone masonry anywhere in the world — but even the less refined sections demonstrate a command of the technique and a practical understanding of structural principles that is genuinely impressive.

The Great Enclosure — the largest single ancient structure in sub-Saharan Africa — is the most impressive of the Great Zimbabwe stone buildings. Its outer wall, approximately 250 metres in circumference, reaches 11 metres in height and 5 metres in thickness at its maximum, enclosing an area of approximately 18,000 square metres. The wall is not a defensive structure in the conventional military sense — it has no battlements, no arrow slits, no towers. It is a wall of prestige and of spatial definition — a statement of the ruler's power expressed in the most direct architectural language available to the builders: the size and the quality of the stone construction.

Within the Great Enclosure, the most remarkable structure is the Conical Tower — a solid cylinder of dry stone approximately 9 metres tall and 5 metres in diameter at the base, standing free within the enclosure without any apparent structural function. Its purpose remains debated: it may represent a symbolic grain bin — a statement of the ruler's wealth and his ability to feed his people — or a phallus, or a cosmological marker. Whatever its intended meaning, it is the most accomplished piece of pure stone construction at the site, its courses perfectly regular, its slightly tapering profile graceful in a way that suggests aesthetic as well as purely structural calculation.

The Zimbabwe Birds — Symbols of a Nation

Among the most celebrated objects found at Great Zimbabwe are eight carved soapstone birds — the Zimbabwe Birds — discovered during the excavations of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries in the Hill Complex of the site. The birds — approximately 40 centimetres tall, each carved from a single piece of soapstone, each mounted on a tall soapstone monolith — are among the most significant works of pre-colonial African art ever found, combining naturalistic bird forms with human features (feet rather than talons, lips rather than beaks) in a way that suggests they represent spiritual intermediaries between the human and the divine — perhaps the ancestral spirits of Shona religion, or the ruler himself in his spiritual aspect.

The Zimbabwe Bird has become the national symbol of Zimbabwe — appearing on the national flag, the national coat of arms, and the country's banknotes — its adoption at independence a further expression of the new nation's reclamation of the Great Zimbabwe heritage as the foundation of its national identity. Five of the original eight birds are in the Great Zimbabwe Site Museum. Two are in the Museum of Human Sciences in Harare. One was removed to South Africa during the colonial period and has been the subject of repatriation discussions.

The Colonial Denial — A Shameful Chapter in Archaeological History

The story of how Great Zimbabwe was interpreted — and misinterpreted — during the colonial period is one of the most troubling episodes in the history of archaeology. When the ruins were first systematically described by the European traveller Karl Mauch in 1871, the question of who built them was immediately contentious. Mauch himself proposed that they were the work of the Queen of Sheba — a theory without any archaeological basis whatsoever, motivated by the assumption that sub-Saharan Africans were incapable of building stone structures of this scale and complexity.

Over the following decades, as Rhodesia was colonised by the British South Africa Company under Cecil Rhodes, the colonial authorities actively suppressed scientific findings that pointed to African origins for the ruins. The archaeologist James Theodore Bent, commissioned to excavate the site in 1891, concluded — against the evidence — that the ruins were of non-African origin. When the professional archaeologist David Randall-MacIver conducted a rigorous excavation in 1905 and concluded, on the basis of the ceramic and stratigraphic evidence, that the ruins were of medieval African origin, his findings were dismissed and attacked by the colonial establishment. Gertrude Caton-Thompson's 1929 excavation reached the same conclusion with even stronger evidence, and was similarly dismissed.

The scientific consensus — established beyond reasonable doubt by the mid-twentieth century — that Great Zimbabwe was built by the ancestors of the Shona people of Zimbabwe has been accepted by the international archaeological community since at least the 1970s. The colonial denial of this truth was not merely an academic error. It was a deliberate act of ideological falsification in the service of a racist political system, and its effects on the history of the region, on the self-understanding of its people, and on the development of African archaeology more broadly were deeply damaging and are not yet fully overcome.

Reflection — The Truth the Stones Tell

Great Zimbabwe does not need defence. The stones speak for themselves — and what they say is unambiguous. A civilisation of the Shona-speaking people of the Zimbabwe plateau, in the eleventh to fifteenth centuries CE, built here one of the most impressive stone structures in sub-Saharan Africa, traded gold and ivory with the merchants of the Indian Ocean world, and created a kingdom of sufficient power and complexity to control the highland plateau of southern Africa for several centuries.

The denial of this truth by colonial ideology was a failure of intellectual honesty so egregious that it has distorted the history of southern African archaeology for generations. The recovery of the truth — through the patient, rigorous work of archaeologists of multiple nationalities over more than a century, and through the political act of naming a new nation after the ruins that the colonial system tried to claim for everyone but their actual builders — is one of the more satisfying episodes in the long story of heritage and identity in Africa.

Great Zimbabwe is what it always was: the capital of a great African kingdom, built by African hands, for African rulers, as an expression of African power and African ambition. UNESCO recognised this in 1986. Zimbabwe recognised it in 1980. The stones recognised it from the beginning.

✦ Conclusion

Great Zimbabwe is one of the most important places in Africa — not in spite of the attempts that were made to falsify its history, but in a sense because of them. The fact that the truth of its origins had to be fought for — that it took more than a century of archaeological work and ultimately a political revolution to establish, beyond serious dispute, that this great stone city was built by the African ancestors of the Zimbabwean people — gives the site a dimension of significance that goes beyond the purely archaeological. It is a monument to the truth of African history, to the sophistication of pre-colonial African civilisation, and to the determination of a people to reclaim the heritage that was taken from them.

The UNESCO inscription of 1986 was part of that reclamation. So was the naming of Zimbabwe in 1980. So is every visit to the site by every person — Zimbabwean or foreign, African or not — who stands before the Great Enclosure and understands what the stones say: that here, in the eleventh to fifteenth centuries, a great African civilisation built something worthy of the name, and that the world is richer for knowing it.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: Who built Great Zimbabwe? Great Zimbabwe was built by the ancestors of the Shona people of Zimbabwe, between approximately the eleventh and fifteenth centuries CE. This conclusion, established by rigorous archaeological evidence, is accepted by the entire international archaeological community. Colonial-era claims attributing the ruins to non-African builders have been thoroughly discredited.

Q2: What does the name Zimbabwe mean? Zimbabwe derives from the Shona words dzimba dza mabwe, meaning houses of stone. The name was adopted for the new nation at independence in 1980 as a direct reference to the Great Zimbabwe ruins and as an assertion of the African civilisational heritage that the colonial period had sought to deny.

Q3: What are the Zimbabwe Birds? The Zimbabwe Birds are eight carved soapstone figures found in the Hill Complex of Great Zimbabwe, each depicting a bird with human features. They are among the most important works of pre-colonial African art ever discovered and have been adopted as the national symbol of Zimbabwe, appearing on the flag, coat of arms, and banknotes of the country.

Q4: Is Great Zimbabwe a UNESCO World Heritage Site? Yes. Great Zimbabwe National Monument was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1986, recognised under criteria (i), (iii), and (vi) as a masterpiece of African stone construction, an outstanding testimony to the Zimbabwe civilisation, and a monument of outstanding universal cultural and historical significance.

Q5: How do I get to Great Zimbabwe? Great Zimbabwe is located approximately 30 kilometres from the town of Masvingo in southeastern Zimbabwe. Masvingo is accessible by road from Harare (approximately 290 km, 3–4 hours) and by bus from Harare and Bulawayo. Organised tours from Harare and Victoria Falls are available. The site is open daily and has a site museum housing the Zimbabwe Birds.
