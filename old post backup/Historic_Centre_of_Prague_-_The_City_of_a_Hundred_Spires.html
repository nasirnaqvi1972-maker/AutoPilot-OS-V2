The City That Survived

Prague is the most complete medieval and baroque city in Central Europe — a distinction it owes not to exceptional foresight but to exceptional luck. While Dresden was destroyed by Allied bombing in 1945, Warsaw was deliberately demolished by German forces, and Vienna was heavily damaged in the final campaigns of the Second World War, Prague was liberated by the Red Army in May 1945 without the kind of sustained urban combat that destroyed other Central European capitals. The historic city — its castle, its Gothic cathedral, its baroque palaces and churches, its medieval bridges and towers — survived the twentieth century essentially intact.

The result is a city of extraordinary architectural density and continuity: a place where it is possible to walk from a Romanesque rotunda of the eleventh century through Gothic churches of the fourteenth, Renaissance palaces of the sixteenth, baroque masterpieces of the seventeenth and eighteenth, art nouveau facades of the early twentieth, and cubist apartment buildings of the 1910s and 1920s — styles found nowhere else in the world in such concentrated form — all within the compass of a few hundred metres and without leaving the historic city centre that UNESCO inscribed on the World Heritage List in 1992.

The name most intimately associated with Prague's greatest period of building is that of the Holy Roman Emperor Charles IV — King of Bohemia, later Emperor, and the most ambitious ruler the city ever had — who in the mid-fourteenth century transformed Prague from a significant Central European capital into one of the great cities of Europe: founding Charles University (the oldest university in Central Europe), building the Charles Bridge, commissioning the Gothic rebuilding of St Vitus Cathedral, and establishing the New Town — Nové Město — as a planned urban extension of medieval Prague of extraordinary ambition. Charles IV's Prague was, for a brief period in the late fourteenth century, arguably the most important city in Europe north of the Alps.

Prague Castle — The Largest Ancient Castle in the World

Prague Castle — Pražský hrad — is the largest ancient castle complex in the world by area, covering approximately 70,000 square metres on Hradčany hill on the western bank of the Vltava. It has been the seat of Bohemian kings, Holy Roman Emperors, and Czech presidents for more than a thousand years — a continuity of political function unmatched by any comparable structure in Europe. The castle is not a single building but a complex of palaces, churches, gardens, and courtyards accumulated over ten centuries of continuous occupation and construction, from the early Romanesque structures of the ninth and tenth centuries through the Gothic, Renaissance, baroque, and twentieth-century additions that have transformed it into the heterogeneous but extraordinary ensemble that stands today.

At the heart of the castle complex stands St Vitus Cathedral — the Gothic masterpiece that Charles IV commissioned in 1344 and that was not completed until 1929, nearly six centuries later. The cathedral is one of the great achievements of Gothic architecture in Central Europe: its nave, with its soaring vaulted ceiling; its chapels, the most important of which — the Chapel of St Wenceslas — is decorated with more than 1,300 semi-precious stones set into the plaster walls and with fourteenth-century frescoes of the life of the patron saint of Bohemia; and its south tower with its famous rose window together constitute a building of exceptional beauty and historical depth.

The crown jewels of Bohemia — the Crown of St Wenceslas, the Royal Orb, and the Royal Sceptre — are kept in a chamber within the cathedral, secured by seven locks whose keys are held by seven different constitutional officials, from the President of the Republic to the Archbishop of Prague. They are displayed to the public only on the most exceptional occasions. The Crown of St Wenceslas is one of the finest medieval crowns in the world and one of the most potent symbols of Czech national identity.

The Charles Bridge — Stone Over the Vltava

The Charles Bridge — Karlův most — is the most celebrated structure in Prague and one of the most famous medieval bridges in the world. Built between 1357 and approximately 1402 CE under the patronage of Emperor Charles IV, it crosses the Vltava River on 16 arches of sandstone, stretching 516 metres in length and 10 metres in width, connecting the Old Town on the eastern bank with the Lesser Town and the castle on the western bank. For nearly five hundred years — until the construction of the first supplementary bridge in 1841 — it was the only crossing of the Vltava at Prague, making it not merely a bridge but the strategic and commercial axis of the entire city.

The bridge is lined with 30 baroque statues and statue groups — added between 1683 and 1714 — depicting saints and religious figures of the Catholic Counter-Reformation tradition, their dramatic gestures and swirling draperies creating one of the finest outdoor sculpture galleries in Europe. The most celebrated of the statues is the bronze figure of St John of Nepomuk — the fourteenth-century Czech priest who was thrown from the bridge by King Wenceslas IV and drowned in the Vltava — whose plaque has been touched by so many millions of pilgrims and tourists over the centuries that the bronze is worn to a high shine at the points of contact.

The experience of walking the Charles Bridge — at dawn, before the tourist crowds arrive, with the morning mist on the Vltava and the castle rising above the western bank and the baroque statues dark against the pale sky — is one of the finest urban experiences available anywhere in Europe. It is a walk through layers of history so compressed and so beautifully composed that even the most historically indifferent visitor is likely to be moved.

The Old Town Square — The Heart of Prague

The Old Town Square — Staroměstské náměstí — is the historic centre of Prague's historic centre: the great public space around which the civic, commercial, and religious life of medieval Prague organised itself, and which remains, in the twenty-first century, the most visited and most photographed public space in the city. Its architecture spans seven centuries — the Gothic Church of Our Lady before Týn with its distinctive twin towers dominating the eastern side; the baroque St Nicholas Church on the northern side; the Romanesque and Gothic Old Town Hall with its astronomical clock on the western side; and the Renaissance and baroque private palaces filling the remaining edges of the square.

The Astronomical Clock — the Orloj — is the oldest functioning astronomical clock in the world, installed in 1410 and still performing its hourly mechanical spectacle for the crowds that gather below it every hour of the day and most of the night. The clock face shows not merely the time but the position of the sun and moon in the zodiac, the phases of the moon, the time of sunrise and sunset, and the current season — a compressed representation of the medieval understanding of astronomical time that is itself a minor masterpiece of medieval scientific and artistic thought. On the hour, a procession of mechanical figures — the twelve Apostles, Death, Vanity, Greed, and the Turkish infidel — parade past the windows above the clock face in a mechanical theatre that has been performing, without interruption, for more than six centuries.

The Lesser Town and Malá Strana — Baroque Perfection

On the western bank of the Vltava, below Prague Castle and above the river, lies Malá Strana — the Lesser Town — the most completely preserved baroque urban district in Central Europe. Built largely in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries on the ruins of the Renaissance town that preceded it, Malá Strana is a neighbourhood of baroque palaces, churches, gardens, and winding lanes whose architectural character is so consistent and so complete that walking through it feels less like visiting a historic district and more like stepping into the interior of a single, very large baroque composition.

The centrepiece of Malá Strana is the Church of St Nicholas — the greatest baroque church in Bohemia and one of the finest in all of Central Europe, its green dome and bell tower dominating the Lesser Town Square. Built between 1704 and 1755 by the father and son architects Christoph and Kilian Ignaz Dientzenhofer, its interior is one of the most exuberantly decorated spaces in baroque architecture: frescoed ceilings of trompe l'oeil grandeur, gilded altars, marble columns, and a sense of spatial drama achieved through the complex geometry of the nave that is the hallmark of the Dientzenhofer baroque at its most accomplished.

Prague's Jewish Quarter — Josefov

Within the historic centre of Prague lies Josefov — the former Jewish quarter, now one of the most significant Jewish heritage sites in Europe. The Jewish community of Prague is one of the oldest in Central Europe, with documentary evidence of Jewish settlement in the city dating to the tenth century. The six synagogues of Josefov — the Old-New Synagogue (the oldest functioning synagogue in Europe, built in 1270), the Spanish Synagogue, the Pinkas Synagogue, the Maisel Synagogue, the High Synagogue, and the Klaus Synagogue — together with the Old Jewish Cemetery constitute an ensemble of Jewish heritage of outstanding historical and cultural significance.

The Pinkas Synagogue is perhaps the most affecting of the six — its interior walls covered with the hand-painted names of 77,297 Czech and Moravian Jewish victims of the Holocaust, their names inscribed alphabetically by family group in red and black paint on the white plaster walls of the synagogue in a work of memorial art of overwhelming power and simplicity. The Old Jewish Cemetery — where burials took place between approximately 1439 and 1787, with up to twelve layers of graves stacked one above the other because of the limited space available — is one of the most remarkable archaeological and cultural landscapes in Europe: a dense accumulation of tilted and leaning gravestones, its surface uneven with the pressure of the layers beneath, the oldest stones worn nearly smooth by time and weather.

Reflection — The City That Time Preserved

Prague is one of those cities — rare enough to deserve special acknowledgement — in which the experience of walking is simultaneously an experience of history, of architecture, and of beauty, without any of the three requiring sacrifice of the other two. The preservation of the historic centre is the result of a combination of fortune (the city was not destroyed in either world war), political circumstance (the communist period limited development but also limited demolition), and the extraordinary quality of what was built here across ten centuries of continuous urban development.

What makes Prague exceptional is not merely the survival of individual monuments — though the castle, the cathedral, the bridge, and the squares are all of the highest quality — but the survival of the urban fabric that connects them: the streets, the lanes, the courtyards, the rooflines, the sequence of spaces and views that give the historic centre its particular character. To walk from the Old Town Square across the Charles Bridge to the Lesser Town and up to the castle is to make a journey through European history so complete and so beautifully composed that it constitutes, in itself, one of the finest urban experiences available anywhere on the continent.

✦ Conclusion

The Historic Centre of Prague is one of the great cities of the world — not merely one of the great historic cities, but one of the great cities, full stop. Its combination of natural setting, architectural quality, historical depth, and urban completeness is equalled by very few places in Europe or anywhere else. The castle on the hill, the cathedral reaching upward from the castle, the bridge across the river, the towers and spires of the old town on the eastern bank — these are the components of a composition of extraordinary beauty that has been accumulating, layer by layer, for a thousand years, and that UNESCO recognised in 1992 as one of the outstanding examples of urban heritage anywhere on Earth.

To visit Prague is to understand, in the most direct possible way, what a great European city looks and feels like when its historic fabric has survived intact — when the streets and squares and buildings that were laid down over a thousand years of continuous urban development are still there, still inhabited, still defining the character of a living city. It is a rare gift to the world, and one that deserves the protection and the reverence that the UNESCO inscription of 1992 was intended to ensure.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: Why is Prague called the City of a Hundred Spires? Prague earned the nickname the City of a Hundred Spires from its extraordinary density of church towers, castle spires, and civic towers visible across the roofline of the historic centre. The actual count of spires exceeds several hundred when all the towers of the historic city are included. The nickname reflects the dominant characteristic of the Prague skyline — its vertical punctuation by an exceptional number of towers of different periods and architectural styles.

Q2: What is the Astronomical Clock in Prague? The Prague Astronomical Clock — the Orloj — is installed on the southern wall of the Old Town Hall in the Old Town Square. First installed in 1410, it is the oldest functioning astronomical clock in the world. On the hour, a mechanical procession of the twelve Apostles and allegorical figures performs a spectacle that has been running continuously for more than six centuries.

Q3: How large is Prague Castle? Prague Castle covers approximately 70,000 square metres — approximately 7 hectares — making it the largest ancient castle complex in the world by area. It contains palaces, churches, gardens, a cathedral, galleries, and administrative buildings accumulated over more than a thousand years of continuous occupation.

Q4: Is the Historic Centre of Prague a UNESCO World Heritage Site? Yes. The Historic Centre of Prague was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1992, recognised under criteria (ii), (iv), and (vi) as one of the outstanding examples of European historic urban landscape.

Q5: What is the best time to visit Prague? Spring (April–May) and autumn (September–October) offer the best combination of mild weather and manageable crowds. Summer (June–August) is the busiest and most crowded period. December is magical for the Christmas markets but very cold. The Charles Bridge and Old Town Square are best experienced very early in the morning, before the tourist crowds arrive.
