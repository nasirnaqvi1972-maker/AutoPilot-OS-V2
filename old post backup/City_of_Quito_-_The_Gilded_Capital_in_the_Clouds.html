A City Above the Clouds

There are cities that rise from plains and cities that are lowered into valleys, but Quito is a city that was lifted. It sits at roughly 2,850 metres above sea level on the eastern slopes of the Pichincha volcano, a long, narrow ribbon of streets laid out along a high Andean basin, with snow-capped Cotopaxi sometimes floating above the haze to the south on a clear morning. The air is thin, the light is hard and brilliant, and the equator passes only a short drive to the north, so that the sun climbs almost vertically at midday and drops with startling speed at dusk. To arrive here is to be immediately conscious of altitude, in the lungs and in the imagination.

At the centre of this setting lies the historic quarter that UNESCO inscribed in 1978, in the very first year of the World Heritage List. Quito shared that distinction with only eleven other properties worldwide, among them the Galápagos Islands, Aachen Cathedral and the historic centre of Kraków. The choice was no accident. Here, wrapped around steep cobbled lanes and shaded cloister gardens, survives the most extensive and least altered colonial urban core in Latin America — a place where more than a dozen great churches, a score of convents and monasteries, and hundreds of whitewashed houses with carved wooden balconies still form a continuous and living city.

What makes Quito exceptional, however, is not merely that so much has survived but that it has survived in use. Government ministers still cross the Plaza Grande to their offices; the faithful still fill the pews of the Jesuit church for evening mass; vendors still sell fruit from the same corners where their grandmothers sold it. This is not a museum quarter preserved behind a rope, but a capital that has kept faith with its own past, and that quiet continuity is the first thing a visitor feels as the bells begin to sound across the rooftops.

Kitu, the Inca Road and the Fire of Rumiñahui

Long before Spanish masons arrived, the high basin was inhabited by the Quitu, a people whose name survives in the city’s own, and later absorbed into the Cara or Caranqui confederation that held much of the northern Andes. By the late fifteenth century the Incas had pushed their frontier northward in a hard and costly campaign, and Quito became the principal northern city of Tawantinsuyu, linked to Cuzco by the great Andean road system, the Qhapaq Ñan. The place that was later to become a Spanish capital had been, for a generation, an Inca one.

The Inca civil war that broke out between the half-brothers Huáscar and Atahualpa, whose power base lay in the north, left the region exhausted and divided at the very moment Francisco Pizarro landed on the Pacific coast. When Atahualpa was seized and executed at Cajamarca in 1533, his general Rumiñahui is said to have refused to yield Quito to the invaders and set the city ablaze rather than let its treasures and temples fall into Spanish hands. Whatever the precise truth of the story, little of the pre-Hispanic city survives above ground, and the Spanish were obliged to build almost from nothing.

That absence matters to anyone trying to read the historic centre. The Quito that stands today is not an Inca city with a colonial veneer, but a Spanish city raised on the ashes of an Inca one, its plazas and streets overlying older foundations that are glimpsed only in archaeological fragments. The result is a layered, melancholy beauty, in which the memory of the destroyed city is carried less by stones than by the continued presence of the Quechua-speaking communities who still live in and around Quito.

Benalcázar’s Grid and the Founding of 1534

The Spanish city was formally founded on 6 December 1534 by Sebastián de Benalcázar, one of Pizarro’s lieutenants, who had marched north with a small force and who found the Inca capital still smoking. He laid out the new town according to the familiar colonial pattern, a grid of straight streets radiating from a central plaza, with the principal institutions of church and crown placed around its edges. The Plaza Grande, now officially the Plaza de la Independencia, took its place at the centre, and almost every important building in the historic quarter still keeps its position relative to it.

The grid had to contend with a most unpromising landscape. Quito is built across ravines and ridges, and the Spanish solved the problem by filling the deep gullies, called quebradas, and bridging them with arched viaducts, so that the apparently regular streets conceal an underlying topography of considerable drama. Some of the quebradas were buried beneath what are now squares; others survive as a hint of unevenness underfoot. It is one of the great quiet engineering achievements of the colonial Americas, and one that visitors rarely notice because it was done so well.

Quito’s status rose rapidly. In 1563 the Spanish Crown established the Royal Audiencia of Quito, an administrative and judicial court with jurisdiction over a vast territory, and with it came the prestige, the bureaucracy and the wealth that fund a capital’s architecture. Religious orders flocked to the new city, and each brought its own ambitions: the Franciscans, Dominicans, Augustinians, Mercedarians and, finally, the Jesuits all set about building monumental churches and convents on a scale that astonishes for a provincial city at the edge of the empire.

The Quito School — Where Europe and the Andes Met in Paint and Wood

If one thing distinguishes Quito’s artistic legacy from that of its colonial peers, it is the Escuela Quiteña, the Quito School of art that flourished from the sixteenth to the eighteenth centuries. The tradition began with a practical need: the friars required altarpieces, statues and paintings in quantity, and the Franciscans founded a school in the convent of San Francisco, reputedly led by the Flemish friar Jodoco Ricke, to train indigenous and mestizo craftsmen in European techniques. What emerged was neither wholly European nor wholly Andean but a distinctive and extraordinarily accomplished synthesis.

Sculptors, Painters and a Distinctive Visual Language

The painters Miguel de Santiago and Nicolás Javier de Goríbar, the sculptors Bernardo de Legarda and Manuel Chili, known as Caspicara, are among the names that survive from a largely anonymous tradition. Their work blends Spanish realism and Flemish detail with unmistakable local touches — Andean fruits and animals in sacred scenes, the faces of saints bearing indigenous features, polychrome and gold-leaf surfaces of a richness that dazzled European visitors. Quito’s artists became so celebrated that their work was exported across South America and as far as Europe, and the city’s reputation as a cultural capital long outlasted the colonial order itself.

La Compañía and the Baroque Splendour of the Churches

Among the many sacred monuments of the historic centre, none overwhelms the senses quite like the Church of La Compañía de Jesús. Begun in 1605 and finished only after more than a century and a half of work, it is a masterpiece of the Latin American Baroque, with a densely carved stone façade whose ornament recalls the Spanish Churrigueresque and the Italian Baroque, and an interior in which almost every surface is carved, painted or sheathed in gold leaf. The quantity of gilding is often said to run to several tonnes of gold, and while the figures are repeated more than they are verified, the effect is beyond argument: the nave glows like the inside of a reliquary.

The Jesuits were not alone in making Quito a city of churches. The Church and Convent of San Francisco, begun in 1535 and completed over many decades, dominates its sloping plaza with a long white façade and stone atrium, and holds a rich museum of colonial sacred art. Santo Domingo, La Merced and San Agustín each add their own flavour, from the mudéjar-inflected wooden ceilings of the Augustinian church to the ornate pulpits and cloisters of the Mercedarians. In San Agustín in 1809 a group of Quito’s citizens signed the first call for independence from Spain, and its convent still bears witness to that quiet act of defiance.

These monuments are celebrated for their individual beauty, but their true significance, recognised by UNESCO under criteria (ii) and (iv), lies in how they function together as a coherent urban ensemble. Together they form an exceptional demonstration of the way European architecture and art were transplanted to the Americas, absorbing local materials, local craftsmanship and local sensibilities until they became something new.

Earthquakes, Rebuilding and the Question of Authenticity

Quito sits on one of the most seismically active edges of the Andes, and its history is punctuated by earthquakes that have repeatedly tested the resilience of its stone and adobe. Major shocks in 1587, 1797 and 1868, among others, caused extensive destruction and loss of life, and the city’s monuments have been repaired and rebuilt many times. The great tremor of 1917 damaged several important buildings, yet UNESCO’s own evaluation affirms that despite such events, Quito retains the best-preserved and least altered historic centre in Latin America.

The question of authenticity is therefore a subtle one. Few buildings in Quito are untouched originals; most are palimpsests of repair, reconstruction and reinterpretation, in which colonial form has been maintained through successive acts of restoration. Rather than damaging the heritage value of the city, this continuous maintenance is arguably what has kept it alive. A modern preservation programme, begun in the second half of the twentieth century and refined after another damaging earthquake in 1987, has sought to guide restoration with care for materials, techniques and the lived character of the district.

The result is a rare kind of urban authenticity that rests not on frozen perfection but on continuity of function and form. When residents repaint a façade in lime wash, replace a rotted beam in a balcony, or restore an altarpiece with the same traditional methods their forebears used, they are keeping the historic centre of Quito true to its own deepest purpose.

Republican Quito — Independence, Sucre and the Neoclassical City

The nineteenth century gave Quito a second architectural layer. The first cry of independence, raised on 10 August 1809, was quickly suppressed, but the idea survived, and on 24 May 1822 the forces of Antonio José de Sucre won the decisive Battle of Pichincha on the slopes above the city, securing the independence of the Quito region and its eventual place in the Republic of Ecuador. The Cathedral that dominates the Plaza Grande, begun in the sixteenth century and rebuilt repeatedly, shelters the tomb of Sucre, a quiet shrine to the man who is arguably the republic’s foundational hero.

Across the square, the Carondelet Palace, seat of the national government, presents the white, restrained face of republican neoclassicism, its columned façade rebuilt in the early nineteenth century on the site of the colonial Royal Audiencia. Farther along, the grandest statement of the modern era is the Basílica del Voto Nacional, a vast neo-Gothic church begun in 1892 whose gargoyles, in a charmingly subversive touch, take the form of Ecuadorian wildlife rather than medieval demons. Giant tortoises, iguanas and condors cling to the stonework, stubbornly asserting that this is an Andean basilica, not a transplanted European one.

These later additions never overwhelm the colonial core. Instead they extend and complicate the story, showing how Quito’s identity has been renegotiated across five centuries without losing its essential character, and why UNESCO’s protection extends to a whole urban landscape rather than a list of isolated monuments.

Life in the Living Centre

Quito’s historic centre is not a quiet monument; it is an inhabited, working, noisy and fragrant part of the city. Along Calle García Moreno, the old processional route, tourists mingle with schoolchildren and clerks; in La Ronda, once the haunt of poets and bohemians, restaurants and workshops now occupy the colonial buildings, and the street comes alive with music in the evening. Markets, church festivals and the annual celebrations of the city’s founding fill the squares with colour and noise.

Above the whole scene stands the hill of El Panecillo, the “little bread loaf,” crowned by a monumental aluminium statue of the Virgin of Quito, installed in 1976 and modelled on the winged Virgin carved by Bernardo de Legarda in the eighteenth century. From the top, the entire historic quarter unfurls below, a sea of red-tile roofs and bell towers pinned between two Andean ranges. Visitors who take the cable car up the flank of Pichincha can look down on the city from nearly 4,000 metres, understanding at last how this ribbon of old streets sits inside the great landscape that shaped it.

The challenge facing Quito today is the challenge faced by every living historic city: how to keep it alive without turning it into a theme park. Pedestrianisation, restoration of public space and efforts to bring residents back into the centre have all been part of that struggle, and while the balance is never perfect, the historic centre remains one of the most convincingly inhabited heritage districts in the world.

Reflection — A Capital That Refuses to Become a Relic

To stand in the Plaza de San Francisco on a bright Sunday morning, as the bells peal and families spill out of mass into the sun, is to understand something that no list of criteria can quite convey. Heritage, in Quito, is not the opposite of daily life but its frame. The city did not merely survive the centuries; it absorbed them, lifting each layer of its history — Quitu, Inca, Spanish, republican, modern — into a continuous urban conversation that still has not ended.

The great lesson of Quito, it seems to me, is that preservation and vitality are not rivals. A city that is used, loved and argued over will always be harder to freeze than one that is fenced off, but it will also endure in a way that no museum can. In the thin, bright air of the Andes, beneath the gilded vaults of La Compañía and the whitewashed walls of San Francisco, one senses a place whose heritage is not simply guarded but inhabited. That, finally, is what UNESCO recognised in 1978, and it is why Quito remains one of the most rewarding historic cities anywhere in the world.

✦ Conclusion

The City of Quito stands as one of the great achievements of colonial urbanism in the Americas, and as proof that a historic centre can remain both genuine and alive. From the Inca roads that first tied the northern Andes to Cuzco, through the grid laid down by Benalcázar in 1534 and the dazzling art of the Quito School, to the republican monuments that mark the birth of Ecuador, its streets and plazas contain the whole story of a region in transformation.

To visit Quito is to climb into thin air and into deep time at once. It is to watch light fall on gold leaf, to hear the bells of a dozen churches answer one another across the rooftops, and to realise that the past here is not a distant country but a neighbour. That is why the Historic Centre of Quito deserved to be among the first sites ever inscribed on the World Heritage List, and why it continues to reward every traveller who takes the time to walk its steep and beautiful streets.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

Why is Quito’s historic centre a UNESCO World Heritage Site?

UNESCO inscribed the Historic Centre of Quito in 1978 under criteria (ii) and (iv). It recognised the city as the best-preserved and least altered historic centre in Latin America, and as an outstanding example of Baroque colonial architecture and urbanism whose churches, convents and the Quito School of art influenced the wider region.

How high is Quito, and will altitude affect visitors?

Quito lies at about 2,850 metres above sea level, one of the highest capital cities in the world. Many visitors experience mild altitude symptoms such as headache, breathlessness or poor sleep during the first day or two. It is sensible to take things slowly, drink plenty of water, avoid heavy meals and alcohol on arrival, and consult a doctor beforehand if you have a heart or lung condition.

What are the most important monuments to see in the historic centre?

The essential sights are the Plaza de la Independencia with the Metropolitan Cathedral and Carondelet Palace, the Church of La Compañía de Jesús, the Church and Convent of San Francisco, the Basílica del Voto Nacional, the street of La Ronda and the Panecillo viewpoint. Santo Domingo, San Agustín and La Merced reward those with more time.

What is the Quito School of art?

The Escuela Quiteña was a flourishing tradition of painting, sculpture and polychrome carving that developed in colonial Quito between the sixteenth and eighteenth centuries. Trained in convent workshops by European friars, indigenous and mestizo artists blended European realism with local motifs, producing devotional works of great skill that were exported across the Americas.

When is the best time to visit Quito?

The drier months, roughly June to September, usually offer the clearest skies and the best views of the surrounding volcanoes. Quito’s temperature is mild year-round because of its altitude, but mornings can be crisp and afternoons rainy outside the dry season, so a light jacket and umbrella are useful at any time.

🧳 Visitor's Guide

Getting There — Fly into Mariscal Sucre International Airport at Tababela, roughly an hour’s drive east of the historic centre; taxis, shuttles and buses run to the city

Best Time to Visit — June to September for the driest weather and clearest mountain views

Altitude — About 2,850 metres; acclimatise gently and stay hydrated

Getting Around — The historic centre is compact and best explored on foot; taxis are inexpensive and the city’s public transport includes buses and a newer metro line

Currency — United States dollar (Ecuador has used the dollar since 2000)

Church Etiquette — Dress modestly, keep voices low and check photography rules; many churches charge a small entry fee or limit photography inside

Viewpoints — El Panecillo hill for the Virgin of Quito, and the TelefériQo cable car on the slopes of Pichincha for high-altitude views

Safety — Keep valuables secure in crowded areas, avoid wandering in unfamiliar quiet streets after dark and check current local advice before travelling
