The Island That Changed Everything

In September 1835, a twenty-six-year-old English naturalist named Charles Darwin arrived in the Galápagos Islands aboard HMS Beagle and spent five weeks observing the wildlife of the archipelago. What he observed — and more importantly, what he made of what he observed in the years of reflection that followed — changed the way human beings understand life on Earth more fundamentally than any other single act of scientific observation in the history of natural science.

Darwin noticed that the mockingbirds of the Galápagos Islands were different on different islands — that each island's population had its own distinctive characteristics. He noticed that the giant tortoises varied between islands in ways that the locals could use to identify which island any given tortoise had come from. He collected finches — thirteen species of them, all apparently descended from a single ancestral species that had arrived on the islands from the South American mainland, each species adapted to a different food source with a different beak shape and a different behaviour. He did not, during his visit, fully grasp the significance of what he was seeing. That significance emerged slowly, over the following years, as he worked through his observations and arrived at the theory that would be published in 1859 as On the Origin of Species by Means of Natural Selection.

The theory of natural selection — that the diversity of living organisms is produced by the differential survival and reproduction of individuals whose inherited characteristics make them better or less well adapted to their environments — is the foundational principle of modern biology. Everything that has been learned about life on Earth in the century and a half since Darwin published his theory has confirmed, extended, and deepened it. The Galápagos Islands, where the process of evolution by natural selection is visible in the living organisms of the archipelago with a clarity available nowhere else on Earth, are UNESCO World Heritage Site number 1 — the very first site inscribed on the World Heritage List, in 1978, in recognition of their outstanding universal significance.

The Geology — Islands Born from the Deep

The Galápagos Islands are volcanic in origin — products of a geological hotspot beneath the Pacific Ocean floor, similar in mechanism to the Hawaiian Islands but in a different tectonic setting. The hotspot generates a plume of heat that melts the overlying oceanic crust, producing magma that erupts on the ocean floor and gradually builds up into volcanic islands. As the Nazca Plate moves eastward over the hotspot, older islands are carried away from the magma source and begin to erode and subside, while new volcanic activity creates new islands to the west. The result is a chain of islands of different ages — the older, more eroded islands to the east, the younger, more volcanically active islands to the west — with Fernandina and Isabela in the west being the most recently formed and the most volcanically active.

The volcanic landscapes of the Galápagos are among the most dramatic on Earth. Fernandina Island — the youngest and most pristine of the major islands, closed to tourists to protect its extraordinary ecological integrity — has never been colonised by introduced species and represents the closest approximation available on Earth to a pre-human volcanic island ecosystem. Its caldera — one of the deepest in the world — periodically produces dramatic eruptions that reshape the island's surface and push the marine iguanas and flightless cormorants and sea lions that inhabit its shores temporarily back from the lava flows before they return to recolonise the cooling rock.

The black lava landscapes of the Galápagos — pahoehoe lava flows of extraordinary texture and variety, lava tubes, tuff cones, calderas, and the strange formations known as hornitos — are as significant a component of the archipelago's outstanding universal value as the wildlife they support. The islands are a geological textbook in the field — a demonstration, legible to any attentive visitor, of the processes by which oceanic volcanic islands are formed, age, and erode.

The Wildlife — Evolution on Display

The Galápagos Islands are the most extraordinary concentration of endemic wildlife — species found nowhere else on Earth — of any comparable area on the planet. Approximately 97 percent of the terrestrial reptiles, 80 percent of the land birds, 30 percent of the plants, and 20 percent of the marine species found in the Galápagos are found nowhere else. The reason for this extraordinary endemism is the isolation of the islands — approximately 1,000 kilometres from the nearest continental landmass — combined with the relative youth of the archipelago (the oldest islands are approximately 4 million years old, the youngest still being formed) and the diversity of habitats across the different islands and elevation zones.

The giant Galápagos tortoise — Chelonoidis niger — is the most iconic animal of the archipelago and one of the most extraordinary on Earth. The largest living tortoise species, capable of reaching weights of more than 400 kilograms and living for more than 150 years, the giant tortoise was once found in enormous numbers across all the major islands. Sailors and whalers in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries loaded the tortoises onto ships in their thousands as a living food supply — the animals could survive for months without food or water in the holds of ships — reducing the total population from an estimated 250,000 to fewer than 15,000 by the early twentieth century.

The Darwin's finches — thirteen species of small passerine birds descended from a single ancestor that colonised the islands from South America — remain the most celebrated example of adaptive radiation in the natural world: the visible process by which a single ancestral species, confronted with an archipelago of different islands with different food resources, diversifies over thousands of generations into multiple species each adapted to a different ecological niche. The variation in beak shape and size between the species — from the heavy, seed-cracking bill of the large ground finch to the fine, insect-picking bill of the warbler finch — is a textbook demonstration of natural selection, still being studied and still yielding new discoveries.

The Tameness — A World Without Fear

One of the most immediately striking characteristics of Galápagos wildlife is its extraordinary tameness — its complete indifference to human presence. Marine iguanas bask within centimetres of visitor feet without moving. Sea lions sleep on the footpath and must be stepped around. Blue-footed boobies perform their courtship displays directly beside human observers without interruption. Galápagos hawks land on the heads of visitors. The giant tortoises browse past within arm's reach.

This tameness is not the tameness of habituation to human beings — it predates any significant human contact. It is the tameness of animals that evolved in an environment without terrestrial predators — an environment in which there was no evolutionary pressure to flee from large approaching objects, because large approaching objects on the Galápagos were never dangerous. The animals of the Galápagos have no instinctive fear of human beings because their evolutionary history gave them no reason to develop one.

The consequence for the visitor is an experience of wildlife encounter utterly unlike anything available at any other natural site in the world. To sit on a rock in the Galápagos and have a sea lion puppy climb onto your lap, or to watch a pair of albatrosses perform their extraordinary bill-clapping courtship ritual from a distance of two metres, or to swim alongside a Galápagos penguin in the equatorial Pacific, is to encounter wild animals not as observers across a barrier of appropriate distance but as participants in their world — guests in an environment in which the categories of wild and tame, dangerous and safe, have a completely different meaning from everywhere else on Earth.

Conservation — The Ongoing Battle

The Galápagos Islands face conservation challenges of formidable complexity — the consequence of their biological significance, their physical isolation, and their growing popularity as a tourist destination. The introduction of invasive species — rats, cats, dogs, goats, pigs, and exotic plants brought to the islands by human settlement — has been the most destructive force in Galápagos ecology, causing the extinction or severe decline of multiple endemic species and requiring continuous and expensive eradication programmes to address.

The human population of the Galápagos — concentrated on the four inhabited islands of Santa Cruz, San Cristóbal, Isabela, and Floreana — has grown rapidly in recent decades, from a few thousand in the 1970s to more than 30,000 today, driven by the economic opportunities created by tourism. This population growth has increased the pressure on the islands' fragile ecosystems from fishing, agriculture, and the demand for services and infrastructure.

Tourism itself — the economic engine that funds much of the conservation work — poses its own challenges. The Galápagos National Park, which covers approximately 97 percent of the terrestrial area of the archipelago, manages visitor access through a system of designated visitor sites, trail limits, and mandatory guide accompaniment, designed to distribute the impact of approximately 270,000 visitors per year across the archipelago in a way that minimises ecological damage.

Reflection — The Islands That Explained the World

The Galápagos Islands are, among other things, the place where the modern scientific understanding of life on Earth was born. Darwin's five weeks on the archipelago in 1835 produced observations that, after twenty-four years of reflection and evidence-gathering, became the theory of natural selection — the most important idea in the history of biology and one of the most important in the history of human thought. The Galápagos did not give Darwin the theory; he brought to the islands a mind already prepared by years of observation and reading. But the islands gave him the evidence — the mockingbirds, the tortoises, the finches — that made the theory visible, that allowed him to see, in the diversity of a single archipelago, the process by which all the diversity of life on Earth had been produced.

To visit the Galápagos today — to stand on the black lava of Fernandina and watch the marine iguanas feeding in the surf, or to sit beside a nesting pair of waved albatrosses on Española and watch them perform their extraordinary courtship ritual, or to snorkel with Galápagos penguins in the cold Humboldt Current water — is to encounter the process that Darwin saw, still operating, still producing the extraordinary diversity of forms that the isolation and the volcanic youth of the islands make possible. It is one of the most extraordinary experiences available to any person on Earth.

✦ Conclusion

The Galápagos Islands are UNESCO World Heritage Site number 1 — the first site ever inscribed on the World Heritage List, in 1978 — and the designation is entirely appropriate. No other site on the List more fully justifies the concept of outstanding universal value: the biological significance of the Galápagos, the role of the archipelago in the development of the theory of natural selection, the extraordinary endemism of its wildlife, the dramatic geological youth of its volcanic landscapes, and the incomparable quality of the wildlife encounters available to its visitors together constitute a natural heritage of such depth and such variety that the four-criteria UNESCO inscription under (vii), (viii), (ix), and (x) — the maximum possible for a natural site — seems the minimum appropriate acknowledgement.

To visit the Galápagos is to encounter the natural world at its most generous, its most extraordinary, and its most instructive. It is to understand, in the most direct possible way, the process that produced all the diversity of life on Earth — and to encounter the products of that process with an intimacy and a directness available nowhere else. Darwin understood what he was seeing on these islands. Most visitors understand it too, by the time they leave. The islands explain themselves, in the language of their wildlife and their geology, with a clarity that five thousand words can only approximate.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: Why are the Galápagos Islands UNESCO World Heritage Site number 1? The Galápagos Islands were the very first site inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List, in 1978, in recognition of their outstanding universal natural significance. They were inscribed under all four natural criteria — for outstanding natural beauty, outstanding geological significance, outstanding ecological importance, and outstanding value for biological diversity and conservation.

Q2: What did Darwin observe in the Galápagos? Darwin observed, during his five-week visit in 1835, that the mockingbirds, tortoises, and finches of the archipelago varied between islands in ways that could not be explained by any theory other than that species change over time in response to their environments — the observation that led, after twenty-four years of further research, to the publication of On the Origin of Species by Means of Natural Selection in 1859.

Q3: Why are Galápagos animals so tame? Galápagos animals are extraordinarily tame because they evolved in an environment without terrestrial predators — an environment in which there was no evolutionary pressure to develop fear of large approaching objects. The animals have no instinctive fear of human beings because their evolutionary history gave them no reason to develop one.

Q4: How do visitors get to the Galápagos? Visitors fly to the Galápagos from Quito or Guayaquil on the Ecuadorian mainland — flights land on either Baltra Island (adjacent to Santa Cruz) or San Cristóbal Island. Most visitors join a multi-day cruise on a licensed Galápagos vessel, which provides the most comprehensive and most flexible way to visit multiple islands.

Q5: How many species are endemic to the Galápagos? Approximately 97 percent of terrestrial reptiles, 80 percent of land birds, 30 percent of plants, and 20 percent of marine species in the Galápagos are endemic — found nowhere else on Earth. The most famous endemic species include the giant Galápagos tortoise (multiple subspecies), the marine iguana, the flightless cormorant, the Galápagos penguin, the waved albatross, and the thirteen species of Darwin's finches.

🧳 Visitor's Guide

Getting There — Fly from Quito (approximately 3 hours) or Guayaquil (approximately 1.5 hours) to Baltra Island (adjacent to Santa Cruz) or San Cristóbal Island; flights operated by LATAM and Avianca

Entry Requirements — All visitors must pay a Galápagos National Park entrance fee on arrival; transit control card required; purchased at mainland airports before departure

Best Way to Visit — Multi-day cruise on a licensed Galápagos vessel (ranging from budget to luxury); island-hopping with day tours from a land base on Santa Cruz or San Cristóbal is a lower-cost alternative

Best Season — The Galápagos is outstanding year-round; December to May (warm season) — warmer water, good snorkelling, green vegetation; June to November (cool/dry season) — cooler water, Humboldt Current bringing nutrient-rich upwelling, excellent wildlife activity including whale watching

Must See — Giant tortoises (Santa Cruz highlands or Isabela); marine iguanas (Fernandina or Española); blue-footed boobies (Española or North Seymour); Galápagos penguins (Bartolomé or Isabela); waved albatross (Española — April to December only); snorkelling with sea lions and marine iguanas

Wildlife Etiquette — Maintain a minimum 2-metre distance from all wildlife; do not touch, feed, or disturb any animal; stay on designated trails; follow guide instructions at all times

Currency — US Dollar (USD — official currency of Ecuador)

Language — Spanish — English widely spoken in the tourism industry
