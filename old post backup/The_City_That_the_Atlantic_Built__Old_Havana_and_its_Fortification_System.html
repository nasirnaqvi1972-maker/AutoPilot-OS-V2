The City That the Atlantic Built

Havana was founded by the Spanish in 1519 — or rather, refounded, since an earlier settlement on the southern coast of Cuba had proved inhospitable — on the shores of one of the finest natural harbours in the Caribbean: a deep, sheltered bay whose narrow entrance could be defended by cannon fire from both sides, whose deep waters could accommodate the largest ships of the age, and whose position on the northwestern coast of Cuba placed it at the convergence of the major ocean currents and trade winds that connected the Caribbean with the Atlantic world. The Spanish understood immediately what they had found. Havana became, within decades of its foundation, the gathering point for the flota — the great convoy system by which the wealth of the Americas was transported to Spain — and the most strategically important city in the Spanish imperial system after Seville itself.

The consequences of this strategic importance for the built environment of Havana were enormous. The wealth flowing through the city funded construction on a scale and a quality unmatched anywhere else in the Spanish Caribbean. The necessity of defending the city against the attacks of pirates, privateers, and rival imperial powers — the English, the Dutch, the French — funded the construction of one of the most elaborate systems of military fortification in the history of the Americas. And the cultural confidence that came with wealth and strategic importance produced a civic architecture of extraordinary ambition and beauty: the plazas, the palaces, the churches, and the domestic buildings of Old Havana that constitute, in their ensemble, the finest colonial urban landscape in the Caribbean and one of the finest in the entire Americas.

UNESCO inscribed Old Havana and its fortification system on the World Heritage List in 1982 — among the earliest inscriptions in Latin America and the Caribbean — recognising it under criteria (iv) and (v) as an outstanding example of a type of architectural ensemble that illustrates a significant stage in human history, and as an outstanding example of traditional human settlement that represents a culture of exceptional significance. The Fortifications — The Shield of the Americas

The fortification system of Havana is one of the most impressive military engineering achievements of the colonial Americas, and its inclusion in the UNESCO inscription alongside the historic city centre reflects the inseparability of the two: Old Havana cannot be understood without its defences, and its defences cannot be understood without the city they were built to protect.

The system was built and expanded over a period of approximately three centuries, from the mid-sixteenth to the late eighteenth century, in response to a series of attacks that made clear the vulnerability of the city's enormous strategic and commercial value to the ambitions of rival powers. The sacking of Havana by the French pirate Jacques de Sores in 1555 — an event that left the city burned and its inhabitants traumatised — initiated the first serious fortification campaign. The construction of the Castillo de la Real Fuerza, begun in 1558, was the first result: a compact bastion fortress on the western side of the harbour entrance, its moat, walls, and towers representative of the most advanced military architecture of the mid-sixteenth century.

The most dramatic episode in Havana's military history — and the one that produced the most ambitious phase of fortification construction — was the British capture of the city in 1762. A British force of approximately 50 ships and 14,000 men besieged Havana for 44 days before the Morro Castle — the great fortress guarding the eastern side of the harbour entrance — fell to a British assault. The British occupied Havana for eleven months before returning it to Spain in exchange for Florida at the Treaty of Paris. The Spanish response to this humiliation was immediate and comprehensive: the construction of the Fortaleza de San Carlos de la Cabaña — the largest fortress built by Spain in the Americas, its walls extending for approximately 700 metres along the ridge east of the harbour, housing a garrison of a thousand men and mounting artillery capable of covering every approach to the city — at a cost equivalent to the entire annual revenue of the Spanish crown in Cuba.

The Four Plazas — The Heart of Old Havana

The urban structure of Old Havana is organised around four major plazas — public squares that served as the social, commercial, religious, and administrative centres of colonial life, each with its own character and its own architectural ensemble of exceptional quality.

The Plaza de Armas is the oldest and most formal of the four — the political heart of colonial Havana, its centre occupied by a marble statue of Carlos Manuel de Céspedes, the father of Cuban independence, surrounded by the arcaded facades of the former Governor's Palace (now the Museum of the City), the Palacio del Segundo Cabo, and the Castillo de la Real Fuerza. On its eastern side, the small church of El Templete marks the spot where the first Mass was celebrated on the site of the city in 1519 — a simple neoclassical structure of great historical weight.

The Plaza de la Catedral is the most architecturally distinguished of the four — a baroque composition of exceptional harmony in which the Cathedral of San Cristóbal de la Habana, with its asymmetrical towers and its elaborately carved facade, is flanked by the Palacio de los Marqueses de Aguas Claras and the Palacio del Conde Lombillo, their ochre and cream facades creating a colour palette of warm, tropical elegance entirely characteristic of Havanan colonial architecture at its finest. The square was described by the Cuban writer Alejo Carpentier — whose account of Havana's baroque character remains the most brilliant literary evocation of the city ever written — as music turned to stone.

The Plaza Vieja — the Old Square — is the most democratic of the four, historically a marketplace and a place of popular assembly rather than a ceremonial space. Its buildings reflect the full range of Havanan domestic and commercial architecture across four centuries, from the sixteenth-century Casa de los Condes de Jaruco to the twentieth-century buildings that close its southern side. The Plaza de San Francisco, adjacent to the former commercial harbour, was the gathering place of the merchant class — its Basilica Menor de San Francisco de Asís, with its tall bell tower, one of the most elegant religious buildings in Old Havana.

The Architecture — Baroque in the Tropics

The architecture of Old Havana is one of the great original achievements of Spanish colonial culture — not a slavish imitation of metropolitan Spanish models but a creative synthesis of those models with the specific conditions of a tropical Caribbean city: the climate, the materials, the light, and the cultural fusion of Spanish, African, and indigenous influences that characterised Cuban society from its earliest colonial decades.

The most distinctive element of Havanan domestic architecture is the portal — the great entrance doorway of the colonial palace or house, typically surmounted by a curved or broken pediment and flanked by pilasters, opening into a central courtyard (patio) around which the rooms of the house are arranged. The patio is the heart of the Havanan house: a private garden open to the sky, its fountain at the centre, its arcaded galleries providing shade and ventilation against the Caribbean heat. The combination of European spatial organisation with tropical adaptation — the high ceilings, the louvred wooden screens (mamparas) that allow air circulation while maintaining privacy, the stained glass panels (mediopuntos) above doorways that cast coloured light into the interior — produces an architectural character of great warmth and originality that is immediately recognisable as Havanan and nowhere else.

The religious architecture of Old Havana is predominantly baroque — the Cathedral, the Basilica of San Francisco, the Iglesia del Santo Angel Custodio — but a baroque inflected by the tropical light and the local stone (a coral limestone of warm golden colour) that gives it a character quite different from the baroque of Spain or Mexico. The Havanan baroque is less elaborate in its decoration, warmer in its colour, and somehow more relaxed in its formal language than its continental equivalents — as if the heat of the Caribbean had softened the rigid formalism of the European models into something more generous and more human.

The Restoration — Eusebio Leal and the Recovery of Old Havana

The story of Old Havana's UNESCO inscription and its subsequent restoration is inseparable from the figure of Eusebio Leal Spengler — the City Historian of Havana from 1967 until his death in 2020, who dedicated more than fifty years of his life to the documentation, protection, and restoration of the historic city. Leal was not merely an administrator. He was an impassioned advocate, a skilled fundraiser, and a genuine visionary who understood, at a time when much of the world had not yet grasped the concept of urban heritage conservation, that Old Havana was one of the great architectural treasures of the Americas and that its progressive deterioration — accelerated by the economic difficulties of the post-revolutionary period — represented a loss of irreplaceable significance.

The model Leal developed for the restoration of Old Havana — using the revenues generated by the historic city's tourism potential to fund the restoration of its buildings, reinvesting the profits of heritage tourism in the social and physical infrastructure of the historic neighbourhood — has been adopted as a model for heritage conservation in other cities around the world. The results are visible throughout the historic centre: the plazas restored to their colonial splendour, the palace facades stripped of layers of paint and plaster to reveal the warm coral limestone beneath, the hotels and restaurants and museums that now occupy the restored buildings generating the revenues that allow the restoration to continue.

Reflection — A City That Time Froze and History Preserved

Old Havana is often described as a city that time forgot — and the description, while not entirely accurate, captures something true. The economic isolation of Cuba following the revolution of 1959 and the subsequent American embargo created conditions in which the commercial development that has transformed the historic centres of so many Caribbean and Latin American cities — replacing colonial buildings with modern construction, disrupting the traditional urban fabric, standardising the architecture of globalisation — was largely prevented. Old Havana deteriorated, but it was not demolished and replaced. The bones of the colonial city survived, waiting for the restoration that Eusebio Leal and his team would eventually provide.

The result is a historic city of extraordinary authenticity — not a theme park reconstruction but a living urban fabric in which the colonial architecture is genuinely old, in which the streets and plazas and courtyards retain the spatial logic of their original design, and in which the daily life of the city — the street musicians, the children playing in the plazas, the old men in doorways, the extraordinary colourful vehicles of the Cuban streets — continues to animate a physical setting of exceptional historical depth and architectural beauty.

Conclusion

Old Havana is one of the great cities of the world — not great in the sense of size or political power, but great in the sense that matters most to the experience of being human in an urban environment: great in beauty, great in historical depth, great in the quality of the public spaces and the private buildings that constitute its built fabric, and great in the intensity of the life that has been lived in its streets and plazas for five hundred years.

The UNESCO inscription of 1982 was a recognition of all of this. The restoration that followed — imperfect, ongoing, funded by the ingenuity of Eusebio Leal and the revenues of tourism and sustained by the commitment of the Cuban state to preserving its most extraordinary architectural inheritance — is one of the great achievements of heritage conservation in the twentieth and twenty-first centuries. Old Havana is not perfectly preserved and it is not perfectly restored. It is something better than either: it is alive, still inhabited, still animated by the daily life of a city that has been through every vicissitude of history and emerged, battered and beautiful, on the other side.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is Old Havana a UNESCO World Heritage Site? Old Havana was inscribed in 1982 for the outstanding quality of its Spanish colonial urban ensemble — its four major plazas, its baroque and neoclassical architecture, its fortification system, and the exceptional completeness of its colonial urban fabric, which represents one of the finest examples of Spanish colonial city planning in the Americas.

What is the fortification system of Havana? The fortification system consists of a series of military forts and city walls built by the Spanish between the mid-sixteenth and late eighteenth centuries to protect Havana's strategically vital harbour. The principal fortifications are the Castillo de la Real Fuerza (1558), El Morro (1589-1630), and La Cabaña (1763-1774), together forming one of the most elaborate colonial military engineering systems in the Americas.

What are the four main plazas of Old Havana? The four principal plazas of Old Havana are the Plaza de Armas (the political and administrative centre), the Plaza de la Catedral (the most architecturally distinguished, dominated by the baroque cathedral), the Plaza Vieja (the commercial and popular square), and the Plaza de San Francisco (the merchants' square adjacent to the harbour).

Who was responsible for restoring Old Havana? The restoration of Old Havana was led by Eusebio Leal Spengler, City Historian of Havana from 1967 until his death in 2020. Leal developed an innovative self-financing model for restoration in which tourism revenues from the historic centre were reinvested in its conservation — a model now widely studied internationally.

What is the best way to explore Old Havana? Old Havana is best explored on foot — the historic centre is compact enough to walk comprehensively in two full days. Begin at the Plaza de Armas, work through the four plazas, explore the side streets between them, and end with the fortifications on the eastern side of the harbour (accessible by the harbour tunnel or by boat).
