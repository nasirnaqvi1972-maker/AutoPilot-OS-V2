A City Founded by a Queen

Few cities have a founding legend as haunting as Carthage. According to tradition, the city was established in 814 BC by Elissa, whom the Romans called Dido, a princess of the Phoenician city of Tyre who fled her murderous brother and sailed westward with a band of followers. The story goes that she bargained with the local people for as much land as an ox hide could cover, then cut the hide into thin strips and enclosed the hill that would become Byrsa. Whether myth or memory, the story captures the ingenuity that made the city.

Carthage means in Phoenician something like New City, Qart-Hadasht, and it rose with extraordinary speed. Its location, on a promontory jutting into the Gulf of Tunis at the narrows of the Mediterranean, was perfect for commerce: from here ships could reach Sicily, Sardinia, Spain and Egypt. Within a few centuries the city had grown from a colonial outpost into the capital of a maritime empire, with a navy and a merchant fleet that dominated the western Mediterranean.

UNESCO inscribed the site in 1979 under criteria (ii), (iii) and (vi), recognising its testimony to the Phoenician and Punic civilisation, its influence on the arts and architecture of the Mediterranean, and its association with events and ideas of universal significance. What remains today is a landscape of fragments, scattered across a modern, leafy suburb of Tunis, but each fragment belongs to a story of epic scale.

Harbours, Hills and the Punic City

The Punic city was organised around the sea. Archaeologists have identified two great harbours: a rectangular commercial port and, connected to it, a circular naval harbour, the cothon, with an island at its centre where the admiral’s headquarters stood and with docks for more than two hundred warships. Their outlines remain visible today as quiet lagoons, among the most evocative features of the site.

Above the harbours rose the hill of Byrsa, the citadel and religious centre of the city, with temples, houses and a defensive wall. Excavations on its slopes have revealed a densely built Punic neighbourhood of tall houses on a regular street plan, a sign of an urban civilisation of considerable sophistication, and later Roman builders partly cut away the hilltop to level a platform for their forum.

The Tophet of Salammbô

In the south lies the Tophet, a sacred precinct where thousands of urns containing the cremated remains of infants and animals were buried beneath carved stone markers. Ancient writers accused the Carthaginians of child sacrifice, and some modern scholars accept this; others argue that the Tophet was a cemetery for children who died young. The debate remains unresolved, and the Tophet stands as a sobering reminder of how much the Punic world is known mainly through hostile eyes.

Hannibal and the Punic Wars

The rise of Carthage brought it into inevitable conflict with Rome. The First Punic War, fought from 264 to 241 BC largely over Sicily, ended in a Roman victory and forced the Carthaginians to pay a heavy indemnity. Humiliated, Carthage turned to its Spanish possessions, where the Barcid family rebuilt its fortunes, and from them came Hannibal, one of the greatest commanders in history.

In 218 BC, Hannibal led an army with war elephants across the Pyrenees and the Alps into Italy, inflicting devastating defeats on the Romans, most famously at Cannae in 216 BC. For years he roamed Italy undefeated, yet Rome refused to yield. The Roman general Scipio, later called Africanus, carried the war to Africa and defeated Hannibal at Zama in 202 BC, ending the Second Punic War and Carthage’s status as a great power.

The final act came in the Third Punic War. Roman politicians, famously including Cato the Elder, insisted that Carthage must be destroyed, and in 149 BC a Roman army besieged the city. After a brutal siege and street fighting, it fell in 146 BC under Scipio Aemilianus and was razed. The popular tale that the Romans sowed the ground with salt is a later embellishment, but the destruction was real and thorough.

Roman Carthage — A Second Life

Rome did not leave the site empty. In 44 BC Julius Caesar planned a colony here, and Augustus refounded it, and within a century Carthage had become the capital of the Roman province of Africa and one of the largest and wealthiest cities in the western empire. Its grain fed Rome; its streets were laid out on a grid, with a forum on Byrsa hill, a circus, an amphitheatre, a theatre and an aqueduct that brought water from springs far in the hills.

The greatest surviving Roman monument is the Antonine Baths, built in the middle of the second century CE and among the largest bath complexes in the empire. The foundations and cellars, with their massive vaults, still convey the scale of what stood above, and the lone reconstructed column beside the sea has become an emblem of the site. The Roman theatre, partly rebuilt, is used for summer concerts, and fine mosaics from the villas are displayed in the Bardo and Carthage museums.

Roman Carthage was also a centre of intellectual and religious life. It produced writers such as Apuleius and Tertullian, and was the home of the African church, with martyrs including Perpetua and Felicity and the bishop Cyprian. Augustine of Hippo studied rhetoric here, and later spent years teaching in the city before his conversion, giving Carthage a major place in the history of Christianity.

Vandals, Byzantines, Arabs and a Crusader King

In 439 CE the Vandals under King Gaiseric seized Carthage and made it the capital of their kingdom, which controlled the western Mediterranean for nearly a century. In 533 the Byzantine general Belisarius reconquered the city for the emperor Justinian, and Carthage remained a Byzantine stronghold, with fortified walls and Christian basilicas, until the Arab conquest of 698 swept it into the new Islamic order and led to its decline in favour of Tunis.

The later history of the site was largely one of quarrying: for centuries, its stones and columns were carried off to build mosques, palaces and houses in Tunis and farther afield. Yet Carthage never fully lost its prestige. In 1270 the French king Louis IX, leading the Eighth Crusade, died of illness at Carthage, and the Cathedral of Saint Louis, now the Acropolium, was built on Byrsa hill in the nineteenth century to commemorate him.

The modern rediscovery of Carthage began in the nineteenth century, with the writer Chateaubriand and the novelist Flaubert, whose Salammbô evoked the vanished Punic city, followed by systematic excavations. Today, an international campaign launched under UNESCO in the 1970s helped promote the protection of the ruins amid the pressures of urban development.

Dido, Aeneas and the Literary Afterlife of Carthage

Carthage’s influence on the imagination has long exceeded its physical remains. In the Aeneid, the Roman poet Virgil made Dido the queen who welcomed the Trojan refugee Aeneas, fell tragically in love with him and killed herself when he left to fulfil his destiny in Italy. The tale gave Roman culture a founding myth in which the enmity with Carthage was explained as the curse of a spurned queen, and it has inspired poets, painters and composers ever since, from Christopher Marlowe to Henry Purcell.

Another strand of memory is the Roman slogan attributed to Cato, that Carthage must be destroyed, which has become shorthand for implacable hostility. Yet the city also appears in the historical writings of Polybius and Livy, who described Hannibal with grudging admiration, and in the novel Salammbô by Gustave Flaubert, which dramatised the mercenary revolt after the First Punic War with an imaginative exuberance that shaped nineteenth-century Orientalism.

These literary afterlives matter because for so long the written record of Punic culture was sparse. The Carthaginians wrote extensively, but almost none of their literature survives, and their story has been told by Greeks and Romans. Modern archaeology, together with the study of Punic inscriptions, has gradually begun to restore the balance, offering a more textured view of the people and their world.

Preserving Carthage in a Modern Suburb

The greatest challenge facing Carthage today is that it lies in the middle of a living suburb. Houses, roads and gardens cover much of the ancient city, and ruins sit between villas, schools and the presidential palace. Archaeological work must therefore proceed in the gaps left by development, and protection depends on land-use controls as much as on excavation.

In 1972 UNESCO launched an international campaign to safeguard Carthage, attracting teams from many countries, including Tunisia, France, Britain, Germany and others, who excavated and recorded different parts of the site. The campaign helped to put Carthage at the centre of international heritage concern and led to its inscription on the World Heritage List in 1979. Since then, Tunisian authorities, in cooperation with international partners, have continued to manage and study the site.

Further pressures include coastal erosion, rising sea levels and visitor wear on exposed monuments. The sea that made Carthage rich now threatens the Antonine Baths and the Punic ports, and managing that risk will be one of the great tasks of the coming decades. The enduring appeal of the site, however, and the pride of Tunisians in their Punic and Roman past, offer hope for its continued protection.

Reflection — The Weight of the Vanquished

History is written by victors, and Carthage is the great case of a civilisation that has been known chiefly through the words of its enemies. The Romans called the Carthaginians treacherous and cruel, and the picture stuck. Yet the stones tell a more complex story: of merchants and shipbuilders, of artisans and sailors, of a city whose harbours bustled and whose houses were tall and well made.

To walk among the ruins is therefore also to listen for what the winners tried to silence. The destruction of 146 BC was not the end but a pivot, and Carthage rose again as a Roman, Christian and Byzantine city. That resilience, and the way the site holds many pasts at once, is what makes Carthage so rewarding. It is a place where the vanquished and the victors share the same ground.

✦ Conclusion

Carthage is one of those rare places where history seems to pile up in layers: a Phoenician colony, a maritime empire, a Roman provincial capital, a Vandal royal seat, a Byzantine bastion and a Crusader’s deathbed. Its ruins are scattered and sometimes modest, but its significance is immense.

To visit the site is to engage with a civilisation that nearly toppled Rome and that has long been defined by its enemies. The Punic ports, the Tophet, the Antonine Baths and the museums together restore something of its voice, and remind us that the history of the Mediterranean belongs to all who lived around it.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

Why is Carthage a UNESCO World Heritage Site?

UNESCO inscribed Carthage in 1979 under criteria (ii), (iii) and (vi). It bears exceptional testimony to Phoenician and Punic civilisation, had a major influence on Mediterranean architecture and art, and is associated with the Punic Wars, Hannibal and the early history of Christianity.

Who founded Carthage?

According to tradition, Carthage was founded in 814 BC by Queen Elissa, or Dido, from the Phoenician city of Tyre. Archaeology suggests settlement in the later ninth or eighth century BC, so the legend and the evidence broadly agree.

Was Carthage really destroyed and salted?

Carthage was destroyed by Rome in 146 BC at the end of the Third Punic War, but there is no ancient evidence that the earth was sown with salt. That detail appears in much later accounts and is generally regarded as a myth.

What can I see at Carthage today?

The main sights include the Antonine Baths, the Punic ports, the Tophet of Salammbô, the Roman theatre, the Byrsa Hill with the Carthage National Museum and the Acropolium, the Roman villas and the amphitheatre. Many sites are scattered, so a car or taxi helps.

How do I get to Carthage from Tunis?

Carthage lies about 15 kilometres north-east of central Tunis. The TGM light rail line from Tunis Marine runs to several Carthage stations and is the easiest and cheapest option; taxis are also widely used.

Time Needed — A full day lets you see the main sites; sites are spread across the suburb

What to Bring — Sun protection, water and comfortable shoes for uneven ground
