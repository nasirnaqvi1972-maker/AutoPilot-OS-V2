The Island at the Edge of the World

There is a particular cruelty in the location of Robben Island. From the cell block of Section B of the Maximum Security Prison — where Nelson Mandela spent eighteen of his twenty-seven years of imprisonment — it is possible, on a clear day, to see Table Mountain across the eleven kilometres of Table Bay that separate the island from Cape Town. The mountain is visible. The city is visible. The ordinary life of South Africa — the life that the prisoners had been taken from, the life that the apartheid system was designed to deny them — is visible, across a stretch of cold Atlantic water that was never, in the years of the prison's operation, crossed by a prisoner who had not been authorised to leave.

Robben Island has been a place of exile, punishment, and imprisonment for longer than South Africa has been a country. Its first political prisoner was Autshumato — known to the Dutch settlers as Harry die Strandloper — a Khoikhoi leader exiled to the island by the Dutch East India Company in 1658 for resisting colonial encroachment on Khoikhoi land. He escaped, swimming or sailing back to the mainland — the only prisoner ever to escape from Robben Island successfully, as far as the historical record shows. Over the following three centuries, the island served as a place of confinement for political prisoners, lepers, the mentally ill, and others whom the successive colonial and apartheid authorities wished to remove from the mainland and from public view.

The most recent and most consequential chapter of the island's history as a place of imprisonment began in 1961, when the apartheid government of South Africa converted the island into a maximum security prison for political prisoners — specifically for the leaders of the African National Congress, the Pan Africanist Congress, and other organisations that were resisting apartheid through various forms of political and armed struggle. Nelson Mandela arrived on the island in 1964, following his conviction at the Rivonia Trial and his sentencing to life imprisonment. He would remain there until 1982, when he was transferred to Pollsmoor Prison on the mainland.

UNESCO inscribed Robben Island on the World Heritage List in 1999, under criteria (iii) and (vi), recognising it as an outstanding testimony to the inhumanity of apartheid and as a site directly associated with the struggle against one of the twentieth century's most systematic and most widely condemned systems of racial oppression. Apartheid — The System That Produced the Prison

To understand Robben Island is to understand apartheid — the system of legislated racial segregation and discrimination that governed South Africa from 1948 to 1994, and that Robben Island's most celebrated prisoners devoted their lives to opposing. Apartheid was not merely a system of racial separation, though it was certainly that. It was a comprehensive and elaborately legislated attempt to organise every aspect of South African society — where people lived, where they worked, where their children went to school, whom they could marry, which beaches they could use, which park benches they could sit on — on the basis of racial classification determined by the apartheid state.

The apartheid system classified South Africans into four racial groups — white, coloured, Indian, and Bantu (later called black) — and allocated rights, privileges, opportunities, and geographical space on the basis of this classification in a hierarchy of systematic inequality. White South Africans had full political rights and access to the best land, schools, hospitals, and economic opportunities. Black South Africans — the majority of the population — had no political rights, were confined to impoverished Bantustans (nominally independent homelands), and were required to carry passbooks identifying their racial classification and authorising their presence in designated areas.

The African National Congress — founded in 1912, making it one of the oldest liberation movements in Africa — had pursued a strategy of non-violent resistance to racial discrimination for decades before the apartheid government's increasingly violent suppression of opposition, culminating in the Sharpeville Massacre of 1960 in which police opened fire on unarmed protesters and killed 69 people, convinced its leadership that non-violent methods alone were insufficient. The formation of Umkhonto we Sizwe — the armed wing of the ANC, of which Nelson Mandela was a co-founder — and the subsequent Rivonia Trial of 1963–1964, at which Mandela and his co-accused were convicted of sabotage and sentenced to life imprisonment, brought the most prominent leaders of the anti-apartheid struggle to Robben Island.

Nelson Mandela — Eighteen Years on the Island

Nelson Rolihlahla Mandela arrived on Robben Island in June 1964, aged forty-five, convicted of sabotage and sentenced to life imprisonment. He was assigned to Section B of the Maximum Security Prison — a cell approximately 2.4 metres by 2.1 metres, containing a sleeping mat, two blankets, and a bucket for sanitation. He would sleep in that cell for the next eighteen years.

The conditions of imprisonment on Robben Island were designed to dehumanise and to break — to reduce the prisoners, through a combination of physical hardship, arbitrary humiliation, and the deliberate denial of basic human dignities, to a state of compliance and resignation. Black prisoners received smaller food rations than coloured and Indian prisoners. All prisoners were required to perform hard labour — breaking stones in the prison quarry in the early years of Mandela's imprisonment. Correspondence was severely restricted. Visitors were limited to one contact visit of thirty minutes every six months. Newspapers were forbidden. Education was restricted. The rules changed arbitrarily, at the discretion of prison officials who wielded their authority over the prisoners with varying degrees of humanity.

What the apartheid government did not anticipate was that the concentration on a small island of the most capable and most committed leaders of the anti-apartheid movement would create not a place of defeat but a place of education, debate, and political development. The prisoners of Robben Island — who called the prison Robben Island University — organised clandestine study groups, debated the strategies and the goals of the liberation movement, maintained their intellectual and political commitments through years of confinement, and emerged from prison not broken but strengthened. Mandela himself studied through correspondence, earned a law degree, and used the years of imprisonment to deepen his understanding of South African history, of political theory, and of the specific character of the society he intended to transform.

The Limestone Quarry — Hard Labour and Its Legacy

Among the many hardships of Robben Island, the work in the limestone quarry holds a particular place in the memory of its prisoners. For years, Mandela and his fellow prisoners were taken each morning to the quarry — a large open-cut excavation in the island's limestone — and required to break stones with hammers and pickaxes under the direct sun, the reflected glare of the white limestone so intense that it permanently damaged the eyesight of many prisoners, including Mandela himself, who has said that the glare was one of the most physically lasting consequences of his years on the island.

The quarry became, despite everything, a place of community and of clandestine intellectual life. While breaking stones, the prisoners talked — debated political theory, discussed tactics, shared information from letters and from the rare newspapers that reached them. They called it the University of the Quarry, adding this name to the broader Robben Island University designation. The quarry became a place where, under conditions of enforced physical labour and systematic humiliation, the intellectual and political life of the anti-apartheid movement continued.

Today, at the face of the quarry where Mandela and his colleagues worked, there is a pile of stones left by former prisoners who return to the island on visits. Each stone was placed by a former prisoner, or by a colleague or comrade of the prisoners, as a mark of respect and of memory. Visitors add stones to the pile as an act of tribute. It is one of the most powerful memorials on the island — not designed by any architect or committee, but created spontaneously by the people who had the most direct reason to remember what happened here.

After Apartheid — The Island as Museum and Memorial

Nelson Mandela was released from prison on February 11, 1990 — not from Robben Island, where he had spent eighteen of his twenty-seven years, but from Victor Verster Prison on the mainland, to which he had been transferred in 1988. His release, broadcast live on television to a global audience of hundreds of millions, was one of the defining moments of the late twentieth century — the moment when the apartheid system's project of silencing and removing its most effective opponents was visibly and definitively reversed.

Four years later, on April 27, 1994, Nelson Mandela voted for the first time in his life — in the first democratic election in South African history. He was elected President of South Africa. The country that had imprisoned him for twenty-seven years elected him as its leader. The apartheid system that had seemed, for decades, impregnable was dismantled by the organised political will of the people it had oppressed.

Robben Island was converted from a prison to a museum in 1997, three years after the end of apartheid. It was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1999. The most distinctive feature of the visitor experience — the feature that distinguishes Robben Island from every other heritage site in the world — is that tours of the Maximum Security Prison are led by former political prisoners. The men who were imprisoned on the island for their resistance to apartheid now guide visitors through the cells and corridors where they were confined, sharing their personal testimony of what happened here and what it meant. It is one of the most direct and most powerful acts of witness available anywhere in the heritage world.

Reflection — What the Island Teaches

Robben Island teaches something that no amount of reading about apartheid can fully convey: that the system was, despite its power and its apparent permanence, ultimately defeated — not by external military force, but by the sustained commitment of the people it was designed to crush. Nelson Mandela spent twenty-seven years in prison. He emerged, at the age of seventy-one, with his commitment to justice undimmed, his capacity for political leadership unbroken, his ability to negotiate a peaceful transition to democracy intact. The apartheid government imprisoned him because it feared what he represented. It turned out to have every reason to fear it.

The UNESCO inscription of 1999 recognised Robben Island not merely as a site of historical significance but as a symbol of the universal aspiration for human dignity and freedom — values that the apartheid system systematically denied and that the prisoners of Robben Island, in their resistance and their endurance, affirmed. The island is a reminder that the most elaborate systems of oppression can be dismantled by human will, that the most determined efforts to silence and remove the advocates of justice cannot ultimately succeed, and that the values for which Mandela and his colleagues suffered — equality, dignity, and the right of all people to participate in the governance of the society they live in — are not negotiable.

✦ Conclusion

Robben Island is one of the most important heritage sites in the world — not because of its architecture, which is unremarkable, not because of its natural setting, which is pleasant but not exceptional, but because of what happened here and what it means. The imprisonment of Nelson Mandela and hundreds of other leaders of the anti-apartheid movement on this island for years and decades was an act of state violence designed to remove the most effective advocates of justice from public life. The failure of that act — the survival and eventual triumph of the movement that the prison was designed to destroy — is one of the most important and most instructive stories of the twentieth century.

To visit Robben Island — to stand in Mandela's cell, to walk through the prison corridors, to listen to the testimony of a former prisoner who experienced these spaces not as a visitor but as an inmate — is to encounter that story in its most direct and most physical form. UNESCO was right to inscribe it in 1999. The world is right to visit it, to learn from it, and to carry its lesson forward: that the aspiration for justice is indestructible, and that the systems built to suppress it are, in the end, always temporary.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: How long was Nelson Mandela imprisoned on Robben Island? Nelson Mandela was imprisoned on Robben Island for eighteen years, from 1964 to 1982. He was then transferred to Pollsmoor Prison on the mainland, and subsequently to Victor Verster Prison, from which he was released on February 11, 1990. His total imprisonment lasted twenty-seven years.

Q2: How do visitors get to Robben Island? Visitors travel to Robben Island by ferry from the V&A Waterfront in Cape Town. The ferry crossing takes approximately thirty minutes each way. Tours of the island, including the Maximum Security Prison guided by former political prisoners, last approximately three and a half hours in total.

Q3: Who are the guides on Robben Island? Tours of the Maximum Security Prison on Robben Island are led by former political prisoners — men who were themselves imprisoned on the island during the apartheid era. Their personal testimony makes the tour an experience of direct human witness unlike anything at any other heritage site in the world.

Q4: Is Robben Island a UNESCO World Heritage Site? Yes. Robben Island was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1999, recognised under criteria (iii) and (vi) as an outstanding testimony to the democracy movements of the twentieth century and as a site directly associated with the struggle against apartheid and with the values of human dignity and freedom.

Q5: Is the ferry to Robben Island affected by weather? Yes. Table Bay can be rough, and ferry services to Robben Island are sometimes cancelled due to adverse weather conditions, particularly in the Cape Town winter (June to August). Visitors should book in advance and check the weather forecast; cancellations are common in winter months.
