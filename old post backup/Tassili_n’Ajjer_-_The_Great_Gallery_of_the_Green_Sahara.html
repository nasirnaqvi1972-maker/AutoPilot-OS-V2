Tassili n’Ajjer — The Great Gallery of the Green Sahara

A Vast Sandstone Plateau in the Algerian Sahara Where Wind-Carved Rock Forests Shelter More Than 15,000 Paintings and Engravings of Herders, Hunters, Giraffes, Cattle and Chariots That Record the Drying of the World’s Largest Desert, Together With Ancient Saharan Cypresses, a Mixed Cultural and Natural UNESCO World Heritage Site

A Garden Painted on Desert Stone

The Sahara is the largest hot desert on Earth, a vast expanse of sand, rock and heat. Yet scattered across its central plateau, on the walls of caves and the ceilings of rock shelters, there are paintings of giraffes, elephants, hippopotamuses and herds of long-horned cattle, of swimmers, archers and women dancing. The animals are the creatures of a green and well-watered land. The artists who painted them lived in a Sahara that no longer exists, and they left a record of it in stone.

This is Tassili n’Ajjer, a plateau in south-eastern Algeria whose name means plateau of the rivers in the language of the Tuareg. UNESCO describes it as a strange lunar landscape of great geological interest which holds one of the most important groupings of prehistoric cave art in the world. More than 15,000 drawings and engravings record the climatic changes, animal migrations and the evolution of human life on the edge of the Sahara, from about 6000 BC to the first centuries of the present era.

Tassili is one of the few places on Earth inscribed for both its culture and its nature. Its eroded sandstone forests are of remarkable scenic beauty, its geology tells a story older than the dinosaurs, and its rare Saharan cypresses are among the oldest trees on the planet. This post explores the plateau, the people who painted it, the world that vanished beneath them and the challenge of protecting such a fragile gallery in such a remote place.

The Plateau of Rivers

Tassili n’Ajjer is a vast plateau of sandstone in the central Sahara, covering some 72,000 square kilometres in south-eastern Algeria close to the borders with Libya, Niger and Mali. The word tassili means plateau in Tamahaq, the language of the northern Tuareg, and Ajjer is the name of the Tuareg people who live in the region, the Kel Ajjer. The plateau rises steeply above the surrounding plains, and its edges are cut by deep canyons and dry river valleys.

Nearby lies the oasis town of Djanet, the gateway to the plateau, from which expeditions set out on foot and by camel. The region is one of the most remote and arid on Earth, with extremely little rain, intense heat by day and cold nights, and its inhabitants have for centuries lived by herding, caravan trade and oasis gardening.

The Stone Forests

The plateau’s most striking natural feature is its landscape of rock forests, fields of towering pillars, arches, blocks and needles carved from the sandstone by wind, sand and occasional water. Standing among them feels like walking through the ruined streets of a giant city or across the surface of another planet, and UNESCO recognises the area under criterion (vii) for its remarkable scenic interest.

The geology is equally important. According to UNESCO, the geological conformation of Tassili includes Precambrian crystalline elements and sedimentary sandstone successions of great paleogeographical and paleoecological interest, so that the plateau records hundreds of millions of years of earth history. The sandstone is soft enough to be shaped by erosion and hard enough to form overhangs and shelters, a combination that gave ancient people a ready-made canvas.

When the Sahara Was Green

To understand Tassili’s art one must imagine a different climate. After the last Ice Age, between roughly 11,000 and 5,000 years ago, the monsoon rains moved north, and the Sahara became a landscape of grassland, lakes and rivers, the so-called African Humid Period. Elephants, giraffes and antelopes grazed where there is now sand, and people followed them, hunting, fishing and later herding cattle.

From about 5,000 years ago the rains retreated, and the Sahara slowly dried. The people of the plateau adapted, changing their way of life as water and pasture disappeared, and eventually many moved to the Nile valley, the Sahel and the oases. The rock art of Tassili records this long transformation in pictures, from the hunting of wild game to pastoral life and finally to the arrival of horses and camels in an arid land.

Discovery of the Paintings

The rock art of Tassili became known to the outside world in 1933, when it was reported by French military patrols in the Sahara. UNESCO notes that it is the rock art that has made Tassili world famous from that date. In the 1950s the French explorer Henri Lhote led expeditions that spent months copying the paintings, and the reproductions he brought back to Paris were exhibited in 1957, bringing the extraordinary images to wide public attention.

Later researchers have studied the site more carefully and corrected some of Lhote’s interpretations, but his work established the plateau as one of the great archaeological discoveries of the twentieth century. Over time more than 15,000 paintings and engravings have been identified, and new finds continue to be made in the remote canyons and shelters.

A Gallery in Many Styles

Scholars divide the art of Tassili into successive styles, distinguished by their subject matter and technique. Some of the earliest paintings are attributed to hunter-gatherer communities, and depict large wild animals such as elephants, rhinoceroses and giraffes, together with human figures. A well-known group of images shows human figures with round, featureless heads, sometimes of enormous size, in poses that suggest ritual or dance.

These early styles gave way to the art of pastoral communities, who painted vivid scenes of cattle herds, herders with their animals, camps, women with elaborate hairstyles and hunters with bows. Later periods introduced horses, chariots and, from about the first centuries AD, camels, together with inscriptions in ancient Libyco-Berber and Tifinagh script, the ancestor of the alphabet still used by the Tuareg. Each phase reflects a change in the climate and in the people who lived on the plateau.

Herders, Cattle and the Life of a Vanished People

The most appealing paintings are those of the pastoral period, which show daily life in extraordinary detail. Herds of cattle with sweeping horns move across the walls of shelters, led by herders; women and children stand outside round huts; groups gather for dances or ceremonies. The animals are rendered with grace and the figures with a feeling for movement that has often been compared to the best of later painting.

These scenes also offer evidence about how the people lived: what they ate, how they dressed, the ornaments they wore and the ways they kept their herds. UNESCO describes the rock art as the most eloquent expression of the relationship between humans and their environment, because it shows people adapting step by step to a changing world. For archaeologists, it is a key source for the history of early pastoralism in Africa.

Horses, Chariots and Camels

As the Sahara dried, the paintings changed. The later styles show horses, often drawn in a lively, stylised manner, sometimes with riders or pulling chariots, and the so-called horse period has generated much discussion about trade and contacts across the desert. Eventually camels appear, the animals that made long-distance caravan travel across the open Sahara possible, and with them the inscriptions of the historical Berber peoples.

The sequence from giraffes to camels is, in effect, a pictorial record of climate change. The engravings, which can be found on open rock faces as well as in shelters, extend the story, and the Tuareg of the region continue the tradition with their own carvings and graffiti. Tassili thus links prehistory to the present, because the people who live there today are heirs to the long human story that its art tells.

The Saharan Cypress: A Living Relic

Tassili is not only a gallery but also a refuge for life. In sheltered valleys and gorges grows a rare tree, the Saharan cypress, Cupressus dupreziana, which survives in the central Sahara in small stands and is found nowhere else. It is one of the oldest and longest-lived trees in the world, with individuals that may be thousands of years old, and only a few hundred are thought to remain. The trees are relics of a time when the climate was wetter, and the park is designated a Biosphere Reserve in part to protect them.

The plateau also supports Saharan myrtle, other desert plants adapted to scarce water, and animals such as Barbary sheep, gazelles, foxes and a wealth of birds and reptiles. Natural pools of water, called gueltas, form in rock basins after rain and sustain communities of life far from rivers. The rarest of all the animals, the Saharan cheetah, is reported only occasionally, a symbol of the fragility of life in this region.

What UNESCO Recognised: Criteria (i), (iii), (vii) and (viii)

Tassili n’Ajjer was inscribed on the World Heritage List in 1982 as a mixed site, under both cultural and natural criteria. Criterion (i) recognised the rock art as a masterpiece of human creative genius, and criterion (iii) recognised it as an exceptional testimony to a civilisation that has disappeared, with images covering many thousands of years. Criterion (vii) honoured the eroded sandstone forests of remarkable scenic interest, and criterion (viii) recognised the geological formations and their paleogeographical importance.

The site is also a national park, extended to 7,200,000 hectares, and a UNESCO Biosphere Reserve designated in 1986. It is protected by Algeria’s cultural heritage law and managed by the national park office of Tassili. Its enormous size, about the area of a medium-sized country, is both a safeguard and a challenge: much of the plateau is almost untouched, but patrolling and monitoring so vast an area is exceptionally difficult.

The Kel Ajjer and Living Culture

The plateau is not an empty place. The Kel Ajjer Tuareg have lived in and around Tassili for centuries, herding goats and camels, tending oasis gardens and guiding caravans, and their knowledge of the terrain is essential to its protection. Many of the guides who lead visitors across the plateau today are Tuareg, and their culture of poetry, music and hospitality is part of the experience of Tassili.

The oasis of Djanet also hosts a ritual festival known as Sebeiba, a dance and ceremony held each year that has been recognised by UNESCO as intangible cultural heritage. Together, the rock paintings of the plateau and the living traditions of its people form a continuous cultural landscape, linking the artists of six thousand years ago to the communities that inhabit it today.

Protecting a Fragile Gallery

The paintings have survived for thousands of years in sheltered places, but they are vulnerable. Wind, sand and salts erode the rock surface, and moisture can cause the pigments to flake. Human damage has been a serious problem: visitors have scratched or sprayed graffiti on the rock, touched the images and, in the past, even wetted or chalked them to make them more visible for photography or copying, which can harm the pigments.

Protection depends on controlling access and educating visitors. Tourism is managed through licensed guides and permits, and the park authorities work with the local community to monitor sites and prevent vandalism. Climate change, increasing temperatures and the threat of looting of archaeological material add to the concerns, and the regional security situation affects the ability of researchers and managers to work. The conservation of Tassili is a long-term task that requires both national commitment and international support.

Visiting Tassili n’Ajjer

Tassili is one of the world’s great adventure destinations, but it is demanding. Visitors usually fly to Djanet and set off with a licensed Tuareg guide on a trek of several days or weeks, travelling on foot with camels to carry supplies and camping under the stars. There are no roads across the plateau, and the climb to the high ground is steep. Visitor numbers are limited and access is regulated, so trips must be arranged in advance through authorised agencies.

The best season is the cooler months from about October to April; summers are dangerously hot. Visitors should be fit, carry plenty of water and sun protection and follow their guide’s instructions, especially about not touching the paintings. Because security conditions in the Sahara and along the borders can change, it is essential to check current government travel advice and the regulations of the park before planning a trip.

Reflection — A Warning Written on Stone

There is something unsettling about the paintings of Tassili. They show a world of abundance that vanished within a few thousand years, a green landscape turned to desert by a shift in the planet’s climate. The artists did not know what was coming, but their pictures record it step by step, as cattle gave way to horses and horses to camels. They are, in a sense, the oldest climate record made by human hands.

The lesson is not only about the past. As the planet warms again, the story of the Sahara reminds us how quickly and completely a landscape can change. Like the monuments of Ancient Thebes with its Necropolis, Egypt to the north-east and the great prehistoric sites such as Stonehenge, Avebury and Associated Sites, United Kingdom, Tassili asks us to read the traces of past peoples carefully, because they may have something to say about our own future.

✦ Conclusion

Tassili n’Ajjer is a place where art, geology and ecology meet. Its stone forests, its more than 15,000 paintings and engravings and its ancient cypresses make it one of the most extraordinary landscapes on Earth, and its inscription in 1982 recognised both its cultural and natural value. It is a gallery whose walls tell the story of the drying of the Sahara.

Protecting it requires care, from the guides who lead visitors to the authorities who manage a park the size of a country. For those who make the journey, the reward is a rare encounter with the first artists of Africa, in a silence broken only by wind and the footsteps of camels.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

Why is Tassili n’Ajjer a World Heritage Site?

It was inscribed in 1982 as a mixed site under criteria (i), (iii), (vii) and (viii). It holds one of the most important groups of prehistoric cave art in the world, with more than 15,000 paintings and engravings, and a lunar landscape of eroded sandstone forests of great geological interest.

How old is the rock art?

UNESCO says the drawings and engravings record the evolution of life on the edge of the Sahara from about 6000 BC to the first centuries of the present era. The images show different styles, from hunters and wild animals to herders and cattle, then horses, chariots and camels.

What does Tassili n’Ajjer mean?

Tassili means plateau in Tamahaq, the language of the northern Tuareg, and Ajjer is the name of the Tuareg people of the region. The name is usually translated as plateau of the rivers.

What is the Saharan cypress?

Cupressus dupreziana is a rare, very long-lived conifer that survives only in the Tassili region. It is a relic of a wetter Sahara, and only a few hundred trees are thought to remain.

How can I visit Tassili n’Ajjer?

Most visitors fly to the oasis town of Djanet and travel with licensed Tuareg guides on multi-day treks. Access is regulated, so trips must be arranged through authorised agencies, and travellers should check current security and travel advice before planning.
