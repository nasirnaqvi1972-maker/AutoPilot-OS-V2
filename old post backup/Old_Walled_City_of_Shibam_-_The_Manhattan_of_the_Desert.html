Old Walled City of Shibam — The Manhattan of the Desert

A Sixteenth-Century Walled City in the Wadi Hadramawt of Eastern Yemen Where Hundreds of Mud-Brick Tower Houses Rise Up to Eleven Storeys From a Rocky Outcrop Above the Palm Groves, One of the Oldest and Best Examples of Vertical Urban Planning in the World, Now Listed Among World Heritage in Danger | UNESCO World Heritage Site

Location:Wadi Hadramawt, Hadramawt Governorate, eastern Yemen, on a rocky outcrop above the wadi floor, with the Shibam oasis around it
Year of UNESCO Inscription:1982 (on the List of World Heritage in Danger since 2015)
Category:Cultural
UNESCO Reference:192

Skyscrapers of Mud

Cross the stony plains of the Hadramawt in eastern Yemen and, from far off, you will see a city that looks impossible. A cluster of tall, slender towers rises from the valley floor, packed shoulder to shoulder, their upper floors glowing white against the ochre cliffs behind. It could be the skyline of a modern metropolis, and indeed that is how it has often been described. Yet every one of these towers is made of mud, and most of them were built before the nineteenth century.

This is Shibam, and UNESCO describes it as one of the oldest and best examples of urban planning based on the principle of vertical construction. Its tower-like structures, rising out of the cliff, have earned the city the nickname of the Manhattan of the desert. Surrounded by a fortified wall, the sixteenth-century city is a masterpiece of compact, defensive and communal living, and its buildings are among the tallest mud structures in the world.

Shibam is also a city in trouble. Mud buildings are vulnerable to water, and the wadi has repeatedly flooded; since 2015 the war in Yemen has added the danger of explosions, neglect and economic collapse. The city is on the List of World Heritage in Danger. This post explores how and why it was built, what makes it extraordinary and the efforts of its residents and the international community to keep it standing.

Where the Wadi Meets the Desert

Shibam stands in the Wadi Hadramawt, a long, broad valley that cuts through the high plateau of eastern Yemen on its way to the sea. The valley floor is flat and fertile when watered, and for centuries it has been farmed with palm groves and irrigated fields, while the cliffs on either side rise in tawny walls. The city sits on a low rocky outcrop above the wadi, with the date-palm oasis spread around it, a patch of green in an otherwise arid land.

The Hadramawt has a long history as a centre of the incense trade, and its ancient capital, Shabwa, was a major city of the Hadramawt kingdom. According to the description published by the Dutch national commission for UNESCO, Shibam became the capital of the Hadramawt region after Shabwa was destroyed around AD 300, and for many centuries it has been a centre of regional trade, scholarship and power. The town today has about seven thousand inhabitants.

Why Build Up?

The reason for building tall is simple: security. The dense setting of Shibam’s tower houses goes back to the need for protection against rival clans and nomadic raiders, and a compact vertical city on an outcrop is much easier to defend than a sprawling one. The surrounding wall closed the circuit, and the narrow streets between the towers could be closed off in a crisis.

The setting also encouraged height. The outcrop offered limited space, the fertile land of the wadi was too valuable to build on, and floods threatened anything on low ground. The solution was to build up, stacking the rooms of a house on top of one another and sharing walls with neighbours. Most of the surviving houses date from the sixteenth century, when the town was rebuilt after a severe flood.

The Anatomy of a Tower House

A typical Shibam house is a tower of five to eleven storeys, rising to as much as thirty metres, and is usually the home of a single extended family. The ground floor is used for storage and, traditionally, for animals; the middle floors contain the family’s living and sleeping rooms; and the upper floors, with the best light and breezes, serve as reception rooms for guests and quarters for the men of the household. The roof terraces provided a place for the family to gather in the evening and to sleep in hot weather.

The houses are decorated with carved wooden doors, shutters and window frames, and the upper floors are often covered with lime plaster that gleams white in the sun, while the lower walls remain the colour of the earth. The lime layer protects the mud from rain and gives the skyline its distinctive banded appearance. Each house is an individual structure, yet together they form a continuous, harmonious whole.

Building With Mud

The material of Shibam is mud brick, made from local clay mixed with straw, shaped in moulds and dried in the sun. The walls are thick at the base and taper as they rise, which gives the towers stability, and the floors are carried on beams of palm wood and local timber, with palm leaves and mud forming the surface. The traditional builders have a deep understanding of how mud behaves, how thick a wall must be to carry a given height and how to protect it from water.

Mud architecture requires constant care. The surface is eroded by rain and must be replastered regularly, and the structure depends on regular repair of cracks and roofs. This tradition of maintenance has been passed down through generations of builders and masons, and it is as much a part of the heritage as the buildings themselves. When maintenance fails, a mud building can fall quickly.

Trade, Migration and the Wealth of the Hadramis

The Hadramawt has long been a land of traders. In antiquity its frankincense and myrrh travelled along caravan routes to the Mediterranean, a trade that also enriched cities such as Petra, Jordan. In later centuries many Hadramis emigrated, to East Africa, India and Southeast Asia, where they became merchants, scholars and community leaders, and some of the wealth they earned abroad was sent home to build and maintain houses in the wadi.

This history of movement helps to explain why Shibam is more than a rustic village. Its houses were built by families with connections across the Indian Ocean, and the city’s architecture reflects a cosmopolitan wealth. The tower houses are expressions of status as well as security, and the competition to build the highest and finest tower helped to create the skyline that visitors see today.

Floods: The Wadi’s Recurring Threat

Water is the great enemy of a mud city. The Hadramawt is arid, but when heavy rains fall in the highlands the wadi can turn into a torrent, and floods have repeatedly threatened Shibam. A severe flood is associated with the rebuilding of much of the city in the sixteenth century, and later floods, including severe flooding in the valley in 2008, damaged the wadi and the World Heritage property. A World Heritage Centre mission to Shibam in 2009 observed with great concern the extent of damage in the valley.

The city has historically been protected by embankments and flood channels, and UNESCO has noted that floods and damage to the wall are among the threats to the property’s state of conservation. The Old City, being built of mud and located in a flood-prone area, remains at severe risk of major damage unless necessary preventive measures are taken.

What UNESCO Recognised: Criteria (iii), (iv) and (v)

Shibam was inscribed on the World Heritage List in 1982 under criteria (iii), (iv) and (v). The inscription recognised the city as an exceptional testimony to a cultural tradition, as an outstanding example of a type of building and urban ensemble illustrating a significant stage in human history and as an outstanding example of a traditional human settlement that is vulnerable under the impact of irreversible change.

UNESCO’s short description sums up the value of the city: a fortified wall surrounds a sixteenth-century city that is one of the oldest and best examples of rigorous urban planning based on the principle of vertical construction. The property is the walled city itself, and the Shibam oasis around it is treated as its landscape buffer zone, though no area figure is recorded in UNESCO’s property data.

2015: War and the Danger List

In 2015 the armed conflict in Yemen began to threaten the country’s heritage, and in the same year the World Heritage Committee placed Shibam on the List of World Heritage in Danger, alongside other Yemeni sites, because of the armed conflict situation. The city was not at the centre of fighting, but it suffered the effects of the war and of the collapse of public services and funding.

On 20 November 2015 a car exploded near the city wall, causing severe damage to historic buildings on the southern and western edges of the city. The Committee noted the damage and the conservation challenges, and a UNESCO Emergency Action Plan, adopted in July 2015, sought to protect Yemen’s heritage amid the conflict. Reactive Monitoring missions have repeatedly been proposed but could not take place because of the security situation.

Keeping the City Alive

Despite the crisis, work has continued. The General Organization for the Preservation of Historic Cities in Yemen and the community of Shibam have carried out damage assessment, documentation, first-aid interventions and capacity building, efforts the Committee has commended. International projects have provided support: the European Union has funded a project, Youth Employment through Culture and Heritage in Yemen, of about 22.5 million US dollars for 2022 to 2026, which offered work to more than six thousand young Yemenis in four historic cities including Shibam.

Other partners have also contributed, including a Japanese-funded project in 2023 on building climate-resilient communities in historic cities, and a project funded by the ALIPH Foundation on the architecture of Shibam. These initiatives recognise that the survival of the city depends on its people, on keeping families in the houses and giving masons the work and materials to maintain them.

The 2026 Decision and the Road Ahead

At its 48th session in Busan in 2026, the World Heritage Committee decided to retain the Old Walled City of Shibam on the List of World Heritage in Danger. It reiterated its request that Yemen, together with the World Heritage Centre and the Advisory Bodies, begin remote preparation of the Desired state of conservation for removal of the property from the Danger List, along with a set of corrective measures and a timeframe, and asked for a new report by 1 February 2027.

Yemen’s own 2025 report highlighted threats including development pressure within the buffer zone and projects near the southern edge of the property within a flood channel, and expressed the hope of making enough progress to support removal from the Danger List. The Committee’s decision indicates that the path to that goal remains long, but that the international community is committed to staying engaged with the city’s future.

Visiting Shibam: An Honest Note

In peaceful times Shibam was one of the most rewarding destinations in the Arabian Peninsula, reached from the nearby town of Seiyun, with visitors climbing to a viewpoint across the wadi for the classic photograph of the towers at sunset and wandering the lanes of the old city. Since 2015 Yemen has been in a state of conflict, and almost all governments advise against travel to the country. Tourism has all but ended.

Anyone interested in Shibam should therefore explore it through books, documentaries and the photographs and reports of heritage organisations, and should support reputable groups working to protect Yemen’s historic cities. If conditions change, check current official travel advice and rely on trusted local contacts before any thought of a visit.

Reflection — Heritage That Needs Its People

Shibam teaches that a city of mud is a city of people. A stone monument may stand for centuries without attention, but a mud tower will survive only as long as someone repairs it, plasters it and lives in it. The great achievement of Shibam is not simply that it was built in the sixteenth century but that it has been maintained ever since, by generations of families and masons who understood their material.

That is why the crisis in Yemen is so serious for the city: it threatens the community, the economy and the skills on which its survival depends. In this Shibam shares the fate of other old cities of the Middle East, such as the Ancient City of Damascus, Syria, where war has threatened a heritage as much human as architectural.

✦ Conclusion

The Old Walled City of Shibam is one of the great urban inventions of the pre-modern world: a compact, vertical, defensible city of mud, built to withstand raiders and floods and to shelter families across generations. Its towers, its walls and its oasis make a landscape of rare unity, and its inscription in 1982 recognised it as a masterpiece of traditional architecture and planning.

Today it is at risk from conflict, floods and neglect, and its future depends on the people who live in it and on support from beyond Yemen. The city has survived for five centuries. With care, it can survive many more, and the world has a stake in seeing that it does.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

Why is Shibam called the Manhattan of the desert?

Because its mud-brick tower houses, many of them five to eleven storeys and up to about thirty metres high, rise together from a rocky outcrop in the Hadramawt valley like a skyline. UNESCO uses the nickname in its description of the city.

Why is Shibam a World Heritage Site?

It was inscribed in 1982 under criteria (iii), (iv) and (v) as one of the oldest and best examples of urban planning based on vertical construction, a fortified sixteenth-century city of mud-brick tower houses.

Is Shibam on the List of World Heritage in Danger?

Yes. It has been on the Danger List since 2015 because of the armed conflict in Yemen, and the World Heritage Committee decided to retain it there at its 48th session in 2026.

What are the houses made of?

They are made of sun-dried mud brick, with floors and roofs of palm wood and local timber and upper walls often covered with lime plaster. The material needs regular maintenance, especially after rain and floods.

Can I visit Shibam?

Almost all governments advise against travel to Yemen because of the conflict, and tourism has largely ended. Anyone considering a visit should check current official travel advice and cannot rely on normal services.
