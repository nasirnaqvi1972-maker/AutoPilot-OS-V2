An Island Alone in the Sea

Some islands feel like fragments of continents; Lord Howe feels like a place made by itself. About 600 kilometres east of the Australian mainland, in the Tasman Sea, a crescent of green peaks rises from the water, cradling a pale lagoon and fringed with white sand. Two mountains, Mount Lidgbird and Mount Gower, tower over the southern end of the island, their cliffs often wreathed in cloud, while the northern end falls away into low hills, forest and farmland. The island is small, only about eleven kilometres long and two wide, but it is among the most beautiful and biologically special islands in the Pacific.

Lord Howe is the main island of a group of twenty-eight islands, islets and rocks, the remnants of a vast volcano that erupted from the seabed some seven million years ago. UNESCO inscribed the group on the World Heritage List in 1982 under natural criteria (vii) and (x), recognising both its spectacular scenery and its exceptional natural diversity. The property covers some 146,300 hectares, including the surrounding sea and coral reefs, of which only about 1,540 hectares are land.

The island's isolation has shaped everything about it. Never connected to a continent, it was colonised only by species that could cross hundreds of kilometres of ocean, and these species, free from competition, evolved into forms found nowhere else. To visit Lord Howe is to see a natural experiment in evolution, and to appreciate how precious, and how fragile, such places are.

Born of a Volcano

The island is the eroded summit of a shield volcano that rose from the ocean floor, over a hot spot in the Earth's crust, between about six and seven million years ago. At its height the volcano may have been far larger, a great cone dwarfing the island seen today, but millions of years of wind, rain and waves have worn it down to the ragged remnants that now rise above the sea. Over time the sea cut deep into the rim, leaving only the southern and northern ridges, which curve around a shallow lagoon.

Mount Gower, at 875 metres, is the highest point, and its summit holds a unique cloud forest, kept moist by the sea mists that cling to the peak. Mount Lidgbird, nearby, rises to about 777 metres, and the two form a dramatic wall of cliffs that is the island's finest sight. Behind them, on the island's western side, the land slopes gently to the lagoon, protected by a barrier of coral that has grown on the volcanic platform.

Some twenty kilometres to the south-east stands Ball's Pyramid, the most spectacular of the satellite islands: a knife-edge spire of volcanic rock rising 562 metres straight from the sea, only about 1,100 metres long and 300 wide. It is the tallest volcanic stack in the world, and the sight of it standing alone in the empty ocean is a reminder of the immense power of the forces that created the islands.

Discovery and the Kentia Palm

The island was uninhabited when Europeans found it. It was first sighted on 17 February 1788 by Lieutenant Henry Lidgbird Ball, commanding HMS Supply on a voyage from Sydney to Norfolk Island, and it was named after Richard Howe, First Lord of the Admiralty. Whalers and other ships began to call at the island for fresh food and water, and in 1834 the first permanent settlers arrived, establishing farms and a trade in provisions for passing vessels.

The islanders found an unexpected source of income in the 1880s, when they began to collect the seeds of the kentia palm, Howea forsteriana, a tall, graceful palm that grows naturally only on Lord Howe. The tree proved ideal as an indoor plant, tolerant of low light and cool conditions, and became a Victorian favourite. Today the seed trade is still an important source of income for the island, and kentia palms are grown in homes and offices across the world.

About 350 people live on the island today, governed by a local board, and the community has a close relationship with the natural world. Tourism is strictly limited to protect the environment, with a cap on the number of visitors at any time, and about three-quarters of the island, the Permanent Park Preserve, is protected as a reserve. The result is a rare example of a community and a landscape living in careful balance.

A Living Laboratory of Endemic Life

Lord Howe's isolation has produced an extraordinary flora and fauna. Of the island's native plants, more than a hundred species are found nowhere else, including four species of palm, among them the kentia palm and the curly palm, as well as ferns, orchids and trees. The forests range from lowland palm forests to the misty, moss-hung forest at the summit of Mount Gower, and their unique composition has led them to be recognised as an ecoregion of their own.

The animal life is equally distinctive. The island has a rich assemblage of endemic invertebrates, including snails, beetles and spiders, many of which live in the highland forest. Birds are the most visible. Several endemic land birds survive, and the island supports a large number of nesting seabirds, among them the Providence petrel, which nests on the slopes of the high mountains, the masked booby, the red-tailed tropicbird and vast colonies of sooty terns and wedge-tailed shearwaters, known locally as muttonbirds.

The island's wildlife reflects the principle at the heart of the World Heritage listing under criterion (x), which recognises the island as an outstanding example of the development of an insular biota adapted through speciation. Each species is a record of an ancient arrival and a long divergence, and the island as a whole is a living textbook of evolution.

The Woodhen That Would Not Vanish

Of all the island's birds, the Lord Howe woodhen is the most remarkable. It is a flightless rail, about the size of a small chicken, with brown plumage, a strong pointed bill and a bold, inquisitive character. Once common across the island, it was nearly wiped out by the arrival of pigs, goats and cats, which destroyed its nests and ate its young, and by hunting. By the 1970s, only about thirty birds survived, all of them in the high forests of the southern mountains.

At the time of the island's World Heritage inscription, it was regarded as one of the rarest birds in the world. A dramatic rescue programme began in the early 1980s. Pigs were eradicated from the island, feral goats were removed, and a small number of woodhens were taken into captivity and bred, then released into the lowlands. The numbers of the bird rose rapidly, and today a few hundred woodhens live on the island, a triumph of conservation.

The woodhen's recovery is one of the great success stories of Australian wildlife management. It shows that, with a clear understanding of the causes of decline, and with determined effort, even a species reduced to a handful of individuals can be brought back from the brink.

The Tree Lobster of Ball's Pyramid

One of the most improbable stories in natural history belongs to the Lord Howe stick insect, Dryococelus australis. It is a large, glossy, almost black insect, about twelve centimetres long, with a robust body that earned it the nickname of the tree lobster. In 1918, the supply ship Makambo ran aground at the island and black rats escaped from the wreck, spreading across the land. Within a few years, the stick insect had vanished from Lord Howe, and the species was declared extinct.

Then, in the 1960s, climbers on Ball's Pyramid reported seeing dead stick insects on the stack, and in 2001 scientists, scaling the cliffs at night, found a tiny population of about two dozen living under a single shrub, clinging to a precarious ledge high above the sea. It was the only place on earth where the species survived. A few individuals were taken into captive breeding programmes, notably at Melbourne Zoo, and the species has since flourished in captivity.

The tree lobster is now one of the most celebrated examples of an apparently extinct species rediscovered. Its story has inspired books and films, and plans to return it to Lord Howe depend on first removing the rats, a task that has been the island's major conservation project of recent years.

The Reef at the End of the World

The waters of the Lord Howe Group are as remarkable as the land. The lagoon on the western side of the main island is sheltered by a fringing coral reef, which is the most southerly true coral reef in the world, lying at about 31 degrees south. It exists because a warm current brings tropical larvae from the north, while its position far from the mainland keeps the water clear and clean.

The result is a meeting place of tropical and temperate marine life. More than eighty species of coral grow on the reef, and the surrounding waters are home to hundreds of species of fish, among them butterflyfish, angelfish, parrotfish and wrasses, along with species found only in the cooler waters of the south. Turtles graze over the sea grasses, and bigger visitors, including kingfish, reef sharks, dolphins and whales, pass through the waters.

The marine area is protected by a system of marine parks and reserves that extends well beyond the reef itself. Snorkelling in the lagoon is a highlight of any visit, and at Ned's Beach, visitors can wade into shallow water and hand-feed fish that come to the shore, a gentle, intimate encounter with the sea.

Rats, Rescue and Visiting Lord Howe

The same rats that wiped out the stick insect proved a catastrophe for the island. Introduced in 1918, they are believed to have contributed to the extinction of several native birds and many invertebrates, and to have damaged the seeds of the palms and the nests of seabirds. For decades, they were the most important threat to the island's wildlife, and the community decided to try to remove them.

In 2019, after years of planning and consultation, a large-scale baiting programme was carried out by helicopter across the island, in an effort to eradicate rodents. After extensive monitoring, the island was declared free of rats and mice in 2021, a major conservation achievement. The success has opened the way to reintroduce lost species and to restore the island's ecosystems.

Visitors reach Lord Howe by air, on flights of about two hours from Sydney, Brisbane or Port Macquarie. Numbers are limited, and accommodation is best booked well in advance. There are no large resorts and no mobile phone towers in the old sense; people walk or cycle. The Mount Gower climb, a full-day walk, may be undertaken only with a licensed guide, and the weather is best from September to May. Visitors should check current conditions and biosecurity rules before travelling.

Reflection

Standing on the beach at dusk, with the lagoon turning pink and the great mountains darkening behind, one has the sense of being at the edge of something fragile and precious. Lord Howe has lost species and recovered others; it has seen the seeds of its own palms carried around the world, and it has learned, painfully, what a single rat can do to a place that evolved without predators. The story of the island is one of vulnerability and renewal, and that is perhaps why it moves so many who visit. It reminds us that nature is not a fixed thing but a continuing negotiation, and that careful, patient work can still change the outcome.

✦ Conclusion

The Lord Howe Island Group is one of the world's finest examples of an oceanic island, a place where volcanic drama, tropical beauty and evolutionary uniqueness come together in a few kilometres of land. Its peaks, its palms, its birds and its reef are all part of a single, tightly woven ecosystem, and the loss of any part would diminish the whole.

It is also a place of hope. The woodhen's recovery, the rediscovery of the tree lobster and the removal of rodents show what determined conservation can achieve. For the traveller, Lord Howe offers solitude and natural wonder; for the world, it offers a model of how a small community and a fragile environment can survive together.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

1. Where is Lord Howe Island?

It lies in the Tasman Sea about 600 kilometres east of the New South Wales coast, and is part of New South Wales, Australia.

2. Why is it a World Heritage Site?

It was inscribed in 1982 under criteria (vii) and (x) for its spectacular volcanic scenery and its outstanding example of an island ecosystem with many endemic species.

3. What is special about Ball's Pyramid?

It is a 562-metre volcanic stack about 20 kilometres from Lord Howe and the tallest of its kind in the world. It is home to the rediscovered Lord Howe stick insect.

4. How do I get there and how many visitors are allowed?

By air from Sydney, Brisbane or Port Macquarie. The number of tourists on the island at any one time is limited, so book early.

5. Can I climb Mount Gower?

Yes, but only with a licensed guide, on a demanding full-day walk. Check conditions and book ahead.
