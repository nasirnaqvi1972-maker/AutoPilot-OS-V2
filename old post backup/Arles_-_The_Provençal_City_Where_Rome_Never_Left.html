Arles — The Provençal City Where Rome Never Left

A Roman City Wearing Medieval Armour

Few cities in France wear their past as openly as Arles. Walk through its old centre, a maze of narrow streets and pale-stone façades just above the Rhône, and you turn a corner to find a Roman amphitheatre rising above the rooftops, its double tier of arches glowing in the Provençal sun. A little farther on stand two lonely columns of a Roman theatre, a stretch of vaulted galleries beneath a medieval square and a great Romanesque church with a portal carved in the twelfth century. Rome is not buried beneath Arles; it is built into it.

UNESCO inscribed the city in 1981 as Arles, Roman and Romanesque Monuments, a title that captures its unusual strength. The earliest of its Roman monuments, the arena, the theatre and the cryptoporticus, date back to the first century BC. A second golden age in the fourth century left the baths of Constantine and the necropolis of Alyscamps. Then, in the eleventh and twelfth centuries, Arles flourished again, and its Romanesque church of Saint-Trophime, with its cloister, became one of the major monuments of Provence.

What makes Arles remarkable is the way these layers fit together. Its monuments were not abandoned and replaced but reused: the amphitheatre became a fortress, the baths a count’s palace, the theatre a quarry. This post explores the city from its Roman foundation through its medieval revival to its life today as a place of festivals, bulls and painters, and explains why it is one of the most significant ancient-to-medieval cities in Europe.

From River Crossing to Roman Colony

Arles owes its existence to the Rhône. At the point where the river divides into the arms of its delta, the site offered a natural crossing and a port connecting the Mediterranean to the interior of Gaul. Celts and Greeks traded here before the Romans arrived, and in the first century BC the town became a major Roman centre. In 46 BC Julius Caesar established a colony for veterans of his Sixth Legion, and the settlement took the name Arelate.

Under Augustus and his successors Arelate grew into a prosperous city, a river port and a node on the great roads of southern Gaul. It acquired the monuments that mark any important Roman town: a forum, a theatre, an arena, baths and a network of underground galleries. The grid of its Roman streets is still faintly visible in the plan of the old centre, and its ancient prosperity formed the foundation for everything that followed.

The Amphitheatre: Arena, Fortress and Village

The most imposing monument is the amphitheatre, built in the late first century AD. Its elliptical wall rises in two storeys of arcades, sixty on each level, and the arena once held spectators in the tens of thousands, who came to watch gladiatorial combats and animal hunts. Even today, its stone seating and its vaulted corridors convey the scale of Roman public entertainment.

Its later history is just as interesting. UNESCO notes that the amphitheatre was turned into a fortress, and in the Middle Ages four towers were added above the arcades, three of which survive. Hundreds of people lived inside the arena in a small walled neighbourhood of houses and a chapel, a village within a monument. In the nineteenth century the houses were cleared and the arena restored, and it now hosts bullfights in the Provençal tradition, concerts and festivals, as it has done for almost two thousand years.

The Roman Theatre and the Venus of Arles

Beside the amphitheatre stand the remains of the Roman theatre, built around the end of the first century BC in the reign of Augustus. Its semicircular auditorium once held thousands of spectators and its stage was backed by an elaborate façade of columns and statues. Today only two columns of the stage building survive, known locally as the Two Widows, together with the curved seating and the lower arches.

The theatre was heavily plundered in later centuries, its stones carried off to build churches, houses and fortifications. In 1651, during excavations, workers found the pieces of a famous marble statue, the Venus of Arles, which was later taken to the Louvre in Paris. Each summer the ruined theatre once again fills with an audience for performances, a reminder that it was built to be used.

The Cryptoporticus: A City Beneath the Forum

Beneath the Place du Forum, the medieval and modern heart of Arles, lies a secret: a network of vaulted underground galleries known as the cryptoporticus. Built in the first century BC, probably to support the terraces of the Roman forum on sloping ground, these galleries form a vast U-shaped structure, dark, cool and astonishingly well preserved. They may also have served as storage and as shelter in hot weather.

The cryptoporticus is among the oldest of the Roman monuments listed by UNESCO and one of the most unusual. Walking beneath the modern square, visitors can follow the rows of arches that carried the ancient forum overhead, an experience that makes the layering of Arles’s history physical and immediate.

Constantine and the Second Golden Age

In the fourth century Arles entered a second period of prosperity. The emperor Constantine lived in the city for periods, and in 314 it hosted an important church council, the Council of Arles, which dealt with disputes in the early Christian church. The city became one of the principal centres of imperial government in Gaul, and its great buildings were enlarged and embellished. A poet of the age even called it a little Rome of the Gauls.

The main surviving monument of this period is the Baths of Constantine, a large bathing complex of which the apsed hall of the hot room, built of brick and stone, still stands near the Rhône. UNESCO notes that the baths were later converted into a count’s palace, another example of reuse. The fourth-century city also gave Arles one of its most atmospheric sites, the necropolis of Alyscamps.

Alyscamps: The Avenue of the Dead

Just outside the Roman walls, the road known as the Via Aurelia was lined with tombs, and from antiquity Arles buried its dead here. The necropolis became known as the Alyscamps, a name derived from the Latin for the Elysian Fields, the paradise of the ancient world. When Christianity arrived, the cemetery became a holy place, its graves clustered around the tomb of an early martyr, and burial here was a privilege sought across Europe.

For centuries the Alyscamps was one of the most famous cemeteries in the Christian world, and its stone sarcophagi lined the avenue in long rows. The poet Dante mentions it in his Inferno, and in the late nineteenth century it was painted by Paul Gauguin and Vincent van Gogh. Many sarcophagi were removed or lost, and the construction of a railway in the nineteenth century cut into the site, but a tree-lined avenue of tombs and a ruined church still survive, among the most evocative places in Provence.

Saint-Trophime: Romanesque Splendour

In the eleventh and twelfth centuries Arles again became one of the great cities of the Mediterranean world, the centre of an archbishopric and part of the kingdom known as Burgundy-Arles. In 1178 the emperor Frederick Barbarossa was crowned king of Arles here, a reflection of its political importance. Its wealth went into new buildings, above all the Church of Saint-Trophime, built on the site of an earlier cathedral and dedicated to the saint traditionally regarded as the first bishop of Arles.

UNESCO recognises Saint-Trophime and its cloister as among the major monuments of Provençal Romanesque art. The great west portal, carved in the twelfth century, is modelled on the triumphal arches of Rome and filled with sculpture showing the Last Judgment, with Christ in majesty above a procession of the saved and the damned. The adjoining cloister, begun in the twelfth century and completed in the fourteenth, has galleries of carved capitals with scenes from the Bible and the lives of saints, among the finest of their kind in France.

What UNESCO Recognised: Criteria (ii) and (iv)

Arles was inscribed on the World Heritage List in 1981 under two criteria. Under criterion (ii), UNESCO recognised that the Cathedral of Saint-Trophime, together with its cloister, ranks among the major monuments of Provençal Romanesque art, a work that influenced and exchanged ideas with the wider Romanesque world. Under criterion (iv), it recognised Arles, with its Roman and Romanesque monuments, as a particularly significant example of how an ancient city was adapted to medieval European civilisation.

The property is made up of several components in the old centre, including the amphitheatre, the Roman theatre, the cryptoporticus and forum, the Baths of Constantine, the Alyscamps and Saint-Trophime. The original title, Roman and Romanesque Monuments of Arles, was later reworded to the present form. The French authorities manage the property in cooperation with the city, which plans and regulates change within the historic centre.

Van Gogh in Arles

Arles has a second claim on the imagination: it was the town where Vincent van Gogh did some of his most famous work. He arrived in February 1888, seeking light and colour, and in about fourteen months produced several hundred paintings and drawings, including The Night Café, Starry Night Over the Rhône and the view of a café terrace on the Place du Forum at night. In December 1888, after a quarrel with his friend Gauguin, he suffered a breakdown, and in 1889 he left for an asylum at nearby Saint-Rémy.

None of Van Gogh’s paintings is in Arles today, but the city has made his presence a part of its identity. Visitors can trace the places he painted, visit the cultural centre in the former hospital where he was treated and see how the light he admired still falls on the façades of the old town. It is a reminder that the heritage of a city includes the imaginations that it has inspired.

A Living City: Bulls, Photographs and Festivals

Arles is a working town with a lively cultural life. Its amphitheatre hosts bullfights in the Provençal and Spanish traditions, and its festivals, among them the Feria in spring and autumn, fill the streets with music and costume. Since 1970 the town has also hosted the Rencontres d’Arles, one of the world’s leading photography festivals, which uses old churches, warehouses and courtyards as exhibition spaces. A striking tower designed by Frank Gehry for the Luma foundation, opened in 2021, shows the willingness of the town to add contemporary architecture to the old fabric.

The city’s Roman past is also on display in the Musée départemental Arles antique, which holds sculpture, mosaics and the remains of a first-century river barge raised from the Rhône. The Rhône itself remains both an asset and a threat, since severe floods, such as those of 2003, have tested the town’s defences. Managing floods, traffic and heavy summer tourism while keeping the historic centre alive is the main challenge of the city today.

Visiting Arles

Arles is small and best explored on foot. The main monuments, the amphitheatre, the theatre, the cryptoporticus, the Baths of Constantine, Saint-Trophime and the Alyscamps, are within a short walk of one another, and a combined ticket usually covers several of them. A day is enough for a quick visit, but two days allows time for the museum, the Van Gogh sites and the markets that fill the streets on Wednesday and Saturday mornings.

The best months are April to June and September to October, when the weather is warm but not oppressive. Summer can be very hot, and the mistral wind occasionally blows hard. Arles is also an excellent base for the Camargue, the delta of salt marshes and wild horses to the south, for the hill town of Les Baux and for the Roman aqueducts and bridges of the region. Check current opening hours and ticket arrangements before visiting.

Reflection — The Wisdom of Reuse

Arles offers a lesson that heritage conservation sometimes forgets: that continuity can be more eloquent than preservation. The amphitheatre was not kept as a ruin but turned into a fortress, a village and a bullring; the baths became a palace; the theatre’s stones became churches. Each reuse destroyed something and saved something, and the result is a city whose history cannot be separated into neat periods.

In this it resembles other layered places that UNESCO protects, from the classical monuments of the Acropolis, Athens, Greece to the medieval abbey of Mont-Saint-Michel and its Bay, France. Each shows that the past is not a separate country but a series of rooms we continue to live in, and Arles invites us to live in them with curiosity and care.

✦ Conclusion

Arles is a city that rewards slow walking. Its Roman arena, theatre, galleries, baths and cemetery, and its Romanesque church and cloister, tell the story of two thousand years of continuous urban life on a bend of the Rhône. UNESCO’s inscription in 1981 recognised them as a significant example of how an ancient city was adapted to medieval European civilisation.

Today the monuments are used as well as admired: bulls run in the arena, plays are performed in the theatre, photographers fill the churches and Van Gogh’s café still glows at night. Come with time, wear comfortable shoes and let the layers of the city reveal themselves one by one.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

What are the Roman and Romanesque monuments of Arles?

They include the Roman amphitheatre, the Roman theatre, the cryptoporticus under the forum, the Baths of Constantine, the necropolis of Alyscamps and the Romanesque church of Saint-Trophime with its cloister. They were inscribed on the World Heritage List in 1981.

Why is Arles a World Heritage Site?

Under criterion (ii) the Cathedral of Saint-Trophime and its cloister rank among the major monuments of Provençal Romanesque art; under criterion (iv) Arles is a significant example of how an ancient city was adapted to medieval European civilisation.

Is the Arles amphitheatre still used?

Yes. It hosts bullfights in the Provençal and Spanish traditions, concerts and festivals. In the Middle Ages it was a fortress, with houses built inside it.

Did Van Gogh live in Arles?

Yes. Van Gogh lived in Arles from February 1888 until 1889, producing several hundred works, including Starry Night Over the Rhône and The Night Café. None of his paintings is in the town today, but many of the sites he painted can be visited.

When is the best time to visit?

April to June and September to October offer warm weather and manageable crowds. Summer is hot and busy. Check opening hours for monuments and the dates of festivals and bullfights before planning.
