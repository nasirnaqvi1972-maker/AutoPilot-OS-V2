The Building That Witnessed the End of One World

At 8:15 on the morning of August 6, 1945, a United States Army Air Forces B-29 bomber named Enola Gay released a single bomb over the city of Hiroshima in western Japan. The bomb — code-named Little Boy, a uranium-235 fission device of approximately 15 kilotons yield — detonated at an altitude of approximately 600 metres above the city, directly above the courtyard of the Shima Surgical Clinic. In the fraction of a second that followed, a fireball of approximately 300,000 degrees Celsius formed at the point of detonation, a shockwave of supersonic velocity expanded outward across the city, and a pulse of thermal radiation sufficient to ignite fires at distances of up to 3.5 kilometres from the hypocenter struck every surface facing the explosion. Approximately 70,000 people died instantly or within the first day. By the end of 1945, the death toll from the immediate explosion, the burns, the blast injuries, and the radiation sickness that followed had reached approximately 140,000. The city of Hiroshima — a city of approximately 350,000 people, a major military and industrial centre, a place of ordinary human life — was, in a single moment, transformed into a landscape of ash.

One building survived in the immediate vicinity of the hypocenter. The Hiroshima Prefectural Industrial Promotion Hall — a five-storey building of brick and reinforced concrete, designed by the Czech architect Jan Letzel and completed in 1915 — was located approximately 160 metres from the hypocenter. Because the bomb detonated almost directly above it, the force of the explosion came primarily downward rather than horizontally, and the building's walls, though gutted by fire and stripped of everything except their structural shell, remained standing. The dome of its roof — an iron framework that had supported a copper-clad cupola — collapsed inward but retained its skeletal form. Everything inside was destroyed. Everyone inside was killed. But the structure itself stood.

It has been standing ever since. Preserved exactly as the bomb left it — its brick walls cracked and exposed, its dome framework bare, its interior a void of exposed rubble — the building was designated the Hiroshima Peace Memorial, or Genbaku Dome (Atomic Bomb Dome), and inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1996 as a site of outstanding universal significance: a monument not to a particular nation's history but to the history of all humanity, and to the shared aspiration for a world in which the weapons that destroyed Hiroshima will never be used again.

The Bomb — What Happened on August 6

The events of August 6, 1945 in Hiroshima represent the single most destructive act carried out by a single weapon in the history of warfare — and an event whose significance extends far beyond the military context of the Second World War into the deepest questions about the relationship between human technological capability and human moral responsibility.

The decision to use atomic weapons against Japan was taken at the highest levels of the United States government, in the context of a Pacific war that had already caused extraordinary casualties on both sides — the battles of Iwo Jima and Okinawa had cost tens of thousands of American and Japanese lives — and in which an invasion of the Japanese home islands was projected to cost still more. The ethical dimensions of the decision — whether the use of a weapon of mass destruction against a civilian population was justified by the military circumstances, whether alternatives existed that were not pursued, whether the targeting of Hiroshima followed by Nagasaki three days later was militarily necessary or represented something beyond military necessity — have been debated by historians, ethicists, and political philosophers ever since, without reaching consensus, and the debate continues today.

What is beyond debate is the human reality of what happened. The 140,000 deaths in Hiroshima were not statistics. They were individual human beings — men, women, and children going about their ordinary lives on an ordinary summer morning — whose lives were ended or irrevocably damaged by a single technological act. The survivors — the hibakusha — carried with them for the rest of their lives the physical and psychological consequences of their exposure: the burns, the radiation sickness, the cancers, the grief, and the particular burden of being witnesses to something that the rest of the world found almost impossible to comprehend.

The Building Before the Bomb — Jan Letzel's Design

The building that became the Genbaku Dome was not always a symbol of destruction. It was, when it was completed in 1915, one of the most elegant and cosmopolitan buildings in the city — a celebration of Hiroshima's modernity and of Japan's Meiji-era openness to international architectural ideas. Jan Letzel, the Czech architect who designed it, was working in Japan in the early twentieth century, and his design for the Hiroshima Prefectural Industrial Promotion Hall reflected the European Renaissance Revival style fashionable in institutional architecture of the period: a symmetrical five-storey facade of brick and stone, its central entrance tower crowned by the distinctive copper-clad dome that would become, in its ruined state, the most recognisable image of the atomic age.

The building served as a commercial and cultural centre for Hiroshima — hosting exhibitions, trade fairs, and civic events in the years between its completion and the morning of August 6, 1945. On that morning, approximately thirty city government employees were working inside. None survived. The building that had been a symbol of Hiroshima's civic ambition and cultural openness became, in a fraction of a second, a symbol of something else entirely — the most extreme possible expression of the destructive potential of modern technology applied to the ending of human life.

The decision to preserve the ruin rather than demolish it was not made immediately. In the years after the war, as Hiroshima was rebuilt around it, there were debates in the city about whether the Genbaku Dome should be maintained as a permanent reminder or cleared away to allow the city to move forward without carrying the physical mark of its most catastrophic moment. The decision to preserve it — confirmed formally in 1966 by the Hiroshima City Council — was a decision made in favour of memory, of witness, and of the moral obligation to ensure that what happened here would never be forgotten or minimised.

The Peace Memorial Park and Museum

The Hiroshima Peace Memorial Park — established on the flat ground near the hypocenter that had been cleared by the bomb — was designed by the Japanese architect Kenzo Tange and completed in 1954. It is one of the finest and most moving examples of memorial architecture in the world: a carefully composed landscape in which the Genbaku Dome, the cenotaph, the Flame of Peace, the Children's Peace Monument, and the Peace Memorial Museum are arranged along a single axis that aligns the cenotaph arch precisely with the dome of the ruined building, so that standing at the cenotaph and looking through its arch the visitor sees the Genbaku Dome framed in the distance — a composition of quiet and permanent power.

The cenotaph — a simple saddle-shaped stone arch designed by Tange — bears the inscription: Let all the souls here rest in peace, for we shall not repeat the evil. The inscription is in Japanese, but its meaning transcends language: it is addressed not to any particular people or nation but to all of humanity, as a statement of the collective moral obligation that the survivors and the descendants of Hiroshima have assumed — and that the UNESCO inscription of 1996 asked the entire world to share.

The Peace Memorial Museum, at the far end of the park from the Genbaku Dome, houses one of the most powerful and most affecting museum experiences in the world. Its collections — the personal objects of the victims, the physical evidence of the bomb's effects, the testimonies of the hibakusha — present the human reality of the atomic bombing in a way that no amount of abstract knowledge or statistical description can approximate. Visitors leave the museum changed. This is its purpose, and it fulfils it.

Sadako and the Thousand Cranes

Among the most powerful stories associated with Hiroshima is that of Sadako Sasaki — a girl who was two years old when the atomic bomb was dropped, who survived the immediate blast but developed leukaemia ten years later as a result of her radiation exposure. According to Japanese tradition, a person who folds one thousand origami paper cranes will be granted a wish. Sadako — hospitalised at the age of twelve with leukaemia — began folding cranes, wishing for her own recovery. She died in October 1955, before she could complete the thousand. Her classmates completed the remaining cranes and buried them with her.

Sadako's story became one of the most widely known human stories to emerge from Hiroshima — translated into children's books in dozens of languages, the basis of memorials in cities around the world, and the origin of the practice of sending paper cranes to Hiroshima as an expression of the shared wish for peace. The Children's Peace Monument in the Memorial Park — a bronze figure of a girl holding a golden crane aloft — was built in 1958 in her honour, funded by donations from schoolchildren across Japan. Every year, tens of millions of paper cranes are donated to the monument by children from every country in the world, hung in colourful garlands around the base of the statue. The cranes are eventually composted and the paper returned to the earth. New cranes arrive the following year.

The UNESCO Inscription — Controversy and Meaning

The inscription of the Hiroshima Peace Memorial on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1996 was not without controversy. The United States and China both abstained from the vote — the United States expressing concern that the inscription might be used to make a one-sided political statement about the atomic bombing, China expressing the view that the site did not meet the standard universal significance criteria. The decision to inscribe it was taken by the World Heritage Committee over these objections, on the basis that the Genbaku Dome represents outstanding universal value as a symbol of the shared human aspiration for peace and nuclear disarmament — a value that transcends the specific military and political context of 1945 and speaks to all of humanity in all periods.

The inscription was recognised under a single criterion — criterion (vi): directly or tangibly associated with events or living traditions, with ideas, or with beliefs, with artistic and literary works of outstanding universal significance. It is one of the very few World Heritage Sites inscribed under criterion (vi) alone — a reflection of the fact that the Genbaku Dome has no architectural merit, no archaeological significance, no natural beauty. It is simply a ruin. But it is a ruin that bears witness to the most consequential single act of violence in human history, and to the collective human aspiration that it should never be repeated. For this, and for this alone, it was inscribed.

Reflection — The Ruin That Speaks

The Genbaku Dome is unlike any other World Heritage Site. It is not beautiful. It is not architecturally significant. It is not ancient. It is not the product of a great civilisation expressing its highest aspirations. It is the product of destruction — the accidental survival of a single building from the most violent single moment in human history, preserved not for what it is but for what it witnessed and for what it means.

What it means is not fixed. For the survivors and the descendants of Hiroshima, it means the specific memory of August 6, 1945 — the deaths, the suffering, the city destroyed. For the broader Japanese public, it means the consequences of militarism and of war. For the international community, it means the nuclear age and the responsibility that all nations bear for the arsenals they have built since 1945. For the children who fold paper cranes and send them to Hiroshima from countries around the world, it means the simple and universal wish that the world should be different from what it has been.

All of these meanings are present in the ruined building on the bank of the Motoyasu River. None of them cancels the others. And the building stands, as it has stood since August 6, 1945 — bearing witness, carrying memory, asking the question that it has been asking since the morning the bomb fell: what will you do to make sure this does not happen again? For readers who find in the Hiroshima Peace Memorial a confrontation with the extremes of human destructiveness in the twentieth century, the UNESCO-inscribed site of Auschwitz Birkenau in Poland — the largest and most lethal of the Nazi concentration and extermination camps, where approximately 1.1 million people, the vast majority of them Jewish, were murdered between 1940 and 1945 — represents a parallel and complementary act of witness. Both sites were inscribed on the World Heritage List not for their architectural merit or their natural beauty but for their function as permanent reminders of what human beings have done to each other — reminders that the world has a collective obligation to preserve, to visit, and to take seriously. ✦ Conclusion

The Hiroshima Peace Memorial is the most important monument to the dangers of nuclear weapons anywhere in the world — and one of the most important monuments to the shared human aspiration for peace. The Genbaku Dome does not need interpretation or explanation. It simply stands, as it has stood since August 6, 1945, on the bank of the Motoyasu River, in the middle of a city that was rebuilt around it, bearing on its ruined walls and its skeletal dome the physical evidence of what happened here.

The UNESCO inscription of 1996 asked the world to share the responsibility for this memory — to recognise that what happened in Hiroshima is not merely Japanese history or American history or the history of the Second World War but universal human history, carrying a warning and a hope that belong to all people in all times. The warning is clear. The hope is embodied in the paper cranes, in the children who fold them, in the millions of visitors who come each year to stand before the dome and look at what a single weapon did to a single city on a single morning — and to resolve, in whatever way they are able, that the world should do better than this.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: What is the Genbaku Dome? The Genbaku Dome — also known as the Hiroshima Peace Memorial or the Atomic Bomb Dome — is the preserved ruin of the former Hiroshima Prefectural Industrial Promotion Hall, the only building to survive in the immediate vicinity of the hypocenter of the atomic bomb dropped on Hiroshima on August 6, 1945. It was preserved exactly as the bomb left it and designated as a memorial to the victims of the bombing and a symbol of the aspiration for world peace.

Q2: How far was the Genbaku Dome from the hypocenter of the atomic bomb? The Genbaku Dome was approximately 160 metres from the hypocenter — the point directly below the bomb's detonation at an altitude of approximately 600 metres. Because the bomb detonated almost directly above the building, the force came primarily downward, which is why the walls survived while everything inside was destroyed.

Q3: Who was Sadako Sasaki? Sadako Sasaki was a girl who was two years old when the atomic bomb was dropped on Hiroshima. She survived the immediate blast but developed leukaemia at the age of twelve as a result of radiation exposure. She began folding origami paper cranes while hospitalised, following the Japanese tradition that folding a thousand cranes grants a wish. She died in October 1955 before completing the thousand. Her story has inspired the practice of sending paper cranes to Hiroshima as a symbol of the wish for peace.

Q4: Is the Hiroshima Peace Memorial a UNESCO World Heritage Site? Yes. The Hiroshima Peace Memorial was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1996, under criterion (vi), as a site directly and tangibly associated with an event of outstanding universal significance — the atomic bombing of August 6, 1945 — and as a universal symbol of the aspiration for world peace and nuclear disarmament.

Q5: Can visitors enter the Genbaku Dome? No. The Genbaku Dome is preserved as a ruin and is not open for entry. Visitors can walk around the outside of the building and view it from the Peace Memorial Park. The interior is visible through the open windows and doorways of the ruined structure.

🧳 Visitor's Guide

Nearest Airport — Hiroshima Airport (approximately 50 km east); also accessible from Osaka Kansai Airport (approximately 2 hours by shinkansen)

Getting There — Hiroshima is on the JR San'yo Shinkansen line — approximately 1.5 hours from Osaka, approximately 4 hours from Tokyo; local tram from Hiroshima Station to Peace Memorial Park (approximately 15 minutes)

Peace Memorial Park — Free entry to the park itself; Peace Memorial Museum entrance fee required (very modest charge)

Peace Memorial Museum — Essential — plan at least 2 hours; emotionally demanding — visitors should allow time to process the experience; open daily except certain public holidays

Best Season — Year-round; the August 6 Peace Memorial Ceremony (held annually on the anniversary of the bombing) is one of the most moving public ceremonies in Japan — arrive very early if attending

Time Needed — Half day for the Genbaku Dome and Peace Memorial Park; full day including the museum, the cenotaph, the Children's Peace Monument, and the surrounding Nakajima District

Respectful Behaviour — The Peace Memorial Park is a site of mourning and remembrance — conduct should be quiet and respectful throughout

Currency — Japanese Yen (JPY)

Language — Japanese — Peace Memorial Museum has excellent English-language exhibits and audio guides
