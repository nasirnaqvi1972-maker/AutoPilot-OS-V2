The Stones That Remember a Forgotten Empire

In the cool highlands of northern Ethiopia, more than two thousand metres above sea level, a field of enormous stones stands in the morning light. They are slender and tall, some upright, some tilted, one fallen in massive fragments across the earth. Each is a single piece of granite, carved on its faces with the outlines of doors, windows and beam-ends, so that it looks like a high-rise building flattened into a monolith. These are the stelae of Aksum, and they are among the most astonishing monuments created by any ancient civilisation in Africa.

The people who raised them belonged to a kingdom that, in its prime, was counted among the great powers of the ancient world. In the third century a Persian writer named Mani is said to have listed Aksum with Persia, Rome and China as one of the four great kingdoms of his age. Its merchants dealt in ivory, gold and incense; its ships sailed the Red Sea and the Indian Ocean; its kings minted coins in their own name; and in the fourth century one of them adopted Christianity and made it the faith of the state. UNESCO describes it as the most powerful state between the Eastern Roman Empire and Persia.

Yet Aksum is not only a ruin. It is a living town, home to tens of thousands of people, and its ancient church of Mary of Zion remains one of the holiest places in Ethiopian Orthodox Christianity. Emperors were crowned here for centuries after the kingdom itself had faded. This post explores the city that gave Ethiopia its first great state, the monuments that survive and the difficult work of protecting them.

Where Africa Met the World

The Kingdom of Aksum flourished from roughly the first century to the seventh or eighth centuries AD, in the highlands of what are now Tigray and Eritrea. Its location was a gift of geography. To the east lay the Red Sea and the port of Adulis, through which ships carried Aksumite goods to Egypt, Arabia, the Roman world and beyond. To the west lay the upland farmlands of the Ethiopian plateau, rich in grain, and the routes toward the Nile valley and the interior of Africa.

From these advantages the kingdom built a trading empire. Ivory, tortoiseshell, rhinoceros horn, gold and incense moved out through its ports, while wine, olive oil, glass and fine textiles moved in. A Greek sailors’ guide of the first century already describes Aksum as a major market for ivory. Greek-speaking merchants lived in the port, and Greek became a language of diplomacy and commerce alongside the local tongue, Ge’ez.

Coins, Script and the Making of a State

Aksum was one of the very few ancient African states to mint its own coinage. Its kings struck coins in gold, silver and bronze, many bearing the king’s portrait and inscriptions in Greek or Ge’ez, and these coins have been found as far away as southern Arabia and India. Coinage was both a convenience of trade and a declaration of sovereignty, an announcement that Aksum stood among the great powers.

The kingdom also developed one of the oldest indigenous writing systems in Africa. Ge’ez script, which evolved from earlier Semitic scripts, remains in use in the Ethiopian church today. Among the most precious written records is the Ezana Stone, an inscription of the fourth-century king that records his military campaigns and his conversion to Christianity in three languages: Ge’ez, Sabaean and Greek. Like the Rosetta Stone, it offers a key to understanding a world where several cultures intersected.

The Stelae Fields: Towers of Stone

The great monuments of the city are its stelae, monolithic granite obelisks that stand in several burial fields. The largest group lies in the Northern Stelae Park, where the visitor can follow the evolution of the form. The earliest stelae are plain and roughly shaped, little more than upright slabs. Later ones are carefully dressed and carved with relief designs that imitate the façades of multi-storey buildings, complete with false doors, windows and the projecting ends of wooden beams, a stone translation of the timber-and-masonry architecture of Aksumite palaces.

The stelae are believed to mark the graves of kings and nobles, and some once bore metal discs on their upper faces. Their size is staggering. The largest, known as the Great Stele, stood some 33 metres tall and is believed to have fallen during its erection, and it now lies broken on the ground, one of the largest single stones ever quarried by humans. Others, at 18, 16 and 15 metres, still stand or lean, a forest of ancient towers rising from the plain.

The Obelisk That Went to Rome

The most famous stele of Aksum is the one that left it. In 1937, during the Italian occupation of Ethiopia, soldiers dismantled a 24.6-metre obelisk weighing about 170 tonnes and shipped it to Rome, where it was erected as a trophy. For decades Ethiopians demanded its return. After years of negotiation it was finally brought home in 2005 in three pieces by air, and with the assistance of UNESCO it was reinstalled in Aksum on 31 July 2008.

The obelisk’s journey became a symbol of cultural restitution and of the way monuments can be caught in the politics of empire. Its return was greeted with celebration in Aksum, and it stands today among the other stelae as a reminder both of the antiquity of the kingdom and of the modern struggles over the ownership of heritage.

Tombs and Palaces Beneath the Earth

Beneath and around the stelae lie the tombs they commemorated. Large underground chambers, built of carefully fitted stone blocks and approached by stairways, served as royal burial places, and although most were looted long ago, their architecture remains impressive. Among the most notable are the tombs traditionally associated with the kings Kaleb and Gebre Meskel, which are built on a scale that suggests the wealth of their builders. West of the town, the Gudit stelae are interspersed with tombs mostly dating to the fourth century.

The palaces of the kings have survived less well, but a few remains hint at their grandeur. The ruins at Dungur, locally called the palace of the Queen of Sheba, show a large building of many rooms on a raised platform. In the sixth century the Greek traveller Cosmas Indicopleustes described the four-towered palace of the Aksumite king, adorned with bronze statues of unicorns. Modern archaeology cannot confirm the unicorns, but it can confirm that the city was once a place of palaces and monumental halls.

A Christian Kingdom: Ezana and the New Faith

In the fourth century a decisive change came to the kingdom. King Ezana, influenced according to tradition by a Christian missionary from Tyre named Frumentius, adopted Christianity, and the royal inscriptions and coins soon abandoned the symbols of the older gods in favour of the cross. Aksum became one of the earliest states in the world to adopt Christianity, and the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church that grew from this conversion remains one of the oldest Christian traditions in Africa.

The consequences were lasting. Christianity gave the Ethiopian highlands a distinctive identity and a link to the wider Christian world, while Ge’ez became the sacred language of liturgy and literature. The faith that took root at Aksum shaped later wonders such as the rock-hewn churches of Lalibela, Ethiopia, built centuries later by a different dynasty but inheritors of the same sacred tradition.

Mary of Zion and the Legend of the Ark

The spiritual heart of Aksum is the church of Our Lady Mary of Zion. According to Ethiopian tradition it houses the Ark of the Covenant, brought from Jerusalem by Menelik, the legendary son of King Solomon and the Queen of Sheba, a story told in the medieval Ethiopian text the Kebra Nagast. The Ark is said to rest in a small chapel, guarded by a single monk who is said to spend his life beside it and whom no other person may accompany.

Whatever the truth of the legend, the church has been a centre of devotion for well over a thousand years. It was restored by Emperor Dawit I in 1406, and Ethiopian emperors were crowned here for centuries. Each year in late November thousands of pilgrims gather in the town to celebrate the festival of Mary of Zion, a living continuation of the ceremonies that once defined the Ethiopian state.

Decline and Afterlife

The decline of Aksum was gradual and had several causes. From the seventh century, the rise of Islam and the shift of Red Sea trade routes reduced the kingdom’s commercial advantage, and the loss of control over the coast weakened its economy. Environmental pressures also played a part: a dense population and the demand for farmland and firewood probably strained the highland environment. By the tenth century the political power of the city had faded, and the centre of Ethiopian life moved southward.

Yet Aksum never lost its aura. Long after its political decline, Ethiopian emperors continued to be crowned there, and the city retained its symbolic status as the sacred origin of the Ethiopian monarchy. The legend of Solomon and Sheba, the memory of the Ark and the great stelae themselves all reinforced the sense that Aksum was the cradle of the nation, and that sense endures today.

What UNESCO Recognised: Criteria (i) and (iv)

UNESCO inscribed Aksum in 1980 under criteria (i) and (iv). Criterion (i), which recognises masterpieces of human creative genius, honours the monolithic obelisks, whose scale, craftsmanship and sculptural refinement place them among the supreme achievements of ancient engineering. Criterion (iv), which recognises outstanding examples of architectural ensembles that illustrate significant stages in human history, honours the stelae, tombs, palaces and ruins together as the surviving testimony of a great African civilisation.

UNESCO’s description notes that the ruins date from between the first and the thirteenth centuries AD and include obelisks, giant stelae, royal tombs and the remains of ancient castles. That span of more than a millennium shows how deeply the city’s story runs, from the heyday of the kingdom to the long afterlife of its sacred memory.

Conservation: Fragile Giants and Unsettled Boundaries

The stelae face practical dangers. UNESCO has recorded concern about the structural instability of one of the standing stones, known as Stele 3, and the great stones are vulnerable to weathering, ground movement and the pressures of a growing town. The boundaries of the property and its buffer zone have never been fully delineated to the standard UNESCO requires, and the Committee has repeatedly asked Ethiopia to submit detailed maps and a minor boundary modification so that development around the monuments can be properly controlled.

The armed conflict that began in the Tigray region in November 2020 raised further fears. In 2021 UNESCO noted with relief that no damage had been confirmed at the cultural sites of Aksum, though later reports spoke of violence against civilians and looting in the region, and the Committee asked for a full assessment. In 2026, at its 48th session in Busan, the Committee welcomed the report of the State Party that the majority of the monuments in the property had not been harmed during the conflict, while asking for an updated report on the state of conservation by 1 December 2027 and encouraging international support.

Visiting Aksum

Aksum is a compact town, and its main sights can be seen on foot or by a short ride. The Northern Stelae Park, the Ezana Stone, the archaeological museum, the church compound and the tombs make a rewarding itinerary of a day or two. The best months are generally the dry season, and visitors who arrive in late November will find the town transformed by the festival of Mary of Zion.

Travel to northern Ethiopia has been affected by the conflict of recent years, and conditions can change. Visitors should consult current government travel advice, arrange guides locally and respect the religious character of the churches, where modest dress is expected and some areas are closed to women or to all visitors. A good guide can add immeasurably to an understanding of the legends and the archaeology.

Reflection — Africa’s Own Antiquity

For too long the story of the ancient world has been told as if it belonged only to the Mediterranean and to Asia, with Africa below the Sahara placed at the margin. Aksum refutes that narrative with granite. Here a literate, Christian, commercial kingdom raised monuments as ambitious as any in Rome, issued coins that travelled as far as India and formulated an identity that has lasted two thousand years. To stand among the stelae is to be reminded that history has many centres.

It is also a reminder of the fragility of heritage in times of conflict. A culture that survived empires, wars and centuries of neglect now depends on the stability of the present, on boundaries drawn on maps, funds raised for conservation and a world that remembers that these stones belong to everyone. Like the stone city of Great Zimbabwe National Monument, Zimbabwe, Aksum is proof that Africa built in stone long before it was visited by outsiders.

✦ Conclusion

Aksum is a place where the scale of the past is written in stone. Its obelisks, its tombs and its sacred church testify to a kingdom that stood among the great powers of the ancient world, that gave Ethiopia its script, its faith and its sense of origin, and that continues to shape the identity of the nation. Its story is one of power and piety, decline and endurance.

The task now is to protect it. Boundaries must be mapped, unstable stones secured and the damage of conflict assessed and repaired. For those who can visit, Aksum offers an encounter with one of Africa’s deepest roots: a field of giants, a legend of the Ark and a town that still gathers each year to honour the Virgin Mary of Zion.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

What is Aksum and why is it a World Heritage Site?

Aksum is a town in the Tigray Region of northern Ethiopia and the site of the capital of the ancient Kingdom of Aksum. It was inscribed on the World Heritage List in 1980 under criteria (i) and (iv) for its monolithic stelae, royal tombs, palaces and other ruins dating from the first to the thirteenth centuries AD.

How tall are the stelae of Aksum?

The largest, the Great Stele, was about 33 metres tall and is believed to have fallen during its erection. The obelisk returned from Rome is 24.6 metres high and weighs about 170 tonnes, and other stelae reach 15 to 18 metres. All are carved from single blocks of granite.

What is the Ezana Stone?

The Ezana Stone is an inscription of the fourth-century King Ezana of Aksum, written in Ge’ez, Sabaean and Greek. It records his campaigns and reflects the conversion of the kingdom to Christianity.

Does Aksum have the Ark of the Covenant?

According to Ethiopian Orthodox tradition the Ark is kept in a chapel beside the church of Our Lady Mary of Zion, guarded by a single monk. The claim is a matter of faith and tradition, and the chapel is not open to visitors.

Is Aksum safe to visit?

Northern Ethiopia has been affected by armed conflict since 2020, and conditions can change. UNESCO has reported that the majority of monuments were not harmed, but travellers should check current government travel advice and local conditions before planning a visit.
