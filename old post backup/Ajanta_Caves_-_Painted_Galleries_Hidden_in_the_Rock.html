A Gallery Hidden in a Gorge

In the rugged hills of the Deccan plateau in western India, a river bends in a great horseshoe through a gorge of dark volcanic rock. High on the inner wall of the curve, about a hundred metres above the stream, is a row of thirty doorways, windows and pillared façades, cut directly into the cliff. These are the Ajanta Caves, a group of Buddhist monasteries and shrines that are among the greatest works of art and architecture of ancient India.

The caves were abandoned around the end of the fifth century CE and fell into obscurity, hidden by jungle and forgotten by the outside world, until a British officer chanced upon them in 1819. What he and later visitors found astonished the world: halls of carved columns and sculpted Buddhas, and, on the walls and ceilings, vast paintings of extraordinary beauty, a visual encyclopaedia of Buddhist legend and of life in ancient India.

UNESCO inscribed the Ajanta Caves in 1983, under criteria (i), (ii), (iii) and (vi), recognising them as a masterpiece of human creative genius, an influential model for Buddhist art across Asia and an exceptional testimony to a vanished tradition. They are, in the words of generations of admirers, a place where one can still sense the devotion and artistry of the monks who lived and worked here.

Why Carve a Monastery from Rock?

The idea of cutting temples and monasteries from rock has deep roots in Indian Buddhism. From the third century BCE, under the patronage of rulers and wealthy merchants, Buddhist monks began to excavate caves as places of retreat, especially in the Western Ghats, where the hard basalt could be shaped and the rainy season made shelter a necessity. Cave dwellings were durable, cool in summer and dry in the monsoon, and offered the seclusion that monks sought.

The location of Ajanta was no accident. The caves lie in a secluded valley, but not far from an important trade route that connected the ports of the west coast with the cities of the interior. Merchants and travellers passed along these roads, and they, along with local rulers, were the principal donors who paid for the excavation and decoration of the caves, commemorating their gifts in inscriptions and paintings.

Two types of cave were created. The chaitya, a prayer hall, has a long apsidal plan with a stupa, a domed reliquary, at its end, around which monks and pilgrims circumambulated in worship. The vihara is a residence, with a central hall surrounded by small cells for the monks, and later a shrine for an image of the Buddha. Both types are represented at Ajanta in outstanding examples.

The First Phase — Satavahana Caves of the Early Buddhists

The oldest group of caves at Ajanta dates from about the second century BCE to the first century CE, during the time of the Satavahana dynasty that ruled the Deccan. They include Caves 9, 10, 12, 13 and 15A, and belong to the early phase of Buddhism, often called Hinayana or Theravada, in which the Buddha was represented by symbols such as the stupa, the footprints or the empty throne, rather than in human form.

The chaitya halls of this phase, notably Caves 9 and 10, are plain and majestic, with high barrel-vaulted ceilings carved to imitate the timber ribs of wooden buildings, and rows of simple octagonal pillars leading the eye to the stupa at the far end. The carvers copied in stone the forms of earlier wooden architecture, down to beams and rafters, which reveals how closely cave architecture followed the building traditions of the time.

Cave 10 has a particularly romantic association: on one of its pillars, still visible, is the signature of John Smith, the British officer who rediscovered the caves in 1819. Some early paintings also survive in these caves, including figures of devotees and a procession of worshippers at a tree, which give a hint of the artistic traditions that preceded the great murals of the later phase.

The Second Phase — The Vakataka Golden Age

After a long pause, work at Ajanta resumed in the fifth century CE, under the patronage of the Vakataka dynasty, which ruled much of central India and was closely allied with the Gupta empire to the north. The great period of excavation and painting, between about 460 and 480 CE, was sponsored by the Vakataka emperor Harishena and his ministers and feudatories, and produced the greatest of the caves.

The ideas of Buddhism had also changed. By this period, the Mahayana tradition, with its emphasis on the figure of the Buddha, on bodhisattvas and on devotion, had become dominant, and the new caves were filled with images of the Buddha in human form, enthroned in shrines and carved on façades. Cave 19, perhaps the most beautiful of the chaitya halls, has a façade of dazzling intricacy, with Buddha figures, bodhisattvas and ornamental motifs covering every surface, and a stupa with a Buddha figure in the apse.

The viharas of this phase, Caves 1, 2, 16, 17 and others, are spacious halls with carved pillars, richly sculpted doorways and a central shrine with a seated Buddha. In Cave 26, the largest chaitya, a great reclining Buddha figure, about seven metres long, depicts the Buddha at the moment of his final passing, his disciples mourning beneath. Taken together, these caves form one of the greatest ensembles of Buddhist art.

The Paintings — A Vision of Ancient India

The glory of Ajanta lies in its paintings. On walls and ceilings of the viharas and chaityas, artists created a vast cycle of murals illustrating the Jataka tales, stories of the Buddha’s previous lives, and scenes from the life of Buddha himself. They are the largest and best-preserved body of painting from ancient India, and, apart from their religious subject, they present a panorama of the life of the time: kings and courtiers, merchants and servants, women at their toilet, hunters, musicians, elephants, monkeys and fantastic creatures.

Padmapani, Vajrapani and the Dying Princess

In Cave 1, the two great Bodhisattvas, Padmapani and Vajrapani, flank the shrine entrance, painted with a grace and sensitivity that has made them icons. Padmapani, holding a lotus, bends his head in a gesture of compassion, his dark skin and jewelled crown set against a rich background. In Cave 16, the Dying Princess depicts a young woman languishing, attended by her maids, in a scene of tender emotion. The figures are modelled with subtle shading and flowing lines, the faces expressive, the hands eloquent, and the composition teeming with detail. The paintings were made in tempera on a dry plaster of clay, lime and straw, using mineral and vegetable pigments, with brilliant reds, ochres, greens and blues, and sophisticated techniques to suggest depth and volume.

Rediscovery in 1819 and the Race to Preserve

The caves lay abandoned for more than a thousand years, shrouded by jungle and visited only by local people. In April 1819, a British officer, John Smith of the Madras Cavalry, was hunting tigers in the gorge when he saw the façade of a cave across the river. He scrambled up, entered Cave 10 and scratched his name on a pillar, a casual act of vandalism that has become a part of the cave’s history. News of the discovery spread quickly, and soon artists, antiquarians and officials were making their way to Ajanta.

In the nineteenth century, copyists were sent to record the murals. The most ambitious effort, by Major Robert Gill, produced a large series of copies, but most were destroyed in a fire at the Crystal Palace in London in 1866, a tragic loss. Meanwhile, the original paintings suffered from damp, soot, graffiti, clumsy varnishing and decay, and by the early twentieth century they were in a perilous state.

Conservation began in earnest in the 1920s, under the Nizam of Hyderabad, in whose territory the caves lay, who commissioned Italian experts to treat the murals. Later efforts by the Archaeological Survey of India have focused on stabilising the plaster, controlling humidity and limiting the damage caused by visitors. Flash photography is now forbidden, and lighting is carefully controlled, to protect the delicate pigments.

Visiting Ajanta and the Legacy of Its Art

Today the Ajanta Caves are one of the most visited heritage sites in India. The site is about a hundred kilometres from the city of Aurangabad, usually reached by road in two to three hours, and visitors walk or ride from a parking area to the cliff, where a path leads along the line of caves. Many of the caves are simply carved, but the main ones, especially Caves 1, 2, 16, 17, 19 and 26, repay careful viewing, though the interior of the painted caves can be dim, and it takes time for the eyes to adjust.

Because the original paintings are so fragile, the Archaeological Survey of India has created faithful replicas of some of the principal caves in a nearby visitor centre, allowing people to see the murals in better light and with more explanation. These reproductions are not a substitute for the real thing, but they help reduce pressure on the originals and offer a valuable introduction to the art of Ajanta.

The influence of Ajanta reaches far beyond India. The style of its sculpture and painting travelled along the Silk Roads and the sea routes of Asia, and echoes can be seen in the cave temples of Central Asia and China, in Sri Lanka and in the reliefs of Southeast Asia. Ajanta thus stands at the source of a stream of Buddhist art that shaped the visual culture of half a continent.

Reflection — The Humanity of the Divine

What strikes the visitor at Ajanta, beyond the technical skill, is the warmth of the vision. The Buddha and his earlier incarnations are portrayed not as remote and abstract but as part of a world of feeling: animals, courtiers, lovers and servants, each rendered with affection and attention. The paintings do not turn away from life; they find the sacred within it, and ask the viewer to look with compassion.

That is perhaps the cave’s greatest gift. In the dim light of a vihara, with the faces of bodhisattvas emerging from the shadows, one feels in the presence of a tradition that saw beauty and wisdom as inseparable. That the paintings have survived for fifteen centuries, in a place so remote, is a small miracle, and a reminder of how much human creativity can endure when it is given care.

✦ Conclusion

The Ajanta Caves are one of the supreme achievements of ancient Indian art: a gorge of cave monasteries and shrines, carved and painted over six centuries, in which the life of the Buddha and the life of ancient India are interwoven with extraordinary skill. Their murals are the finest surviving examples of early Indian painting, and their sculpture and architecture are landmarks of Buddhist art.

To visit them is to enter a world of quiet devotion and vivid imagination, and to feel the presence of the monks and artists who created them. Careful conservation and respectful visiting will help ensure that Ajanta continues to speak to future generations.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

Why are the Ajanta Caves a UNESCO World Heritage Site?

UNESCO inscribed them in 1983 under criteria (i), (ii), (iii) and (vi) as a masterpiece of Buddhist art, an influential model for Buddhist art across Asia and an exceptional testimony to the monastic life of ancient India.

How many caves are there and when were they made?

There are thirty caves. The earliest were excavated from about the second century BCE to the first century CE, and the second phase, which includes the most famous paintings, was made in the fifth century CE under the Vakataka dynasty.

Who rediscovered the caves?

A British officer, John Smith, came across them in 1819 while hunting. He scratched his name on a pillar in Cave 10, where it can still be seen.

Can I take photographs inside the caves?

Flash photography is prohibited to protect the paintings, and rules on photography may change, so check the regulations on arrival. Some caves are very dim, and visitors should allow time for their eyes to adjust.

What is the best way to visit Ajanta and Ellora?

Many visitors base themselves in Aurangabad and visit Ajanta and Ellora on separate days, as they lie in different directions. The caves are usually closed on one weekday, so check opening times in advance. The cooler months from October to March are the most comfortable.

🧳 Visitor's Guide

Getting There — The caves are about 100 kilometres from Aurangabad, usually reached by road in two to three hours; Aurangabad has an airport and a railway station

Best Time to Visit — October to March for cooler weather; the monsoon brings waterfalls but wet conditions

Opening Days — The caves are typically closed one day a week; check the Archaeological Survey of India for current times

Tickets — Entry is by ticket, with a higher rate for foreign visitors; buses run from the car park to the caves

Photography — Flash is forbidden inside caves; follow signs and staff instructions

Walking — There are steps and uneven paths; wear comfortable shoes and carry water

Replica Caves — The visitor centre has replicas of key caves, useful for seeing the paintings in better light

Respect the Site — Do not touch the paintings or sculptures and remove your shoes where required in shrine areas
