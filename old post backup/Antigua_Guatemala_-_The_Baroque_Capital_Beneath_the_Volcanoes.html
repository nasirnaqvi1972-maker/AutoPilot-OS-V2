A Capital Beneath the Volcanoes

There are cities that seem to have been designed by a painter. Antigua Guatemala is one of them. It lies in a broad highland valley at about fifteen hundred metres above sea level, ringed by green mountains and dominated by three great volcanoes: the Volcán de Agua, the perfectly shaped cone that rises directly above the city, and the Fuego and Acatenango pair to the west, one of them usually trailing a plume of smoke. Beneath them spreads a regular grid of cobbled streets lined with low, colourful buildings, red-tiled roofs and the carved stone façades of churches.

The beauty has a violent origin. This was once the capital of the Captaincy General of Guatemala, a Spanish colony stretching from Chiapas in southern Mexico to Costa Rica, and for more than two centuries it was one of the richest and most cultured cities of the Americas. Then, in 1773, a series of earthquakes brought down much of the city. The Spanish crown ordered the capital moved, most of the inhabitants left, and the abandoned churches, convents and palaces were left to stand as ruins among the surviving streets. Their dignified decay is what visitors now come to see.

UNESCO inscribed Antigua Guatemala in 1979 as one of the earliest and most outstanding examples of colonial city planning in Latin America, and as a city whose Baroque monuments, preserved as ruins, record the history of an entire age. This post follows the city from its founding through its golden age and its destruction to its present life as a living heritage town in an earthquake-prone land.

A City Founded Three Times

The Spanish city of Santiago de Guatemala was founded in 1524 by the conquistador Pedro de Alvarado, but its early years were unsettled. The first settlement near the Kaqchikel Maya capital of Iximché was soon abandoned, and the Spanish moved to a site at Almolonga, now Ciudad Vieja, in 1527. In 1541 a catastrophic flood of water and mud from the Volcán de Agua destroyed that settlement, and the Spanish decided to start again in a safer place.

In 1543 they chose the Valley of Panchoy, a few kilometres to the east, and laid out the new capital on a grid of straight streets around a central square. The name Santiago de los Caballeros de Guatemala was kept, and the city soon became the seat of the Audiencia, the royal court that governed the region. In 1566 King Philip II granted it the title of very noble and very loyal city. The place that exists today is the product of this third foundation.

The Grid and the Volcanoes

UNESCO gives special importance to the layout of the city. Antigua is described as one of the earliest and most outstanding examples of city planning in Latin America, and its basic grid plan, dating from 1543, has been maintained to the present day. The streets run at right angles around the Plaza Mayor, where the cathedral, the governor’s palace and the town hall face one another across an open square, and the blocks beyond are filled with religious complexes, houses and gardens.

The plan followed the principles of Spanish colonial urbanism, which sought to impose order and hierarchy on the new world, but the setting made it unique. At every intersection the eye is drawn upward to the volcanoes that frame the streets, a reminder that the city was built in a place of both great fertility and great danger. The same volcanic soil that made the valley rich also made it vulnerable.

Capital of a Kingdom

For more than two centuries Antigua was the administrative, religious and cultural centre of Central America. The Captain General governed from the palace on the Plaza Mayor, the bishop presided over the cathedral, and the great religious orders, the Franciscans, Dominicans, Mercedarians, Jesuits and others, built monasteries and schools that filled the city. The University of San Carlos was founded here in 1676, and printing presses produced books and pamphlets in a city of scholars, lawyers and clerics.

The city’s wealth came from the surrounding land, from the labour of Indigenous people and from the products of the colony, such as cacao, indigo and cochineal. Its artistic life flourished, producing sculptors, painters, silversmiths and architects whose work spread through the region. At its height in the eighteenth century, the capital was a place of sophisticated churches, elaborate processions and learned conversation.

Earthquake Baroque: Architecture Built to Survive

The architecture of Antigua is Spanish Baroque adapted to a seismic landscape. Builders learned from experience that tall towers and slender walls collapsed in earthquakes, and they responded by building low, with very thick walls, massive buttresses and squat towers. The result is a distinctive style sometimes called earthquake Baroque, in which the exuberant ornament of the Baroque is applied to façades of unusual solidity.

The style combines Spanish, Indigenous and local influences. Stucco decoration, often richly sculpted with columns, niches, vines and saints, covers the fronts of the churches in elaborate patterns, while the interiors were once filled with gilded altarpieces. UNESCO recognises the city as containing living traces of Spanish culture in its principal monuments, built in the Baroque style of the eighteenth century and preserved today as ruins.

Churches and Convents: A City of Ruins and Prayer

The religious monuments are the glory of Antigua. The Church of La Merced, with its golden-yellow façade covered in intricate stucco, is one of the most photographed buildings in Central America, and it remains in use. The Church of San Francisco is the resting place of Hermano Pedro de San José de Betancur, a seventeenth-century Franciscan renowned for his care of the poor and the sick, who was canonised in 2002 and is venerated as Guatemala’s first saint.

Elsewhere the ruins tell the story of 1773. The great cathedral, once the grandest church in the region, survives only in part, its soaring vaults and columns open to the sky. The convent of Las Capuchinas, with its circular tower of nuns’ cells, the Jesuit college, the Dominican complex of Santo Domingo and many others are in various stages of ruin and restoration. Walking among them one sees both the ambition of the colonial church and the power of the earthquakes that brought it down.

The Plaza Mayor, the Palace and the Arch

At the centre of the city, the Plaza Mayor remains the stage of daily life. On its north side stands the Palace of the Captains General, a long arcaded building with a double row of arches that once housed the colonial government and now holds museums and offices. Opposite it is the cathedral complex, and on the other sides are the arcades of the town hall and shops.

The most beloved landmark of the city is the Arco de Santa Catalina, a yellow arch with a clock tower that spans a street near the convent of Santa Catalina. Originally built in the seventeenth century so that nuns could cross between the convent and its school without being seen in the street, it frames the Volcán de Agua in one of the most famous views in the country. The city’s scale is human and walkable, and the views repay unhurried exploration.

1773: The Year the Capital Fell

The city had survived earthquakes before, and a severe one in 1717 had already caused major damage. But in the middle of 1773 a series of powerful tremors, known as the Santa Marta earthquakes, struck Antigua repeatedly over several months and destroyed a large part of the city. The authorities, convinced that the site was too dangerous, decided to move the capital to a new location, and the new city of Guatemala, the ancestor of present-day Guatemala City, was founded in 1776.

Many inhabitants resisted the move, and some refused to leave their homes, but the official institutions and most of the wealthy families departed. The abandoned city was now called La Antigua Guatemala, the old Guatemala, the name it still bears. UNESCO notes that the relocation of the capital and the abandonment of the area by most of its population permitted the preservation of many of its monumental Baroque-style buildings as ruins, an unintended consequence of catastrophe.

What UNESCO Recognised: Criteria (ii), (iii) and (iv)

The 1979 inscription was based on three criteria. Under criterion (ii), UNESCO recognised Antigua as containing living traces of Spanish culture in its Baroque monuments, now preserved as ruins. Under criterion (iii), it recognised one of the earliest and most outstanding examples of city planning in Latin America, with a grid plan that has been maintained since 1543. Under criterion (iv), it recognised the many churches and monasteries as a testimony to the influence of the Christian church on every aspect of daily life during the colonial period.

UNESCO also notes that Antigua has retained the integrity of its sixteenth-century layout and the physical integrity of most of its built heritage. The property is recorded as a serial site with nine components, including not only the central city but also nearby places such as Ciudad Vieja, the site of the earlier capital, and several surrounding villages and estates, which together form a historical landscape around the colonial centre.

Living Traditions: Holy Week in Antigua

Antigua is not a museum. Its greatest annual event, Semana Santa or Holy Week, fills the streets with some of the most impressive religious processions in the Americas. Huge wooden floats bearing images of Christ and the Virgin, carried on the shoulders of dozens of bearers, move slowly through the streets accompanied by incense, music and penitents in purple robes. The processions are a form of living heritage that continues the colonial tradition.

Before each procession, residents create alfombras, temporary carpets of coloured sawdust, flowers and pine needles laid out in elaborate patterns on the cobbles, which the floats then trample as they pass. The ephemeral art takes hours to make and is destroyed in minutes, a gesture of devotion that is as moving as it is beautiful. Holy Week draws pilgrims and visitors from all over the world and is the most crowded and vibrant moment of the city’s year.

Conservation: Earthquakes, Traffic and Unfinished Boundaries

Protecting Antigua has never been easy. Earthquakes remain the greatest threat: the Guatemala earthquake of 1976 and another in January 2001 aggravated damage to ruined monuments and increased the danger of collapse of some structures. UNESCO has long urged a comprehensive plan for the conservation of the city, covering traffic, tourism and housing, and has stressed the need to deal with ruins that might collapse in a future earthquake.

The property has also lacked a formally defined buffer zone, an issue that the World Heritage Committee has raised for years, and a debate over a shopping centre in the early 2000s showed how development pressure can threaten the setting. The city is protected by national law and by a national council for its protection, and recent work has sought to integrate natural and intangible heritage, such as the surrounding landscape and the biological corridors, into management. The nearby volcanoes remain active, and the deadly eruption of the Volcán de Fuego in 2018 was a reminder of the forces that shape this landscape.

Visiting Antigua

Antigua is one of the most popular destinations in Guatemala, and it is easy to see why. The city is compact and walkable, with museums, cafés, markets and hotels in restored colonial houses, and it is a short journey from Guatemala City. Many visitors come for the architecture, others to study Spanish in the city’s language schools, and others to climb the nearby volcanoes, including the active Pacaya and the challenging ascent of Acatenango, from which the eruptions of Fuego can be watched at night.

The dry season, from roughly November to April, offers the clearest skies, while Holy Week in March or April is spectacular but crowded and requires early booking. Visitors should wear comfortable shoes for the cobbles, be mindful of the altitude and follow local guidance on volcano hikes and safety. The colonial centre is generally welcoming, but as in any busy destination it is wise to take ordinary precautions.

Reflection — Beauty Built on Fault Lines

Antigua teaches a paradoxical lesson: that destruction can be a form of preservation. Had the earthquakes of 1773 not ended the city’s time as capital, its Baroque monuments would probably have been rebuilt, renovated and replaced as fashions and fortunes changed, in the way that most colonial centres were. It was abandonment that froze them in time, and it is the ruins, ironically, that give the city its unique poetry.

There is also a lesson about living with risk. The people of Antigua have rebuilt their houses, restored their churches and resumed their processions through centuries of tremors and eruptions, and the city’s architecture itself is a record of adaptation. In this it resembles other Spanish colonial capitals of the Americas, such as Historic Centre of Mexico City and Xochimilco, Mexico and Old Havana, Cuba, but none has been shaped so profoundly by the instability of the ground beneath it.

✦ Conclusion

Antigua Guatemala is a city that has been destroyed and rebuilt many times and has never lost its character. Its grid of 1543, its earthquake Baroque churches and its roofless cathedrals tell the story of a colonial capital at the height of its power and of the natural forces that brought it down. Its Holy Week processions and its daily life show that the heritage is alive.

To visit is to walk through layers of history in the shadow of the volcanoes: Maya, Spanish, colonial, republican and modern. The city asks for care in return, in the form of respect for its ruins, support for its conservation and an awareness that its beauty is inseparable from the fragility of the land on which it stands.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

Why is Antigua Guatemala a UNESCO World Heritage Site?

It was inscribed in 1979 under criteria (ii), (iii) and (iv) because it preserves the Baroque monuments of a Spanish colonial capital as ruins, retains one of the earliest and most outstanding grid plans in Latin America from 1543, and demonstrates the influence of the Christian church in its many churches and monasteries.

What happened to Antigua in 1773?

A series of earthquakes known as the Santa Marta earthquakes destroyed much of the city. The Spanish authorities decided to move the capital, and a new city, later Guatemala City, was founded in 1776. Most inhabitants left, and the abandoned monuments survived as ruins.

What is earthquake Baroque?

It is the local variant of Spanish Baroque architecture, adapted to withstand earthquakes with low profiles, very thick walls, strong buttresses and squat towers, while retaining richly decorated stucco façades.

When is the best time to visit Antigua?

The dry season, roughly November to April, has the clearest weather. Holy Week, in March or April, is the most spectacular time but is also very crowded, and accommodation should be booked well in advance.

What are the main sights in Antigua?

Highlights include the Plaza Mayor and the Palace of the Captains General, the cathedral ruins, La Merced, San Francisco and the tomb of Hermano Pedro, the convent of Las Capuchinas, the Arco de Santa Catalina and nearby volcano hikes such as Pacaya and Acatenango.
