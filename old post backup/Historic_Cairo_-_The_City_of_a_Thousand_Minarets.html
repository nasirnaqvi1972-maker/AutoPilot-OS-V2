Al-Qahira, the Victorious

Cairo is not a city that discloses itself at a glance. It is vast, loud and overwhelming, home to more than twenty million people in its greater metropolitan area, its traffic thick and its air hazed with dust. Yet at its heart, on the east bank of the Nile, lies a district of extraordinary density and beauty, where medieval streets wind between mosques and madrasas, where minarets of different centuries crowd the skyline, and where the call to prayer rises from a hundred towers at once. This is Historic Cairo, also known as Islamic Cairo, one of the great urban monuments of the world.

The district covers a little more than five hundred hectares and holds hundreds of mosques, tombs, madrasas, mansions, caravanserais, gates and fortifications that span the whole of the Islamic era in Egypt, from the seventh century to the nineteenth. It is one of the largest and most concentrated collections of historic Islamic architecture on earth, and, unlike many museum cities, it is still alive: people pray in its mosques, trade in its markets and raise their children in its alleys.

UNESCO inscribed Historic Cairo on the World Heritage List in 1979 under criteria (i), (v) and (vi), recognising it as a masterpiece of human creative genius, an outstanding example of a traditional human settlement and a place directly associated with ideas of universal significance. The inscription describes one of the world's oldest Islamic cities, with its famous mosques, madrasas, hammams and fountains, and the new centre of the Islamic world, reaching its golden age in the fourteenth century.

Four Cities in One

Cairo as it is known today grew from a series of successive capitals, each built beside the last. The first was Fustat, founded in 641 by the Arab general Amr ibn al-As after the conquest of Egypt, on the east bank of the Nile near the old Roman fortress of Babylon. It became the first Muslim capital of Egypt and the home of its first great mosque, and little of it survives above ground, but its memory is preserved in the name of the modern district of Old Cairo.

In 870, the Abbasid governor Ahmad ibn Tulun founded a new, grander city to the north-east, called al-Qata'i, and built there, between 876 and 879, the great mosque that bears his name. The Mosque of Ibn Tulun is one of the most impressive early Islamic monuments in the world: a vast open courtyard surrounded by pointed arcades of brick and stucco, with a distinctive spiral minaret reminiscent of the ninth-century architecture of Samarra, the Abbasid capital in Iraq. It is the oldest mosque in Cairo to retain its original form.

The city that gave Cairo its name came a century later. In 969, the Fatimid general Jawhar al-Siqilli, acting for the caliph al-Mu'izz, founded a new palace-city to the north of Fustat, which was called al-Qahira, meaning the Victorious. Tradition says that the name was chosen because the planet Mars, known in Arabic as al-Qahir, was rising at the moment the foundations were laid. The new city was at first a royal enclave, closed to ordinary people, but it expanded rapidly and eventually swallowed its predecessors, becoming the Cairo of today.

Fatimid Cairo — Gates and Scholars

The Fatimids, an Ismaili Shiite dynasty that ruled much of North Africa and the Middle East from Egypt between 969 and 1171, left their mark on the city in stone. Their enclosure was ringed with walls and gates, three of which survive: Bab al-Futuh and Bab al-Nasr in the north, built in 1087 by the vizier Badr al-Jamali, and Bab Zuwayla in the south, completed in 1092. These monumental gates, with their massive stone towers, rounded bastions and fine carving, are among the finest examples of medieval military architecture in the Islamic world.

Between them runs the principal street of the old city, now known as Al-Muizz Street, a long spine lined with mosques, markets and palaces from several centuries. Along it stand the Mosque of al-Hakim, with its imposing minarets, and countless monuments of later periods, and in recent decades it has been extensively restored, creating one of the greatest open-air museums of Islamic architecture in the world.

The Fatimids also founded the Mosque of al-Azhar, begun in 970 and completed in 972, which grew into a centre of religious teaching and later became one of the oldest universities in the world. Al-Azhar remains today among the most prestigious institutions of Islamic learning, and its courtyard, with minarets and arcades added by successive rulers, is a living archive of Cairo's architectural history.

Saladin and the Citadel

In 1171, the Kurdish commander Salah al-Din, known to the West as Saladin, ended Fatimid rule and restored Sunni Islam in Egypt, founding the Ayyubid dynasty. He understood the strategic importance of Cairo and set about strengthening its defences, planning a great wall to enclose both Cairo and Fustat and, in 1176, beginning the construction of a mighty fortress on a rocky spur of the Muqattam Hills overlooking the city.

The Citadel of Cairo, completed under his successors, became the seat of government and the royal residence for about seven hundred years, from the Ayyubids through the Mamluks and the Ottomans to the dynasty of Muhammad Ali in the nineteenth century. Its walls and towers, built of the same limestone as the pyramids, are a formidable sight, and the complex contains palaces, mosques, barracks and museums from many eras.

Dominating the skyline of Cairo today is the Mosque of Muhammad Ali, built between 1830 and 1848 in the Ottoman style, with a great central dome and two slender minarets. Its silhouette, inspired by the mosques of Istanbul, is the best-known image of the city, and the terrace of the Citadel offers a sweeping view across the roofs of Cairo to the pyramids of Giza on the western horizon.

The Mamluk Golden Age

The Mamluks, a dynasty of former slave-soldiers who seized power in 1250 and ruled until the Ottoman conquest of 1517, presided over what UNESCO calls the golden age of Cairo. Under their patronage, the city became the greatest in the Islamic world, a centre of trade, scholarship and art, and a place of extraordinary architectural creativity. Sultans and emirs competed in raising great complexes combining mosque, madrasa, mausoleum and sometimes hospital or fountain, built of finely cut stone and decorated with geometric patterns and calligraphy.

The most celebrated is the Mosque-Madrasa of Sultan Hasan, built between 1356 and 1363, near the Citadel. Its enormous portal rises to a height of some thirty-six metres, its minaret soars above the neighbouring streets, and its interior, with a vast vaulted courtyard and four great iwans, is a space of astonishing grandeur. It is widely regarded as one of the supreme monuments of Islamic architecture.

Other great Mamluk works include the complex of Sultan Qalawun, built in 1284 and 1285 and containing a hospital, a madrasa and a mausoleum, and the exquisite funerary mosque of Sultan Qaitbay, whose carved stone dome in the northern cemetery is among the loveliest in Cairo. Their intricate stone domes, with their carved arabesques, and the varied minarets that rise from them, give Cairo the skyline that made it famous as the City of a Thousand Minarets.

The City of the Dead

On the eastern and southern edges of the historic city lie the great cemeteries, known collectively as the Qarafa, or popularly as the City of the Dead. They are not simple graveyards but entire districts of tombs, mausoleums, mosques and funerary complexes built by Mamluk sultans and emirs, as well as holy men and scholars, among them the mausoleum of Imam al-Shafi'i, the founder of one of the four main schools of Sunni law, who died in Egypt in 820.

What makes the Qarafa extraordinary is that it is lived in. For centuries, families have made their homes among the tombs, caretakers and craftsmen, and in modern times the population has grown until whole neighbourhoods, with shops, schools and mosques, now occupy the cemeteries. The domes of Mamluk mausoleums rise above laundry lines and satellite dishes, and children play football among the tombs.

The cemeteries are also among the most important sites for the study of Mamluk architecture, since they contain monuments that have survived better than many in the crowded centre. Restoration projects, undertaken in recent decades with the support of national and international bodies, have begun to repair the damaged domes and minarets, and visitors who venture into the Northern Cemetery find themselves in a world that is at once ancient and unexpectedly vital.

Markets, Neighbours and Old Cairo

The medieval city was always a place of trade, and its markets remain at the heart of its life. Khan el-Khalili, the great bazaar founded in the fourteenth century on the site of a Fatimid mausoleum, is a maze of narrow lanes packed with merchants selling lanterns, spices, brass, perfume and textiles, and the nearby al-Hussein Mosque and the old coffeehouses are gathering places for Cairenes and visitors alike.

South of the medieval core lies Old Cairo, the district of Fustat and the Roman fortress of Babylon, where the Coptic Christian community has worshipped for centuries. The Hanging Church and the other ancient churches within the fortress walls, the Ben Ezra Synagogue, where the famous Geniza documents were found, and the Nilometer on Roda Island, built in 861, are often visited together with the Islamic monuments, and together they show that Cairo has always been a city of many faiths.

This diversity is a part of the city's identity. The Muslim, Christian and Jewish communities of Cairo lived side by side for more than a thousand years, trading, studying and worshipping in neighbouring streets, and the physical record of that coexistence is preserved in the stone of the old city.

Visiting and Preserving Historic Cairo

Historic Cairo is a challenging and rewarding place to visit. The most convenient way to explore it is on foot, along Al-Muizz Street and through the lanes of Khan el-Khalili, with taxis or ride-hailing services to reach the Citadel, the Sultan Hasan complex and the cemeteries. The best season is from October to April, when temperatures are mild; summers are extremely hot. Visitors should dress modestly, remove their shoes when entering mosques and avoid visiting during Friday midday prayers.

The district faces serious pressures. The 1992 earthquake damaged many monuments, and pollution, groundwater, traffic, overcrowding, neglect and unregulated building have taken a continuing toll. Restoration has been undertaken by the Egyptian authorities and by international partners, among them the Aga Khan Trust for Culture, which transformed a rubbish dump into the Al-Azhar Park, opened in 2005, and led the rehabilitation of the neighbouring Darb al-Ahmar district.

Travellers should check current government advisories and local conditions before visiting Egypt, take sensible precautions in crowded areas and consider hiring a licensed local guide, whose knowledge adds immensely to the experience of a city in which every wall has a story.

Reflection

To climb to the terrace of the Citadel in the late afternoon, as the light turns the dust of Cairo to gold and the calls to prayer begin to rise from the minarets below, is to feel the weight of a thousand years of history pressing against the present. The city is not a museum; it is a living, breathing organism whose past is woven into every street. The builders who raised these domes and gates were not thinking of posterity. They were thinking of God, of power, of learning and of commerce. That their work should have survived, battered and beloved, into the age of the smartphone is a remarkable testimony to the stubbornness of beauty.

✦ Conclusion

Historic Cairo is among the greatest urban monuments of Islam, a place where more than a thousand years of faith, power and craftsmanship are written in stone. Its gates, mosques, madrasas and tombs show the evolution of a style, and its streets show the continuity of a community that has lived among these monuments for generations.

It is also a city under pressure, and its survival is far from guaranteed. The challenges of pollution, population and neglect are real, but so is the commitment of those who work to protect and restore it. To walk in Islamic Cairo is to enter a world in which the past is not remote but present, and to understand why this crowded, noisy, magnificent city has been called the mother of the world.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

1. What is Historic Cairo?

It is the medieval core of the Egyptian capital, also called Islamic Cairo, containing hundreds of mosques, madrasas, tombs, gates and houses built between the seventh and nineteenth centuries.

2. When was it inscribed on the World Heritage List?

It was inscribed in 1979 under criteria (i), (v) and (vi).

3. Who founded Cairo?

The Fatimid general Jawhar al-Siqilli founded the city of al-Qahira in 969 for the caliph al-Mu'izz. It grew beside earlier capitals, including Fustat and al-Qata'i.

4. What are the main monuments to see?

The Citadel and Muhammad Ali Mosque, the Mosques of Ibn Tulun, Sultan Hasan and al-Azhar, the gates of Bab Zuwayla and Bab al-Futuh, Al-Muizz Street, Khan el-Khalili and the cemeteries.

5. When is the best time to visit?

October to April is the most comfortable period. Dress modestly and avoid mosque visits during Friday midday prayers.
