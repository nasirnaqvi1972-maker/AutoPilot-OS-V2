The Elephant That Stood Up

There is a famous image from Mana Pools: a huge bull elephant balanced on his hind legs, front feet braced against the trunk of a tree, trunk stretched upward to reach the pods in the branches. He holds the pose, swaying slightly, for as long as it takes, and then drops back onto four feet and shuffles off with his prize. The scene takes place in the dry season, in the shade of the winterthorn trees that line the Zambezi floodplain, and is one of the best-loved spectacles in African wildlife.

Mana Pools National Park lies on the middle Zambezi in northern Zimbabwe, where the river sweeps along a broad floodplain beneath the steep wall of the Zambian escarpment. With the neighbouring Sapi and Chewore Safari Areas, it forms a World Heritage Site of 676,600 hectares, one of the largest and wildest conservation areas in southern Africa. The name comes from the Shona word for four, mana, which refers to the four large pools left behind by the wandering river.

UNESCO inscribed the property in 1984 and recognises it under natural criteria (vii), (ix) and (x). The first praises the annual gathering of animals in the riverside parkland as one of Africa's outstanding wildlife spectacles, the second the ecological processes of the floodplain, and the third the importance of the habitat for threatened species. It is a place that rewards slowness, silence and attention.

A River's Playground

The Zambezi is Africa's fourth-longest river, and in its middle reaches, downstream from the great dam at Kariba, it flows in wide curves across a flat valley floor between two escarpments. Over thousands of years the river has shifted back and forth across this plain, leaving behind a patchwork of old channels, sandbanks and islands, and the four pools that give Mana Pools its name are former river courses that have been cut off and have held water ever since.

The alluvial plain within the park covers about sixty square kilometres, and its constantly changing character is the key to the park's ecology. Each year the river deposits fresh sediment, creating new sandbars and eroding old ones, and the vegetation follows, in a mosaic of grassland, thicket and woodland at different stages of growth. UNESCO's criterion (ix) recognises this ongoing process of ecological succession.

Behind the floodplain, the land rises in terraces to the foot of the Zambezi escarpment, about fifty kilometres to the south, with woodland types changing along the way: riverine forest on the sand, mopane on the clay soils, mixed woodland on the slopes and miombo on the hills. The variety of habitats supports an unusually rich community of animals, and the park forms a continuous protected area with Zambia's Lower Zambezi National Park on the opposite bank.

The Winterthorn Woodland

The tree that defines Mana Pools is the winterthorn, Faidherbia albida, also known as the apple-ring acacia. It has a peculiar and useful habit: it drops its leaves in the rainy season and comes into leaf in the dry season, reversing the usual rhythm of African trees. At the height of the dry season, when almost everything else is brown and bare, the winterthorns are green, and their branches carry crops of nutritious pods that fall to the ground and feed the wildlife.

The great groves of winterthorn along the river create a park-like landscape, with tall trees casting deep shade above a floor of open grass and leaf litter. Beneath them, in the dry months, animals gather in great numbers, and the pods are a staple food of elephants, buffalo, impala, kudu, waterbuck and baboons. The elephants' technique of rearing up to reach the high branches, and of shaking the trees with their foreheads, is a regular sight.

Other trees add to the character of the woodland. Huge mahoganies, ana trees and sausage trees stand along the river, and the dense thickets known as jesse grow on deep sands, providing cover for shy species such as bushbuck and nyala. The vegetation is dynamic, shaped by the elephants that feed on it, and is a constant reminder that in an African wilderness, every animal is also a landscape architect.

Elephants, Buffalo and the Great Gathering

Each dry season, from about June to October, as the pans inland dry up, the animals converge on the river. Elephants come in their hundreds, family herds led by old matriarchs, along with large groups of buffalo, zebra, eland, impala, kudu and waterbuck. The Zambezi itself holds some of the largest concentrations of hippos and Nile crocodiles in Africa, and every sandbank seems to support a pod of the former or a row of the latter.

Predators follow the herds. Lions, leopards and spotted hyenas hunt along the floodplain, and cheetahs have been recorded in the area. Mana Pools is also one of the best places in Africa to see African wild dogs, or painted dogs, which are among the continent's most endangered carnivores. Packs of these remarkable, mottled hunters range across the park and have been the subject of long-term research and conservation work. An isolated northern population of nyala, a handsome spiral-horned antelope, lives in the thickets.

The birdlife is equally rich. Carmine bee-eaters nest in colonies in the riverbanks, fish eagles call from the treetops, and herons, storks, kingfishers and skimmers work the sandbanks and pools. Over four hundred species have been recorded in the park, making it an important bird area, and the combination of birds, mammals and landscape gives every day on the floodplain its own character.

On Foot Among Giants

What sets Mana Pools apart from most African parks is that visitors are allowed to walk. In many reserves, safari-goers are confined to vehicles, but here, accompanied by licensed guides trained in tracking and in the behaviour of dangerous animals, they may travel on foot across the floodplain. The experience is intense and humbling: with no vehicle between you and the elephant, the scale of the animals and the detail of the landscape become vivid in a way that a jeep cannot match.

The guides read the signs: the direction of the breeze, the flick of an ear, the position of a calf, the spacing of a herd. They teach visitors to move quietly, to keep a safe distance and to respect the animals' space, and the elephants, which have been used to walkers for decades, are often remarkably tolerant, feeding calmly within sight of a small group. It is a privilege that depends on a high standard of conduct, and the guides are careful to preserve it.

Other ways of exploring are canoeing on the Zambezi, a quiet passage along channels past hippos, crocodiles and elephants drinking on the bank, and game drives on the park's tracks. Each offers a different view of the same wild community, and all require guides or considerable experience, since the animals are fully wild and the river is dangerous.

Sapi and Chewore — The Wild South

Behind the river lie the Sapi and Chewore Safari Areas, which together make up most of the World Heritage Site. They are rugged, little-visited regions of hills, gorges and woodland, stretching south from the Zambezi toward the escarpment, with almost no roads and few visitors. Chewore, the larger, is particularly broken and inaccessible, with deep gorges cut by seasonal rivers, and it shelters populations of elephant, buffalo, lion, leopard and other species that range freely between the safari areas and the national park.

Unlike the national park, the safari areas allow controlled hunting under quota, a use that has long been debated but that the management argues helps to fund anti-poaching and to give local people a stake in conservation. The area was once also a stronghold of the black rhinoceros, which was hunted to local extinction in the 1980s and 1990s, and which is currently absent. The habitat remains suitable, and its return in secure conditions is a long-term aspiration.

Together, the three areas form a large, relatively intact ecological unit, adequate in size for natural processes to continue, and contiguous with other protected areas in Zimbabwe and Zambia. In an age of shrinking wilderness, their value as a stronghold of biodiversity is immense.

Pressures on a Wild Place

Mana Pools is not without its difficulties. Poaching, particularly of elephants for ivory, has been a persistent threat across Zimbabwe, and the park has seen its share of illegal killing. Funding for management has often been inadequate, and conservation partnerships with international organisations have played an important role in supporting patrols, aerial surveys and research.

Water is a more fundamental concern. The Zambezi is among the most heavily developed rivers in Africa, and changes to its flow, whether through dams upstream or proposals for new schemes along the middle valley, could affect the delicate balance of the floodplain. UNESCO's advisers have long drawn attention to the risks of developments that would alter the river's natural regime, and the site's integrity depends on keeping the Zambezi alive.

Climate change adds to the uncertainty, with severe droughts reducing the food available to wildlife. In 2019, a drought so severe that animals were dying led to the controversial decision to supply hay bales to the elephants, prompting a debate about the limits of intervention in wild ecosystems. The park was designated a Ramsar wetland of international importance in 2013, a recognition of its role in the regional hydrology.

Visiting Mana Pools

The park lies in the Zambezi Valley, about 380 kilometres north of Harare, reached by a long drive through Karoi and Makuti to the park headquarters at Marongora, and then by a rough track down the escarpment to the river. The route requires a high-clearance four-wheel-drive vehicle, though many visitors prefer to fly in to one of the airstrips or to travel with a safari operator.

The best time to visit is the dry season, from about May to October, when the animals concentrate along the river and the vegetation is open. The park is typically closed during the rainy season, from about November to April, when the roads become impassable. Accommodation includes the public camp at Nyamepi, run by the national parks authority, and a small number of private lodges and temporary camps, and visitors should book well in advance.

The environment is wild and demanding. Visitors should take malaria precautions, protect themselves against the sun and tsetse flies, and carry adequate fuel, food and water. Walking safaris should be undertaken only with licensed guides. Travellers should also check current travel advisories and conditions in Zimbabwe, and confirm access and permit requirements with the parks authority or an operator before departure.

Reflection

At the end of a dry-season day on the Zambezi, as the light turns copper and the elephants drift in silhouette between the winterthorns, one understands that Mana Pools is less a place than a rhythm. The river moves, the pools shrink, the herds arrive and the pods fall, and the cycle has repeated itself for longer than anyone has counted. To walk through it, with nothing between you and the vastness of the valley, is to be reminded that human beings are visitors in a world that works perfectly well without us, and that the greatest privilege of a wild place is simply to be allowed to watch it.

✦ Conclusion

Mana Pools National Park, with the Sapi and Chewore Safari Areas, is one of Africa's great wildernesses, a riverine ecosystem of exceptional beauty and richness. Its elephants and wild dogs, its winterthorn woodlands and its shifting pools make it a place of constant interest, and its tradition of walking safaris offers an intimacy with wildlife that is increasingly rare.

It is also a fragile place, dependent on a living river, on adequate protection and on careful management of tourism. To visit it is a privilege, and to protect it is a responsibility that extends well beyond Zimbabwe's borders.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

1. Where is Mana Pools National Park?

It lies in the middle Zambezi Valley in northern Zimbabwe, on the border with Zambia, about 380 kilometres from Harare.

2. Why is it a World Heritage Site?

It was inscribed in 1984 under natural criteria (vii), (ix) and (x) for its outstanding wildlife spectacle, its ecological processes and its importance for biodiversity.

3. Can I walk in Mana Pools?

Yes. Walking safaris with licensed guides are one of the park's distinguishing features. Walk only with an authorised guide.

4. What is the best time to visit?

The dry season, from about May to October, is best for wildlife. The park is usually closed in the rainy season.

5. What does the name mean?

Mana means four in Shona, referring to the four large permanent pools that are old channels of the Zambezi.
