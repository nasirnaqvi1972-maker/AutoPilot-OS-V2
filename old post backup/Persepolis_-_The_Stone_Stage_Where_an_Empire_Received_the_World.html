Persepolis — The Stone Stage Where an Empire Received the World

A Vast Half-Natural, Half-Artificial Terrace at the Foot of Kuh-e Rahmat, Raised from About 518 BCE by Darius the Great and His Successors as the Ceremonial Showplace of the Achaemenid Empire, Where Stairways, Winged Bulls and Forests of Columns Still Speak of Kingship | UNESCO World Heritage Site

A Platform Raised Between Mountain and Plain

Approach Persepolis from the plain of Marvdasht and the first thing the eye registers is not a building at all but a great pale shelf of stone, laid against the flank of a brown mountain as if the hill itself had been asked to hold up a palace. The shelf is the terrace, and everything the Achaemenid kings wished to say about themselves was written upon it. It is half natural and half artificial, cut from the living rock on one side and built up with enormous masonry on the other, and its surface covers some twelve and a half hectares. Above it, in the clear light of Fars, stand the columns that have become the emblem of ancient Iran.

What strikes the visitor, once the first impression has settled, is how little of Persepolis was ever meant to be lived in. This was not a fortress, nor an ordinary capital with markets, barracks and law courts, but a stage. The architecture is arranged as a sequence of approaches, thresholds and audiences, so that a person climbing from the plain would be slowed, awed and sorted by rank before ever reaching the presence of the king. UNESCO describes the ensemble as a showplace for royal receptions and festivals, and the phrase is exact. Persepolis was built to be seen, and to be seen in a particular order.

Parsa, the City of the Persians

The Greeks called it Persepolis, the city of the Persians, but the people who built it knew the region as Parsa, the homeland from which their dynasty had sprung. Cyrus the Great had founded the empire in the middle of the sixth century BCE, and his capital at Pasargadae, a short distance to the north-east, still stands as a monument to that first conquering generation. It was Darius I, the king who seized the throne in 522 BCE and then spent his reign consolidating a realm that stretched from the Aegean coast to the Indus, who chose a new site on the plain of Marvdasht and began a new kind of capital.

The date usually given for the beginning of work on the terrace is about 518 BCE, with the earliest surviving remains belonging to the following years, and the building programme was by no means finished in his lifetime. Darius laid out the platform, the great stairway and the Apadana, his audience hall. His son Xerxes completed the Apadana, raised the Gate of All Nations and began the vast Hall of a Hundred Columns, while Artaxerxes I finished the work that his father had begun. Palace after palace was added across the following generations, so that the terrace became a layered record of successive reigns, each king leaving his name and his inscriptions in stone.

Why build here at all, when Susa and Babylon already served as administrative centres of the empire? The likeliest answer is that Persepolis was a statement of identity. Babylon was a city of the conquered; Susa was a city inherited. Persepolis was Persian ground, the dynasty's own land, and it was designed to express an ideology of kingship in which many peoples stood beneath one ruler. Those who have walked the great Mesopotamian capital of Babylon, Iraq will recognise how consciously the Achaemenids borrowed from older traditions of power, and how decisively they reworked them into something new.

The Stairway and the Gate of All Nations

The visitor of the ancient world did not simply walk onto the terrace; he ascended to it. A monumental double stairway of shallow, broad steps, designed it is said so that horses and robed courtiers could climb in dignity, rises from the plain and turns back upon itself to reach the upper level. The steps are low enough to be taken at a stately pace, which is perhaps the point. Nobody arrived at the king in a hurry, and the climb itself was the first lesson in hierarchy, an unhurried procession from the ordinary world into the ordered one.

At the head of the stair stands the Gate of All Nations, a square hall of about twenty-five metres on each side, with four columns supporting its roof. It takes its name from inscriptions attributed to Xerxes, who boasted that he had built it, and it was the point at which the delegations of the empire were gathered before they were admitted onward. Its western and eastern doorways were guarded by colossal bulls with human heads, composite creatures borrowed from the visual language of Assyria and transformed into guardians of a Persian threshold. The old Mesopotamian bull-men are here made to serve a new and larger sovereignty.

The Apadana and the Procession of Peoples

The greatest hall of the terrace was the Apadana, the audience hall begun by Darius and completed under Xerxes. Its platform is approached by two monumental stairways, and it is on the walls of these stairways that the most famous work of Persepolis was carved. Here, in shallow and exquisitely controlled relief, the peoples of the empire climb toward the king bearing gifts. There are twenty-three delegations in all, divided from one another by little cypress trees, each led by a Persian or a Mede and each carrying the characteristic treasure of its homeland: horses, camels, vessels of gold, textiles, wild animals, and even a jar decorated with winged griffins carried by an Armenian delegate.

The frieze is not a document of conquest in the manner of an Assyrian battle relief. Nobody is being dragged or beaten. The figures walk with measured dignity, sometimes holding one another's hands or speaking over their shoulders, and the individual character of costume, hair and beard has been rendered with an ethnographic care that still delights the eye. The conception is of an empire as a harmonious gathering, with the king as its centre of gravity. Scholars have long suggested that the scene may refer to a great seasonal festival at the vernal equinox, the Persian new year later known as Nowruz, although the exact nature of any such ceremony remains debated.

At the corners of the Apadana, in the foundation deposits, Darius placed tablets of gold and silver bearing a trilingual inscription in Old Persian, Elamite and Babylonian, a message to the future that his empire was divinely given and justly ruled. Of the columns that once held up the roof, thirteen still stand, slender shafts topped by capitals carved as pairs of kneeling bulls. Their grace is startling for stone of such scale. The architects used light roofs and wooden lintels, which allowed them to space the columns widely, and the resulting hall seems to float on its own height rather than crouch beneath its ceiling.

A Forest of Columns: The Hall of a Hundred Columns and the Palaces

South and east of the Apadana lies the Hall of a Hundred Columns, also called the Throne Hall, a square chamber seventy metres on each side. Xerxes began it, and Artaxerxes I completed it before the end of the fifth century BCE. As the name implies, its roof was held up by a hundred slender columns arranged in ten rows of ten, and its doorways carry reliefs of the king enthroned above the supporting figures of the subject peoples, the throne itself held aloft by the empire it governed. It is a diagram of sovereignty in carved stone, and a hall in which an army could have assembled in comfort.

Close to the terrace edge stands the Tachara, the palace of Darius completed by Xerxes after 486 BCE and the best preserved building on the site. Its doorways are still crisp, its jambs still carry the processions of Persian servants bearing food and the king passing in majesty beneath a parasol, and its rooms retain something of the human scale of palace life. Xerxes called it a winter palace in his inscriptions. Further south, the Hadish Palace of Xerxes, covering some two and a half thousand square metres, is the building where many scholars believe the great fire began.

Clay That Survived the Fire

The most unexpected treasure of Persepolis is not carved on its stairways but baked on small clay tablets. The Persepolis Fortification Archive, discovered during the excavations of the 1930s by the Oriental Institute of the University of Chicago, consists of administrative documents from the reign of Darius, many of them written in Elamite, which recorded the issue of rations, the movement of goods and the payment of workers across the region around the capital. The fire that destroyed the wooden palaces seems to have hardened the clay, and a collapsing wall may have helped to bury and preserve the tablets.

These documents change the way the site must be understood. The sculpted reliefs speak the language of ideology, of kings and harmony and hierarchy, but the tablets speak the language of the granary and the workshop. They show an economy of enormous organisation in which men, women and children received measured allowances of grain, wine and beer in return for labour, and in which officials sealed and recorded every transaction. The people who raised the terrace, and the people who fed them, appear here in their own administrative voices, and the empire becomes human in a way that no throne room can make it.

The Night of the Fire, 330 BCE

In 330 BCE the Achaemenid Empire came to its end. Alexander the Great, having defeated the last of the dynasty, Darius III, in a series of battles that began at Issus and Gaugamela, marched into Persis and took the royal treasuries. The ancient sources, among them Diodorus Siculus, Quintus Curtius Rufus and Plutarch, tell how his army was permitted to sack the city, and how Persepolis was soon after consumed by fire. The most dramatic account, which Diodorus appears to have derived from the lost history of Hieronymus of Cardia, describes a drunken banquet at which an Athenian courtesan named Thaïs urged the king to burn the palace in revenge for the burning of Athens by Xerxes.

Whether the fire was an accident, a drunken impulse or a calculated political act remains one of the great unresolved questions of ancient history. Some scholars read it as symbolic retribution for the Persian invasion of Greece, a gesture that closed the Panhellenic war of vengeance Alexander had proclaimed. Others consider it a deliberate declaration that the old dynasty had ended and that the new king had become master of Asia. Others still believe that fire spread in the aftermath of looting. The sources disagree, and the archaeology, while it shows clear evidence of burning in several buildings, cannot settle the motive.

The terrace was never again a capital, and its ruin was, paradoxically, its preservation, for the reliefs sheltered beneath fallen earth survived in a freshness that exposed monuments could not claim. Those who know the Acropolis of Athens will see in Persepolis the dark mirror of the Greek memory, the place where an old injury was said to have been repaid.

Ruin, Memory and Rediscovery

After the fire, Persepolis passed into memory rather than disappearing from it. Rubble from the ruined site was later reused in building the nearby city of Istakhr, which became an important Sasanian centre, and a Sasanian inscription of 311 CE by Prince Shapur Sakanshah calls the place Sad-stun, the Hundred Pillars. In medieval Persian tradition the ruins were attached to the legendary hero-king Jamshid, and the terrace was known as Takht-e Jamshid, the Throne of Jamshid, or as Chehel Minar, the Forty Minarets. The columns were remembered, but their builders were half forgotten, and an ancient imperial capital had become a place of folklore.

Scientific excavation began in earnest in the 1930s. Ernst Herzfeld conducted the first systematic exploration in 1931, working with Erich Schmidt and the Oriental Institute of the University of Chicago, while the French architect André Godard worked for the Iranian state and became the first director of its archaeological service. Their seasons cleared the stairways and the halls, revealed the reliefs and uncovered the archive that now informs so much of our knowledge. In 1971 the site served as the stage for the lavish celebration of 2,500 years of the Persian monarchy, a spectacle that cast its own long political shadow on the modern history of Iran.

Beyond the Terrace: The Tombs of Kings

No visit to Persepolis is complete without lifting one's eyes to the cliffs that surround it. Immediately behind the terrace, three tombs are cut into the slope of Kuh-e Rahmat, while some thirteen kilometres to the north-west the sheer rock face of Naqsh-e Rustam holds four royal tombs of the Achaemenid dynasty. These are traditionally attributed to Darius I, Xerxes I, Artaxerxes I and Darius II, and their cross-shaped facades, carved high above the plain, are among the most memorable images of ancient Iran. A long inscription by Darius, written around 490 BCE, describes his conquests and the peoples of his empire.

At the base of the cliff stands the Kaaba of Zoroaster, a square stone tower of the fifth century BCE that copies a twin at Pasargadae and whose original purpose remains unknown. Centuries after the Achaemenids, the Sasanian kings added their own reliefs to the same rock. The most famous shows Shapur I on horseback with the Roman emperor Valerian bowing before him, a record of the Persian victory over Rome at Edessa in 260 CE. The cliff thus becomes a layered chronicle, in which one dynasty's grave is made the stage of another's triumph.

This accumulation of meaning is characteristic of the Persian plateau. At Persepolis one meets the Achaemenids, at Naqsh-e Rustam the Sasanians, and in the folk memory of the countryside the legendary kings of the Shahnameh. The traveller who has stood in the great square of Meidan Emam in Esfahan will recognise how Iran has always used stone, tile and spectacle to express the relationship between kingship and the sacred. Persepolis is the earliest and most austere statement of that tradition.

Protecting the Terrace

UNESCO inscribed Persepolis on the World Heritage List in 1979 as reference 114, recognising it as a masterpiece of architecture, an exceptional testimony to an ancient civilisation and a lasting image of Achaemenid kingship. The property has been protected in the Iranian national monuments register since 1931, is owned by the state, and since 2001 has been managed with the help of an on-site research and conservation base that works alongside universities and institutes. UNESCO also notes that restoration has used traditional methods and materials, and that there are no modern reconstructions at Persepolis.

The threats to the site are real, if quieter than the fire of 330 BCE. UNESCO draws attention to the pressure of urban growth in the town of Marvdasht, new villages and polluting industry around the boundary, and to the need for continuing monitoring of air pollution and weathering. Fragile stone left open to the sky for twenty-five centuries is vulnerable to rain, wind, salts and the footsteps of many visitors. The construction of the Sivand Dam, begun in 2006, also aroused concern about the possible effect of rising water on Persepolis and Pasargadae, though engineers have said that both sites stand above the waterline.

Walking the Terrace Today

A sensible route follows the order the ancient architects intended. Begin at the stairway, pass through the Gate of All Nations, turn to the Apadana and its processions, then move on to the Hall of a Hundred Columns, the Tachara and the Hadish Palace, ending with the view from the upper edge of the terrace over the plain. The museum area, in the reconstructed buildings associated with the royal harem, offers small finds and context, and the path up the hill behind the site gives a sweeping outlook over the whole platform. The tombs of Naqsh-e Rustam and the earlier capital of Pasargadae make natural companions on the same day or the next.

Visitors should remember that Persepolis is a living part of Iranian national life. Families come here for picnics, students come for school visits, and in spring the site receives crowds who gather to mark Nowruz in a place that many Iranians regard as a symbol of their long history. The great carved figures of the stairway have watched over countless such visits. To stand among them, listening to the wind move through the broken columns, is to feel that the stage built for the world to see has never quite emptied of its audience.

Reflection

Persepolis forces a question that every great ruin eventually asks: what are monuments for? The Achaemenid kings who raised this terrace wished it to be a permanent image of their authority, an architecture in which the many nations of the world were seen to bear the weight of one throne. They did not expect the throne to fall, nor the timbers to burn, nor the stone to outlast the dynasty by more than two thousand years. Yet in a sense the terrace has succeeded beyond their intention. The empire has gone, but the image has remained, and it continues to make a claim on the imagination of every visitor.

What is most moving, perhaps, is the quality of the claim. The reliefs do not boast of massacre. They show people carrying gifts, walking in order, speaking to one another as they climb. Whether or not that picture was ever true, it was the picture that the Achaemenids wished to leave to the future, and it is a more generous one than most empires have chosen to carve in stone. Standing before it at dusk, one understands why Persepolis has drawn travellers, scholars and dreamers for four centuries, and why it has become for Iranians, as well as for the world, a place of memory and pride.

✦ Conclusion

Persepolis is the supreme surviving image of the Achaemenid Empire: a terrace of stairways, gates and columns that turns political ideology into architecture and carves the diversity of the ancient world into a single procession. Its inscription on the World Heritage List in 1979 recognised three qualities at once, the creative achievement of its builders, the unique testimony of its ruins to a vanished civilisation, and the enduring symbolic power of its royal imagery. Each is visible to anyone who climbs the stair.

For the traveller, the site offers something more than the thrill of antiquity. It offers a lesson in how a state can imagine itself, and how that imagination can outlive the state. The fire of 330 BCE destroyed the roofs but left the reliefs, the archive survived to reveal the ordinary workings of the empire, and later centuries added their own layers of legend and scholarship. To visit Persepolis is to walk through all of those layers at once, and to come away convinced that some stones are made to be remembered.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

1. When was Persepolis inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List?

Persepolis was inscribed in 1979 as reference 114, under criteria (i), (iii) and (vi). The listed property covers 12.5 hectares, and the UNESCO page records no extensions.

2. Who built Persepolis and when?

Darius I, known as Darius the Great, began the terrace around 518 BCE, and his successors continued the work. Xerxes I completed the Apadana and built the Gate of All Nations, and Artaxerxes I finished the Hall of a Hundred Columns, so the complex took several generations to reach its final form.

3. Why was Persepolis burned?

The ancient sources say that the city was burned in 330 BCE after Alexander the Great captured it, with Diodorus Siculus, Quintus Curtius Rufus and Plutarch telling the story of a drunken banquet urged on by the courtesan Thaïs. Historians still debate whether the fire was an accident, an act of revenge for the burning of Athens or a deliberate political gesture.

4. What are the main sights at Persepolis?

The principal sights are the great stairway, the Gate of All Nations, the Apadana with its reliefs of the tribute bearers, the Hall of a Hundred Columns, the Tachara palace and the Hadish Palace. Nearby, the royal tombs at Naqsh-e Rustam and the rock-cut tombs behind the terrace complete the picture.

5. When is the best time to visit Persepolis?

Spring and autumn offer the most comfortable temperatures, and spring also brings the Nowruz crowds. Summer midday heat is severe on the shadeless terrace, so early morning or late afternoon visits are best at any time of year.

🧳 Visitor's Guide

Getting There — The site lies roughly 50 to 60 kilometres north-east of Shiraz, the provincial capital of Fars, and is reached by road from the city in around an hour

Entry — The site is state-managed and ticketed; opening hours and ticket prices change, so check current information locally before you travel

Time Needed — Three to four hours for the terrace; a full day with Naqsh-e Rustam

Best Season — Spring and autumn for mild weather; summer midday heat is intense and the terrace offers little shade

Best Time of Day — Early morning or late afternoon, when the low light brings out the relief carving

Combine With — Naqsh-e Rustam, Pasargadae and the old city of Shiraz

Practical Tip — Bring a hat, water and sturdy shoes, and respect the barriers around the reliefs, since the carved stone is fragile and must not be touched or climbed
