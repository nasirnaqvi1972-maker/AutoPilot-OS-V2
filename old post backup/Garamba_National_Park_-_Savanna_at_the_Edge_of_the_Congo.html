An Unexpected Savanna

The Democratic Republic of the Congo is famous for the rainforest that fills much of the country, the second-largest tropical forest on earth. Yet in the far north-east, close to the border with South Sudan, the trees give way to something very different: a sweep of tall grassland, scattered woodland and winding rivers, a landscape more familiar from East Africa than from central Africa. This is Garamba National Park, one of the oldest and most important protected areas on the continent.

UNESCO inscribed Garamba in 1980 under natural criteria (vii) and (x), recognising its superlative natural phenomena and its importance for the conservation of biological diversity. The park covers about 492,000 hectares of savanna and woodland, with the rivers Garamba and Dungu running through it, and is part of a wider complex of protected hunting reserves that together form one of the largest conservation landscapes in the region.

Garamba is also a place of hard stories. It has been a stronghold of wildlife and a battleground for its survival, and it has been on the List of World Heritage in Danger since 1996. To write about Garamba is to write about the beauty of a wild landscape and the courage of the people who defend it.

Elephants, Giraffes and a Rich Wildlife

Garamba’s savanna supports a notable variety of large mammals. African savanna elephants wander its grasslands and swamps, hippos wallow in the rivers, and buffalo graze in large herds. Lions, leopards and spotted hyenas hunt across the plains, while antelopes such as Lord Derby’s eland, one of the largest of its kind, roan antelope, hartebeest, waterbuck and kob are found here in varying numbers. The park’s rivers support crocodiles and numerous fish.

Perhaps most remarkable is the park’s population of Kordofan giraffes, which is the only giraffe population left in the Democratic Republic of the Congo. These tall animals, reduced to very small numbers by hunting and conflict, have been the subject of dedicated protection efforts, and their survival in Garamba is a source of hope. Their presence adds an unmistakable touch of East African savanna to the Congo basin.

Birdlife is rich as well, with species typical of the Sudanian savanna, and the park lies at the meeting point of several biogeographic zones, which gives it a distinctive mixture of plants and animals. This ecological diversity, together with its large expanses of relatively undisturbed habitat, is a major reason for its World Heritage status.

The Northern White Rhino — A Story of Loss

No animal is more closely identified with Garamba than the northern white rhinoceros. In the 1960s, the park was home to roughly a thousand of these huge, placid grazers, the last significant wild population of their subspecies. Poaching for the horn, driven by demand in Asia and the Middle East, reduced their numbers to just a handful by the early 1980s, and although determined conservation efforts allowed a modest recovery in the following decades, the respite did not last.

In the early twenty-first century, poaching again intensified, and no wild northern white rhinos have been confirmed in Garamba since about 2006. The subspecies now survives only in captivity: the last male, called Sudan, died in 2018 in Kenya, and only two females remain under protection at the Ol Pejeta Conservancy. Scientists and conservationists are now exploring advanced reproductive technologies in an attempt to save the subspecies, a desperate effort that underlines how close the world came to losing it.

The story of the northern white rhino is a sobering lesson. It shows how quickly a species can slide from abundance to near extinction when demand for its products, weak enforcement and instability combine, and how difficult it is to reverse the decline. It is also a reminder that the fate of species is tied to the security and welfare of the people who live around them.

From Hunting Reserve to National Park

The land of Garamba has long been home to people, including the Azande, who established a powerful kingdom in the region in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. During the colonial period, when the territory was ruled by Belgium, areas of the savanna were set aside as hunting reserves, and in 1938 Garamba was formally declared a national park, one of the first protected areas in Africa. Its creation reflected both the concern of conservationists and the colonial authorities’ interest in controlling access to wildlife.

The Gangala-na-Bodio station, established in the colonial era within what became the park, was home to an unusual experiment: the capture and training of African elephants for work, an effort inspired by the use of Asian elephants and promoted by the Belgian king. Although the project never became widespread, the station remains a curious part of the park’s history and, in modified form, a part of its present.

After independence in 1960, the park passed to Congolese management, and in the following decades it faced the pressures of political instability and economic hardship. Funding was scarce, equipment was limited, and rangers worked under difficult conditions, but a dedicated group of staff and scientists continued to protect the park, often at great personal risk.

Conflict, Poaching and the Danger List

Garamba has endured some of the most severe pressures faced by any protected area. Civil war in neighbouring countries brought armed groups and weapons to the region, while heavily armed poachers killed elephants for ivory and rhinos for horn. In the 2000s the rebel group known as the Lord’s Resistance Army, which had fled from northern Uganda, moved into the area and attacked villages and park staff, creating widespread fear and displacement.

Poaching has also taken a heavy toll on elephants. Large herds have been reduced, and in some cases attacks have been carried out from the air, with helicopters used to hunt elephants. Rangers have often been outgunned and many have died in the line of duty, a sacrifice that is too rarely recognised. In 1996, UNESCO placed Garamba on the List of World Heritage in Danger, a status that remains and that reflects the seriousness of the threats.

The conflict has also affected local communities, who have suffered violence and displacement, and who are essential partners for any lasting conservation. Without security, livelihoods and trust, the park’s future will remain precarious, and efforts to protect wildlife must therefore go hand in hand with efforts to support the people who live around it.

A Partnership for Recovery

Since 2005, Garamba has been managed through a partnership between the Congolese conservation authority, the ICCN, and the non-profit organisation African Parks, which brings professional management, funding and training. The partnership has strengthened ranger patrols, improved equipment and communications, and increased monitoring of wildlife by aerial survey. These efforts have helped to stabilise the populations of elephants and giraffes and to protect key areas of the park.

The partnership also invests in the neighbouring communities, supporting schools, health services, agriculture and employment, in the belief that conservation can succeed only if local people see benefits. Cooperation with cross-border partners in South Sudan and with regional authorities helps to address the movement of armed groups and poaching networks across frontiers.

The task remains enormous. Insecurity in the region persists, resources are limited and wildlife populations remain fragile, but Garamba’s story is no longer only one of loss. It is also a story of resilience and determination, and the continued presence of elephants and giraffes on its plains is a living testament to the work of those who defend them.

Giraffes on the Savanna — The Last Herd of the Congo

The giraffes of Garamba are a story of their own. Across Africa, giraffe numbers have fallen sharply in recent decades, and the Kordofan giraffe, once widespread across the Sahel and the savannas of central Africa, is now critically endangered. In Garamba, the population was reduced by hunting and the pressures of conflict to a tiny remnant, and for some time there was concern that they might disappear entirely from the Congo.

Careful protection by rangers and monitoring by researchers have helped these animals to hold on, and in recent years the population has shown signs of stability and modest growth. Aerial surveys, camera traps and ground observations provide data on their numbers and movements, while community outreach aims to reduce hunting pressure. It is a slow and painstaking process, but every calf born on the savanna is a small victory.

The giraffes also illustrate why Garamba matters internationally. The park is the last refuge in the whole of the Democratic Republic of the Congo for a species that once roamed widely, and its loss would mean the disappearance of an entire iconic group of animals from the national fauna. Conserving Garamba is, in this sense, not only a national priority but a contribution to the survival of Africa’s tallest animal.

Reflection — The Cost of Keeping a Wild Place

It is easy to speak of wilderness as a given, something that exists on its own and is merely visited. Garamba shows the truth: wilderness is something that must be defended, day by day, by people who bear real costs to do so. Every elephant that still walks the savanna, every giraffe that browses the acacia, is there because someone chose to stand between it and a gun.

That is why the park matters beyond its borders. It is a test of whether the world can protect its most threatened landscapes in the face of poverty, conflict and demand for illegal wildlife products. If Garamba can be secured, it will offer proof that recovery is possible, and a measure of hope for other wild places that are fighting similar battles.

✦ Conclusion

Garamba National Park is one of the great wildlife sanctuaries of Africa, a savanna of elephants, giraffes and rivers at the northern edge of the Congo. It is also a site of loss and endurance, where the northern white rhino was driven to the brink of extinction and where armed conflict and poaching have repeatedly threatened its survival.

Its future depends on security, partnership and the support of the communities around it. As long as its elephants and giraffes endure, and as long as people are willing to defend them, Garamba remains a place of hope in the story of African conservation.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

Why is Garamba a UNESCO World Heritage Site?

UNESCO inscribed Garamba in 1980 under natural criteria (vii) and (x) because of its outstanding savanna landscapes and its importance for biodiversity, including elephants, giraffes and, formerly, the northern white rhinoceros.

Why is Garamba on the List of World Heritage in Danger?

It has been on the Danger List since 1996 because of threats from poaching, armed conflict and insecurity, which have led to severe declines in wildlife, particularly rhinos and elephants.

What happened to the northern white rhino?

Poaching drove the subspecies to near extinction in the wild. No wild northern white rhinos have been confirmed in Garamba since about 2006, and only two females remain, under protection in Kenya, since the last male died in 2018.

What animals live in Garamba?

The park is home to savanna elephants, hippos, buffalo, lions, leopards, Lord Derby’s eland and other antelopes, as well as the only giraffes in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, which belong to the Kordofan subspecies.

Can tourists visit Garamba?

Access is very limited, and the region has experienced insecurity. Governments have often advised against travel to this part of the Democratic Republic of the Congo. Anyone interested in visiting should consult current official travel advice and the park’s management before making any plans.

🧳 Visitor's Guide

Travel Advice First — Consult your government’s current travel advisory; north-eastern Congo has faced serious insecurity and visits may be restricted or inadvisable

Access — The park is remote; access has typically been by air or by long overland journeys from Dungu, and facilities are limited

Permission — Visiting requires authorisation from the park management and cooperation with local authorities

Best Time — The dry season, roughly December to March, is usually better for wildlife viewing and road conditions

Health — Malaria prevention, vaccinations and a medical kit are essential; medical facilities are limited

Responsible Support — Support conservation by learning about and donating to reputable organisations working in the region

Respect — Follow ranger guidance, respect local communities and never buy products derived from wildlife

Alternatives — Documentaries, scientific reports and conservation updates offer ways to experience Garamba’s story from afar
