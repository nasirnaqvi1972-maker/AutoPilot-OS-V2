A Forest in the Dark

The visitor who passes from the bright courtyard of orange trees into the prayer hall of the Mezquita of Córdoba steps into a strange, shadowy forest. Hundreds of columns of marble, granite and jasper rise in rows into the gloom, each supporting a pair of superimposed arches striped in red brick and white stone, so that the space seems to unfold endlessly in every direction, like an orchard of stone. Light filters in from distant windows, and the arches blend into a rhythm so regular that the eye can scarcely hold it. It is one of the most extraordinary rooms ever built.

The Mezquita, as it is known in Spanish, is the heart of the historic centre of Córdoba, a city in Andalusia that was once the capital of a great Islamic state and one of the largest and most cultured cities in the world. Around it lies a dense network of narrow, winding streets, whitewashed houses with hidden patios, medieval walls, a Roman bridge and the lanes of the old Jewish quarter, a town whose layers of Roman, Islamic and Christian history lie one on top of the other.

UNESCO inscribed the Mosque of Córdoba on the World Heritage List in 1984, and in 1994 extended the property to include the surrounding historic centre, now an area of about eighty hectares. It is recognised under criteria (i), (ii), (iii) and (iv), the full set of cultural criteria that includes masterpiece of human genius, influence on the development of architecture, testimony to a vanished civilisation and an outstanding example of a type of building.

Roman Corduba and the Coming of Islam

Córdoba's history is very long. The Romans founded a colony on the site in the second century BC, and Corduba became the capital of the province of Hispania Baetica, one of the richest in the empire. It was the birthplace of the philosopher Seneca and the poet Lucan, and its Roman remains, including a temple, a theatre and the great bridge across the Guadalquivir, are still visible in the modern city. After the fall of Rome, the city passed to the Visigoths, who made it a centre of the Christian church.

In 711 or 712, Muslim armies from North Africa, having crossed the Strait of Gibraltar, seized Córdoba, and the city became the seat of the governor of the new province of al-Andalus. For decades it was a provincial town within the Umayyad empire, ruled from Damascus, and then, when the Umayyad dynasty was overthrown in Damascus in 750, a survivor of the family made his way across North Africa to Spain.

This was Abd al-Rahman I, a young prince who, in 756, took control of Córdoba and declared himself emir, founding an independent Umayyad state in the west. He and his successors made the city their capital and set about turning it into a worthy rival to Damascus and Baghdad, a centre of government, learning and display.

The Caliphate and the Brightest City in the West

In 929, the emir Abd al-Rahman III proclaimed himself caliph, claiming the title of spiritual and political leader of all Muslims in defiance of the Abbasids in Baghdad and the Fatimids in Cairo. The Caliphate of Córdoba, which lasted until 1031, was the high point of Islamic Spain, a time of political strength, economic prosperity and cultural brilliance. By the tenth century, Córdoba had become one of the largest cities in Europe, second only to Constantinople, and a magnet for scholars, poets and craftsmen.

Under Abd al-Rahman III and his son al-Hakam II, the city was embellished with palaces, mosques, baths, gardens and markets. Just outside the walls, they built the palace-city of Madinat al-Zahra, begun around 936, an immense complex of audience halls and gardens that is itself a World Heritage Site. Al-Hakam II was a great bibliophile, and his library, reputed to hold hundreds of thousands of volumes, was one of the largest in the world.

The achievements of the caliphate were intellectual as well as material. Scholars translated Greek and Arabic works on medicine, astronomy and philosophy, physicians advanced surgical knowledge, and poets composed verse of great refinement. The tolerance of the age, in which Muslims, Christians and Jews lived and worked together, though not without tension, allowed ideas to cross cultural boundaries, laying the foundation for the later transmission of Arab learning to Europe.

The Great Mosque — A Building in Four Acts

The Mezquita was begun in 784 by Abd al-Rahman I, on the site of a Visigothic church, and it was enlarged by successive rulers in four main stages. Abd al-Rahman II extended the prayer hall in the ninth century, al-Hakam II doubled its size in the 960s, and the chief minister al-Mansur added a large eastern section in the 980s. Each phase is distinguishable in the forest of columns, though the overall impression is of astonishing unity.

The columns, more than eight hundred of them in the present building, were largely taken from Roman and Visigothic buildings, which explains their variety of stone and of capital. To raise the ceiling higher than the short columns allowed, the architects devised the double arches, with a lower horseshoe arch supporting an upper round arch, built in alternating voussoirs of white stone and red brick, a pattern that gives the interior its distinctive rhythm.

The most sumptuous part is the area around the mihrab, the prayer niche, added by al-Hakam II between 961 and 976. Its horseshoe arch is faced with glittering mosaics of glass and gold, made by craftsmen from Byzantium, and the domes above it are carved into ribbed star patterns of exquisite delicacy. The Mezquita is not only a masterpiece of Islamic architecture but a monument to the cultural contacts that linked Córdoba to the wider Mediterranean.

From Mosque to Cathedral

The caliphate collapsed in a civil war that began in 1009. The Berbers entered and sacked Córdoba in 1013, the last caliph was deposed in 1031, and the city declined into one of several small kingdoms, though it remained a centre of learning. In the twelfth century, it produced two of the greatest thinkers of the Middle Ages: the Muslim philosopher Ibn Rushd, known in the West as Averroes, and the Jewish scholar Moses Maimonides, who was born in the city around 1138.

In 1236, the Christian king Ferdinand III of Castile conquered Córdoba, and the Great Mosque was consecrated as a cathedral. For almost three centuries it was altered only modestly, with chapels added around the edges, but in the sixteenth century the cathedral chapter built a large Renaissance nave in the middle of the prayer hall, an intervention that shocked many contemporaries and has remained controversial. The result is a building in which a Gothic and Renaissance church grows from inside a mosque, a physical statement of the layering of the city's history.

The Christian rulers also built new defences. In 1328, Alfonso XI began the Alcázar de los Reyes Cristianos, a fortress with massive towers and beautiful terraced gardens that later served as the residence of Ferdinand and Isabella during the final campaign against Granada. At the southern end of the Roman bridge, the Torre de la Calahorra, a fortified gate, guarded the river crossing.

Lanes, Patios and the Jewish Quarter

Beyond the Mezquita, the historic centre is a labyrinth of narrow lanes and small squares, many of them following the lines of the medieval city. The most atmospheric district is the Judería, the old Jewish quarter, where whitewashed walls are hung with flowerpots, and the passages wind between hidden courtyards. The famous Calleja de las Flores, a short alley that opens onto a view of the cathedral tower framed by flowers, is one of the most photographed corners of the city.

The Jewish community of Córdoba, among the most important in medieval Spain, left a precious monument in the small synagogue built in 1314 and 1315, with beautiful stucco decoration in the Mudéjar style and Hebrew inscriptions on the walls. It is one of the very few medieval synagogues surviving in Spain, and stands as a reminder of the long history of Jewish life in the city that ended with the expulsion of 1492.

The patios, the interior courtyards of the old houses, are a defining feature of Córdoba. Inherited from Roman and Islamic traditions, they provide shade, coolness and a private garden within the dense urban fabric, and each May the residents open them to the public and decorate them with potted flowers in the Festival of the Patios, recognised by UNESCO as an element of intangible cultural heritage.

The Bridge, the Baths and the River

The Roman Bridge, with its sixteen arches spanning the Guadalquivir, was first built in the early years of the Roman empire and has been repaired many times since, but its sturdy piers and arches still carry foot traffic into the old town. It is the finest vantage point from which to see the city, with the Mezquita and its bell tower rising above the rooftops, and the Calahorra Tower guarding the far end. The river itself, which once powered mills and irrigated gardens, is a quiet presence.

Along the riverbank, other traces of the past remain. The caliphal baths, a vaulted complex of the tenth century, show the importance of bathing in Islamic life, and old mills, water-wheels and gardens recall the agricultural wealth that supported the city. In the centre, squares such as the Plaza del Potro, with its fountain and inn mentioned by Cervantes, preserve the character of the Renaissance town.

The historic centre still functions as a living neighbourhood, with homes, workshops and cafés, and the pressures of tourism and modern development are a continuing concern. The management of the area, which has no buffer zone and relies on strong local planning protections, must balance the needs of the residents with the pull of visitors.

Visiting Córdoba

Córdoba is easily reached by high-speed train from Madrid in under two hours, and from Seville in about forty-five minutes. The historic centre is compact and best explored on foot, and the main sights, the Mezquita, the Judería, the Alcázar and the Roman bridge, lie within a few minutes' walk of each other. The Mosque-Cathedral charges an entrance fee, with a period of free entry in the morning on most days, though the arrangements change and visitors should check current times and book ahead in busy periods.

The best seasons are spring and autumn, when the weather is mild and the patios are in flower, especially in May, during the festival. Summer is extremely hot, with temperatures that can exceed forty degrees Celsius, and visits are best made early in the morning or in the evening. Visitors should dress modestly in the cathedral and carry water at all times.

A short excursion to Madinat al-Zahra, about eight kilometres west of the city and also a World Heritage Site, completes the picture of the caliphal city. Visitors should check opening hours, ticket requirements and any restrictions on access before they travel.

Reflection

To stand at the mihrab of the Mezquita, with the gold of the mosaics catching the light, and then to turn around to see the cathedral's Renaissance vaults rising behind the forest of columns, is to be reminded how rarely history gives us a clean page. Córdoba is a palimpsest, written over and over by hands that each believed they were completing something. What survives is not a single story but a conversation, between Rome and Islam, between mosque and church, between the city that was and the city that is, and the astonishing thing is that the conversation can still be heard.

✦ Conclusion

The Historic Centre of Córdoba is one of the great palimpsests of European civilisation, a city in which Roman, Islamic, Jewish and Christian cultures left their marks in stone. Its Great Mosque is one of the supreme monuments of world architecture, its streets preserve the pattern of a medieval city, and its patios, bridge and fortresses fill out a rich and complex portrait of a place that once stood at the centre of the western world.

It is also a city that continues to live in its history. Its residents still tend the patios, its craftsmen still work in leather and silver, and its streets still echo with the sounds of a thousand years of everyday life. To visit is to experience a rare thing: a historic centre that is not a museum, but a home.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

1. Where is the Mezquita of Córdoba?

It is in the historic centre of Córdoba, in Andalusia, southern Spain, beside the Guadalquivir River. Córdoba is about two hours by high-speed train from Madrid.

2. When was it inscribed on the World Heritage List?

The Mosque of Córdoba was inscribed in 1984, and the property was extended in 1994 to include the whole historic centre. It is recognised under criteria (i), (ii), (iii) and (iv).

3. Who built the Mezquita?

Abd al-Rahman I began it in 784, and it was enlarged by Abd al-Rahman II, al-Hakam II and al-Mansur in the following two centuries. It became a cathedral after the Christian conquest in 1236.

4. What else is there to see?

The Roman Bridge, the Calahorra Tower, the Alcázar of the Christian Monarchs, the Jewish Quarter and synagogue, the caliphal baths and the patios. Madinat al-Zahra is nearby.

5. When is the best time to visit?

Spring and autumn are best, especially in May for the Festival of the Patios. Summer is very hot.
