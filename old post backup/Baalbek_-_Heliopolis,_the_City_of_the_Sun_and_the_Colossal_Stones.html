The Stones That Made the Sun City

There are places where the ancient world seems to have been built on a scale beyond reason, and Baalbek is among them. In the high valley of the Bekaa in eastern Lebanon, between the snow-capped ridges of Mount Lebanon and the Anti-Lebanon range, a platform of colossal stone supports the ruins of the greatest temple complex of the Roman Near East. Six enormous columns, each about twenty metres tall and crowned by a Corinthian capital, still rise above the site, remnants of a temple whose full colonnade of fifty-four columns once dwarfed the courtyard in front of it. Beneath them, in the wall of the platform, lie stones so large that they have puzzled visitors for two thousand years.

The Romans called the city Heliopolis, the City of the Sun, a Greek name that reflected the ancient worship of the sun and sky god Baal Hadad at the site. Under Rome the sanctuary became one of the most celebrated in the empire, drawing pilgrims from across Syria and beyond, and the emperors who beautified it, from Augustus to the Severans, lavished on it the full resources of an imperial treasury. The result is an ensemble that includes the largest temple of Jupiter ever built, a nearly intact temple to Bacchus and a graceful circular shrine to Venus.

UNESCO inscribed Baalbek on the World Heritage List in 1984 under criteria (i) and (iv), recognising it as a masterpiece of human creative genius and as an outstanding example of a Roman sanctuary, one of the most impressive testimonies to the Roman period at its apogee, displaying the power and wealth of the empire. It is one of the six World Heritage Sites of Lebanon, and one of the most visited.

From Baal to Jupiter

The roots of the sanctuary go back well before Rome. Excavations beneath the Temple of Jupiter have revealed traces of human habitation from the Early Bronze Age, between about 2900 and 2300 BC, and it is likely that the hill had been a sacred place for millennia before the Romans arrived. The local people worshipped a trinity of deities: Baal Hadad, the god of storm and fertility, a goddess of love and fertility identified with Atargatis or Astarte, and a young god associated with vegetation and the cycle of the seasons.

When Alexander the Great and his successors brought Greek culture to the Near East, the site was renamed Heliopolis, and the sun god was identified with Helios. Under the Romans, who took control of Syria in 64 BC, the deities were again reinterpreted, becoming Jupiter Heliopolitanus, Venus and Mercury, the Triad of Heliopolis. The syncretism was characteristic of Roman religion in the East, in which the gods of Rome were grafted onto local cults rather than replacing them.

Augustus established a colony of veterans at Heliopolis in the last decades of the first century BC, and it was probably at that time that the decision was taken to build a new and grander temple of Jupiter on the ancient summit. The hill was levelled, an immense podium was constructed and the foundations of the great temple were laid, beginning an undertaking that would continue for two centuries.

Two Centuries of Building

The Roman complex at Baalbek grew in stages. The Temple of Jupiter, begun under Augustus, was probably completed by about the middle of the first century AD, under Nero, who is credited with building the great altar in front of it. The emperor Trajan added a vast forecourt, a portico of pink granite columns brought from the quarries at Aswan in Egypt, and over the following decades further courts, exedrae and monumental stairways were added.

In the third century, under the Severan emperors and their successors, the sanctuary reached its fullest extent. The great hexagonal court, traditionally attributed to Philip the Arab, who reigned from 244 to 249 and was born in the nearby province of Syria, was added as an entrance hall, and the Temple of Bacchus, with its sumptuous decoration, and the circular Temple of Venus were built. A fourth temple, dedicated to Mercury, stood on a nearby hill, although it has not survived.

The whole complex was an act of imperial theatre. Pilgrims approached through a monumental propylaeum, passed through the hexagonal court and into the Great Court, where an enormous altar stood in front of the temple. The visitor was meant to feel the greatness of the god, and the power of the Roman state that served him.

The Temple of Jupiter and the Trilithon

The Temple of Jupiter was among the largest temples in the Roman world. It stood on a platform some seven metres above the court, and was approached by a monumental staircase. Its facade was formed by a double row of columns, ten across the front and nineteen along each side, which together with the cella enclosed an enormous interior. The columns that survive are of limestone, built in three drums, each about twenty metres high, and with their capitals and entablature they rise to about twenty-two metres.

More astonishing than the columns are the stones of the podium. In the western wall of the platform, set about seven metres above the ground, are three huge blocks of limestone known as the Trilithon. Each is about nineteen metres long, four metres high and three metres thick, and each is estimated to weigh some eight hundred tonnes. They were quarried nearby, lifted, moved and fitted into place with such precision that a knife blade cannot be slipped between them. How the Roman engineers achieved this, with capstans, ramps and human and animal muscle, is still a subject of study.

The quarry, a few hundred metres to the south, holds an even larger block, known as the Stone of the Pregnant Woman, or Hajar al-Hibla in Arabic. Cut but never moved, it lies half-attached to its bedrock, a monolith estimated at about a thousand tonnes or more. Local legends tell that the stones were placed by giants, but the truth is more impressive: they are the work of Roman organisation, ambition and technique.

The Temple of Bacchus

If the Temple of Jupiter inspires awe by its scale, the Temple of Bacchus charms by its beauty and completeness. It stands to the south of the great temple, on its own raised podium, and is among the best preserved of all Roman temples. Larger than the Parthenon in its dimensions, it has a colonnade of fluted Corinthian columns on all four sides, a massive doorway and much of its original roof and entablature still in place.

The temple was built in the middle of the second century, and its decoration is among the richest of any Roman building. The great doorway, some thirteen metres high, is framed by carved jambs and a lintel decorated with vine scrolls, eagles, garlands, and figures of gods and goddesses, and in the centre of the lintel an eagle, symbol of Jupiter, spreads its wings. The interior has fluted columns, niches and a raised sanctuary at the end, and the whole building conveys the sensuous richness of the Roman East.

The temple is dedicated, according to tradition, to Bacchus, god of wine, though the identification is uncertain, and it may have been a temple to another deity of the Heliopolitan cult. Its excellent preservation is due partly to its use in later centuries as a fortress, and partly to the stability of the stone. It remains the best place to understand how a great Roman temple looked, and felt.

Venus, the Hexagon and the Great Court

Southeast of the Temple of Bacchus stands one of the most original buildings of the Roman world, the Round Temple of Venus. Probably built in the third century, it is a small circular shrine set on a podium, with a portico of Corinthian columns whose entablature curves inward between them, creating a play of concave and convex shapes that anticipates the Baroque. Inside, niches once held statues, and the circular cella was crowned by a dome.

The sanctuary was entered through a series of courts. The first, a hexagonal forecourt, led into the Great Court, a vast rectangle of about 135 by 113 metres, surrounded by porticoes and semicircular halls decorated with niches, columns and carved ornaments. In the middle stood a huge altar of sacrifice, with a stepped platform, and two basins for ritual washing, one of which was decorated with a relief of a lion.

Taken together, the courts, the altar and the temples formed a complete processional experience, in which the pilgrim moved from the outer world through ever more sacred zones to the presence of the god. The design shows how a Roman sanctuary in the East combined the Italian temple with local traditions of courtyard and processional approach.

Churches, Mosques and Castles

The pagan sanctuary did not survive the triumph of Christianity. In the late fourth century, the emperor Theodosius I closed the temples and ordered the construction of a basilica in the Great Court of the Temple of Jupiter, with an apse to the west, demolishing parts of the older complex. The Christian church, however, did not last, and the site declined as the Roman Empire retreated from the region.

In the seventh century, Baalbek fell to the Arab armies, and the temple complex was turned into a fortress, one of the largest in the Levant. The Umayyads built a mosque within the walls, and the Ayyubids and Mamluks rebuilt the citadel with massive towers and walls, using stones taken from the Roman buildings. Mongol raiders, earthquakes, and in particular the great earthquake of 1759, brought down many of the columns of Jupiter, leaving the six that stand today.

In the nineteenth century, European travellers, artists and archaeologists rediscovered the ruins, and in 1898 the German emperor Wilhelm II visited the site. Between 1900 and 1904, a German expedition carried out a major excavation, uncovering the courts and the podium, and the work provided the foundation for later conservation. In the twentieth century, Baalbek became a place of culture as well as history, with an annual international festival that brought music and dance to the ancient stage.

A Heritage Under Threat

The history of Baalbek has been shaped by conflict, and in recent years the threat has been acute. The site lies in the Bekaa Valley, an area that has been affected by repeated hostilities, and in November 2024 Israeli air strikes hit locations near the temples, damaging nearby heritage buildings. In response, UNESCO, meeting in extraordinary session, granted provisional enhanced protection under the Hague Convention to a number of Lebanese cultural sites, including Baalbek, and a UNESCO mission visited in March 2025 to carry out a rapid damage assessment.

The World Heritage Committee, at its session in 2025, expressed concern about damage incurred at and near the property as a result of hostilities, encouraged Lebanon to carry out urgent repairs and to relaunch monitoring of the temple complex, particularly of the Temple of Jupiter, and requested an updated report on the state of conservation by 1 December 2026. Other challenges include the need to clarify the boundaries of the property and to manage development in its surroundings.

Anyone considering a visit must treat the security situation seriously. Many governments advise against travel to the Baalbek area, and conditions can change rapidly. Travellers should consult current official advice, seek local information and consider whether a visit is advisable. For the moment, the greatness of Baalbek may best be appreciated through the work of scholars and the efforts of those who protect it.

Reflection

The first view of the six columns, rising out of the plain against the white line of the mountains, is one of those experiences that justify the idea of world heritage. They are enormous, and yet they are only what remains; the temple they belonged to was larger still, and it was only one of the buildings of a city that thought of itself as the dwelling of gods. It is easy to be overwhelmed by the scale, but it is better to look at the details, at the vine scrolls on the doorway of the Temple of Bacchus, at the careful joint between two blocks of eight hundred tonnes, at the marks of the chisel. They are the traces of thousands of individuals, most of them unnamed, who built this monument for reasons that were at once political, religious and human.

✦ Conclusion

Baalbek is one of the supreme achievements of Roman architecture and one of the great ancient sites of the Middle East. Its colossal temples, its enormous stones and its rich decoration reveal the ambitions of an empire at its height, and its long afterlife as church, mosque and fortress shows how a monument can be reinvented by the societies that inherit it.

It is also a site whose future is uncertain. Conflict, neglect and development pressure all threaten it, and its protection depends on the commitment of the people of Lebanon and the international community. To see its columns against the sky is to understand why so many have worked to keep them standing.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

1. Where is Baalbek?

It lies in the Bekaa Valley in eastern Lebanon, about 85 kilometres north-east of Beirut.

2. Why is Baalbek a World Heritage Site?

It was inscribed in 1984 under criteria (i) and (iv) as an outstanding example of a Roman sanctuary and one of the most impressive testimonies to the Roman Empire at its height.

3. What are the largest stones at Baalbek?

The three stones of the Trilithon in the podium of the Temple of Jupiter each weigh some 800 tonnes. The Stone of the Pregnant Woman in the quarry is larger still.

4. What temples can be seen?

The Temple of Jupiter, of which six columns stand; the well-preserved Temple of Bacchus; and the circular Temple of Venus.

5. Is it safe to visit?

Many governments advise against travel to the Baalbek area because of conflict. Check current official advice and local conditions before planning a visit.
