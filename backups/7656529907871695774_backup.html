Abbey of St Gall — The Library of the Soul's Healing

A Hermit's Valley, a City's Heart

The Swiss city of St Gallen is known today for its embroidery, its university and its handsome old town, but it exists because of a monk who wanted to be alone. In the early seventh century, in a wooded valley south of Lake Constance, an Irish hermit built a cell beside a stream, and the monastery that grew up around his memory became, over the following centuries, one of the great centres of learning in Europe. Today the abbey district, with its twin-towered cathedral and its celebrated library, stands in the middle of the city that carries the saint's name.

The Abbey of St Gall, as UNESCO calls it, preserves the physical and intellectual heritage of that foundation. The visible buildings are largely Baroque, the product of a great rebuilding in the eighteenth century, but they stand on the site of a Carolingian monastery and shelter manuscripts that date back more than a thousand years. The property is compact, only about 3.6 hectares, but its significance is enormous.

UNESCO inscribed the abbey on the World Heritage List in 1983 under criteria (ii) and (iv). The first recognises the influence of the Carolingian abbey on the development of monastic architecture across Europe, through the famous Plan of St Gall; the second recognises the abbey as a typical example of a great Benedictine monastery, a centre of art and knowledge, with its rich library and scriptorium. The description speaks of a place reflecting twelve centuries of continuous activity.

From Gallus to Otmar

The founder of the community was Gallus, an Irish monk who came to the continent with the missionary Columbanus around the year 590. After a quarrel with the local authorities, Columbanus left the region, but Gallus, who was sick, stayed behind and, according to tradition, settled around 612 in the valley of the Steinach, where he lived as a hermit until his death in the 640s. Legend says that he tamed a bear that came to his fire, and the bear has remained a symbol of the city ever since.

After his death, a small community of followers kept his cell and his memory alive. In 719 or 720, a priest named Otmar took charge of the church over the saint's tomb and organised the community into a proper monastery, becoming its first abbot. In 747, the monastery adopted the Rule of Saint Benedict, the code of monastic life that would govern it for the next millennium, and it began to attract gifts of land and the patronage of the Frankish rulers.

The Carolingian dynasty, which ruled much of western Europe from the eighth to the tenth centuries, saw monasteries as engines of religion, education and administration, and St Gall flourished under their protection. In 833 King Louis the German confirmed the abbey's immunity, and in 854 he released it from the obligation to pay tithes to the Bishop of Constance, giving it a remarkable degree of independence.

The Plan of an Ideal Monastery

The most famous object associated with St Gall is a drawing. Around the year 820, in the nearby monastery of Reichenau on Lake Constance, a scribe prepared a detailed plan on five sheets of parchment sewn together, showing the layout of an ideal Benedictine monastery. It was addressed to Abbot Gozbert of St Gall, who ruled from 816 to 837 and was then planning the rebuilding of his own abbey. The drawing survives in the abbey library, and it is the earliest known architectural plan on parchment.

The Plan of St Gall depicts more than forty buildings and is annotated with 341 inscriptions in Latin. It shows a great church at the centre, with a cloister, dormitory, refectory and cellar around it, together with a school, a guesthouse for pilgrims and nobles, an infirmary, a physicians' house, workshops, stables, barns, a brewery and a bakery, and gardens for vegetables, herbs and orchard fruit. It is a picture of a self-sufficient community designed to serve God, to educate its members and to receive strangers.

The plan seems never to have been built exactly as drawn, but it was extraordinarily influential. It reflects the reforms of monastic life decided at the Council of Aachen in 816 and 817, and it presented an ideal that shaped the layout of monasteries across Europe for centuries. UNESCO's criterion (ii) rests heavily on it. In the plan, the abstract ideals of Carolingian reform take visible form.

Scriptorium and School

St Gall was not only a place of prayer but of learning. From the eighth century, its monks copied, illuminated and collected books, building a scriptorium that was among the most productive in Europe. They preserved texts of the Bible and the Church Fathers, but also classical Latin authors, grammars and works of science, and the community maintained a school that taught the sons of nobles as well as future monks.

The ninth and tenth centuries were a golden age. The monk Notker Balbulus, Notker the Stammerer, composed poetry and sequences for the liturgy, and his contemporary Tuotilo was famed as a composer, sculptor and goldsmith. The abbey's illuminated manuscripts, among them the Golden Psalter of the ninth century, are celebrated for their craft, and Irish and Anglo-Saxon influences can be seen in the decorated initials of the earliest books.

The abbey's library also played a part in the recovery of antiquity. In 1416, during the Council of Constance, the Italian humanist Poggio Bracciolini visited St Gall and found there a complete text of Quintilian's work on rhetoric, a discovery that delighted scholars of the Renaissance. The abbey's manuscripts thus became a bridge between the learning of ancient Rome and the humanism of the early modern world.

Prince-Abbots and Conflict

In the central Middle Ages the abbey grew rich and powerful. In 1207 the abbot became a prince of the Holy Roman Empire, ruling a territory that stretched across much of north-eastern Switzerland. The city of St Gallen, which had grown up beside the abbey, acquired its own privileges and became a centre of the linen trade, and relations between the monastery and the town were often tense, especially after the town adopted the Protestant Reformation in the 1520s while the abbey remained Catholic.

The two communities lived side by side, separated by a wall, for centuries. During a war in 1712, the troops of Zurich and Bern occupied the abbey and carried off many manuscripts, some of which were returned in 1718 and the rest in the twentieth century. The episode shows the value placed on the collection, which had come to symbolise the abbey's independence.

The end came with the upheavals of the Napoleonic era. In 1805, the canton of St Gallen, formed after the collapse of the old order, dissolved the abbey, ending a thousand years of monastic life. The buildings passed to the canton, the library remained in the abbey precinct, and in 1847 the church became the cathedral of a new diocese of St Gallen.

The Baroque Cathedral

The cathedral that dominates the abbey district today is a creation of the eighteenth century. Between 1755 and 1768, the abbots rebuilt the conventual area in the Baroque style, replacing the medieval buildings with a unified and magnificent ensemble. The architects, who included Peter Thumb of Vorarlberg and Johann Michael Beer von Bildstein, designed a church with a great central rotunda, a long nave and a choir, and twin towers rising to 68 metres above the city.

The interior is among the finest examples of late Baroque and Rococo art in Switzerland. The walls and vaults are covered in pale stucco, touched with gold, and the ceiling is painted with frescoes by Josef Wannenmacher depicting scenes from the lives of the saints. A beautifully carved choir screen divides the nave from the choir, and the tomb of Gallus is honoured in the crypt below.

The abbey church has been a cathedral since the creation of the Diocese of St Gallen in 1847, and it remains a living place of worship as well as a heritage monument. Visitors can enter freely during the day and may be lucky enough to hear the organ or the cathedral choir.

The Library — Pharmacy of the Soul

Across the courtyard from the cathedral stands the Abbey Library, whose Baroque hall, built between 1758 and 1767 to the designs of Peter Thumb, is often described as one of the most beautiful library rooms in the world. The long, bright hall is lined with carved wooden bookcases in warm tones, and the ceiling is decorated with painted panels, framed by delicate stucco. The floor is of polished parquet, and visitors are required to wear felt overshoes to protect it, gliding silently through the room.

Above the entrance, a Greek inscription describes the room as a healing place of the soul, and the idea of books as medicine for the spirit pervades the library. The collection includes about 170,000 volumes, among them some 2,100 manuscripts, of which more than four hundred are over a thousand years old. Treasures include the Plan of St Gall, early Irish gospel books, the Golden Psalter and some of the oldest surviving manuscripts in the German language.

The library also holds a surprise: an Egyptian mummy, that of a woman named Shepenese, who lived around 700 BCE, which was brought to St Gall in the nineteenth century and remains one of the most popular exhibits. The combination of the sacred, the scholarly and the curious reflects the library's long history as a collection of the wonders of the world.

Visiting the Abbey District

St Gallen is easily reached from Zurich, about an hour away by direct train, and from the Lake Constance region. The abbey district lies in the old town, a few minutes' walk from the railway station, and the old town's pedestrian streets, with their half-timbered houses and painted oriel windows, make an attractive approach.

The cathedral is open to visitors throughout the day, except during services, and admission is free. The library, which is a museum, charges an entrance fee and is normally open daily, with some closed periods for maintenance and exhibition changes, so visitors should check the current hours before they travel. Guided tours are available, and photography in the library is restricted to protect the collection.

Nearby, the old town has a number of museums, including the Textile Museum, which recalls the city's embroidery industry, and the surrounding region of Lake Constance and the Appenzell hills offers excellent walking. The best season is from spring to autumn, though the abbey is atmospheric in winter, when the town's Christmas market fills the streets.

Reflection

There is a stillness in the library at St Gall that is difficult to describe. Visitors drift across the parquet in their felt slippers, speaking in whispers, while the light falls on gilt spines that have stood here for two and a half centuries and on glass cases in which manuscripts a thousand years old lie open. The monks who made these books copied them by hand, in cold rooms, for the sake of a work that would outlast them. They could not have imagined the Renaissance that would draw on their labours, or the visitors who would come in slippers to admire them. Yet in that quiet room, the chain of copyists, readers and keepers is still unbroken.

✦ Conclusion

The Abbey of St Gall is a rare surviving witness to the monastic civilisation that preserved and shaped the intellectual life of early medieval Europe. Its Baroque buildings are splendid, its library is one of the finest in the world, and its Plan of an ideal monastery is a unique record of the ambitions of the Carolingian age.

It is also a living part of a modern city. The cathedral still holds services, the library still welcomes readers, and the old precinct still forms the centre of St Gallen. To walk through the abbey district is to see how a hermit's cell became an institution, how an institution became a city, and how the written word has carried the past across the centuries.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

1. Where is the Abbey of St Gall?

It is in the city of St Gallen in north-eastern Switzerland, about an hour by train from Zurich.

2. When was it inscribed on the World Heritage List?

It was inscribed in 1983 under criteria (ii) and (iv).

3. What is the Plan of St Gall?

It is a ninth-century architectural drawing on parchment, made around 820, showing an ideal layout for a Benedictine monastery. It is the earliest known architectural plan on parchment and is kept in the abbey library.

4. Who founded the abbey?

Tradition names the Irish hermit Gallus, who settled in the valley around 612. Otmar became the first abbot in 719 or 720, and the monastery adopted the Benedictine Rule in 747.

5. Can I visit the library and cathedral?

Yes. The cathedral is open daily with free admission, and the library is open to visitors for a fee, with felt slippers provided. Check current opening hours before visiting.
