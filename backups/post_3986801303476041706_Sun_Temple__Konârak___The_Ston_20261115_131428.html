A Chariot at the Edge of the Sea

On the sandy coast of Odisha, where the Bay of Bengal rolls in against a long pale beach, stands a temple that was built to move. It takes the form of a colossal chariot, a stone vehicle mounted on twelve pairs of great wheels and drawn by seven galloping horses, and it was conceived as the carriage of the sun god Surya, crossing the heavens from east to west. Time and the sea have been unkind to it: its great tower fell centuries ago, and the surviving porch and platform are only a part of the original design. Yet even in ruin the Sun Temple of Konârak is one of the most extraordinary buildings in India.

The temple lies at the small town of Konark, in the Puri district of Odisha, about thirty-five kilometres north-east of the sacred city of Puri. Its name derives from the Sanskrit words kona, meaning corner, and arka, meaning sun, and it was long known to European sailors as the Black Pagoda, a dark landmark on the coast. It stands today in a compact site of about ten hectares, enclosed by a wall and surrounded by gardens.

UNESCO inscribed the Sun Temple on the World Heritage List in 1984 under criteria (i), (iii) and (vi). It is recognised as a masterpiece of human creative genius, an outstanding testimony to the thirteenth-century Hindu kingdom of Odisha, and a monument directly associated with the ancient tradition of sun worship and with the arts of dance and sculpture that flourished around it.

King Narasimhadeva and the Building of the Temple

The temple was built in the thirteenth century by King Narasimhadeva I of the Eastern Ganga dynasty, who ruled Odisha from 1238 to 1264. The Gangas were a powerful line of kings whose territory stretched along the eastern coast of India, and they were great patrons of religion and the arts, responsible for the immense Jagannath Temple at Puri. Narasimhadeva is said to have commissioned the Sun Temple around 1250, perhaps to commemorate military victories over the Muslim rulers of Bengal, and certainly to express the power and piety of his dynasty.

The scale of the undertaking was immense. Tradition says that it took twelve years and the labour of twelve hundred craftsmen, led by a master builder named Bisu Maharana, and that the cost was the full revenue of the kingdom for the period. Folk tales have grown around its construction, among them the story of Dharmapada, the young son of the chief architect, who is said to have solved the problem of placing the final capstone on the tower when all the master craftsmen had failed. These stories belong to legend rather than record, but they reflect the awe with which later generations regarded the temple.

The temple was dedicated to Surya, the sun god, who occupies a special place in Hindu tradition as the visible source of light and life. Sun worship had a long history in India, and it flourished in Odisha, where the coast, facing the rising sun over the sea, was seen as a particularly auspicious place for his worship. The temple at Konârak is the finest and last great monument of that tradition.

The Wheels of Time

The temple's design is a single, bold metaphor. The platform on which it stands is carved with twenty-four huge wheels, each about three metres in diameter, arranged in twelve pairs on either side. In front of the entrance, seven horses, carved in the round, strain forward as if drawing the building after them. Together they transform the temple into the chariot of Surya, a divine vehicle crossing the sky.

The wheels are not mere decoration. Each has eight broad spokes and eight thinner ones, and the rim and hub are covered with exquisite carving, including floral scrolls, birds, animals and figures. They also function as sundials: the shadow cast by the hub on the spokes can be used to tell the time with considerable accuracy, a fitting conceit for a temple to the sun. Traditional interpretations see in the twenty-four wheels the hours of the day, in the twelve pairs the months of the year, and in the seven horses the days of the week, so that the whole structure becomes a calendar in stone.

The result is a work of architectural poetry. The temple does not merely house an image of the sun god; it enacts his journey. For the pilgrim approaching from the east, as the sun rose above the sea behind the entrance, the chariot must have appeared to glide forward, carrying the light of the day.

A Temple of Many Parts

Like other great temples of Odisha, the Sun Temple was designed as a sequence of distinct units arranged along an east-west axis. At the western end stood the main sanctuary, the deul, which housed the image of Surya and was crowned with a great curvilinear tower that is believed to have risen to about seventy metres. In front of it stood the jagamohana, or assembly hall, topped by a pyramidal roof of stepped tiers, and further east the Nata Mandira, or hall of dance, and the Bhoga Mandapa, where offerings were prepared.

Only the jagamohana and the Nata Mandira survive in substantial form. The porch, which rises to about thirty metres, is a masterpiece of Kalinga architecture, its roof built of three tiers of stone adorned with sculpture, each stage decorated with musicians and heavenly beings. The main tower collapsed at some point before the nineteenth century, perhaps because of structural weakness, perhaps because of the instability of the sandy ground and the weight of its stone, and its fall left the sanctuary open to the sky.

The building materials were as varied as the design. The bulk of the temple was built of khondalite, a dark local stone that weathers to a rich brown, while the doorways and some of the principal images were made of chlorite, a greenish stone that takes a high polish, and laterite was used for the foundations. The huge blocks, many weighing tens of tonnes, were fitted with astonishing precision, and were held together with iron clamps and dowels rather than mortar.

The Sculpture — A World in Stone

The Sun Temple is a gallery of sculpture on an epic scale. Every surface of the plinth, walls and roof is covered with carvings: processions of elephants and horsemen, hunting scenes, battles, musicians and dancers, scenes of court life, animals and plants, and a multitude of mythical beings. The sculptors worked with a freedom and vitality that distinguishes the art of Odisha, and their figures, with their rhythmic poses and sensuous modelling, seem to be caught in the middle of a dance.

Among the most striking are the great guardian figures: huge lions crushing elephants, carved at the entrance as symbols of the triumph of the king, and the three images of Surya set into niches on the walls of the porch, carved in dark chlorite, each facing a different direction to catch the sun at morning, noon and evening. They show the sun god in the northern manner, booted and belted, with lotus flowers in his hands. One relief, famously, depicts a giraffe, brought as an exotic gift from Africa by way of Bengal, evidence of the wide trading connections of medieval Odisha.

The temple also contains many erotic sculptures, depicting couples in intimate embraces. They are a well-known feature of temple art in India and are generally understood as expressions of the creative energy of life, the union of the human and the divine, and the abundance of the world. They form a part of the temple's complex symbolism rather than its main subject, and they have been interpreted in many ways by scholars and worshippers.

Ruin and the Sea

What led to the decline of the temple is not completely clear. By the sixteenth century, the worship of Surya at Konârak had ceased, and the temple seems to have fallen into neglect. The coast was attacked more than once by invaders, and the main image of the sun god was reportedly moved for safekeeping to Puri, where it is said to have been kept in the great temple of Jagannath. The famous Aruna pillar, which once stood at the entrance, was also taken to Puri in the eighteenth century, where it stands today before the Jagannath Temple.

Popular legend tells that a great magnet, a lodestone, was fixed at the top of the tower, and that it drew ships off their course and wrecked them, so that sailors had it removed, causing the collapse of the whole structure. It is a good story, but there is no evidence for it. More plausible explanations include the structural failure of the huge tower, perhaps due to the weakness of its foundations on the sandy coast, as well as damage from earthquakes, storms and deliberate destruction.

As the sea withdrew and the sand drifted in, the ruins were gradually buried, and by the nineteenth century the temple had become a mound of rubble, overgrown with jungle. The stones were quarried by local builders, and valuable sculptures were taken away. Only the porch stood relatively intact, its interior choked with debris.

Rescue and Conservation

The salvation of Konârak began in the early twentieth century, when the British colonial authorities, alarmed by the danger that the surviving porch would collapse, decided to take action. Between about 1901 and 1903, the interior of the jagamohana was filled with sand and its openings sealed, a drastic measure that stabilised the structure but also closed the interior to view. The surrounding ruins were cleared, and the site was placed under the care of the Archaeological Survey of India.

Since then, the temple has been the object of continuing restoration. Conservators have cleaned and repaired the carvings, reconstructed parts of the platform and built gardens around the monument, and the site has been tended with growing care. The challenges are formidable: the salt-laden sea air attacks the stone, monsoon rains and humidity encourage decay, and the sheer number of visitors puts pressure on the fragile structure. Debates continue about the possibility of reopening the sealed porch and the risks involved.

Konârak is also a living cultural centre. Each year, in February, pilgrims gather at the nearby beach of Chandrabhaga for a festival of ritual bathing, and in December the temple provides a magnificent backdrop for a festival of classical Indian dance, in which the dancers of Odisha, heirs to the tradition celebrated in the temple's own carvings, perform before the monument.

Visiting Konârak

The temple is easily visited from Puri, about thirty-five kilometres away, and from Bhubaneswar, the capital of Odisha, some sixty-five kilometres to the north-west, which has the nearest airport and railway connections. The drive from Puri follows the coast along the Marine Drive, passing through casuarina groves and fishing villages, and takes about an hour.

The monument is open daily, and visitors pay an entrance fee, which is higher for foreign tourists. The best times are early morning, when the light is soft and the crowds thin, and late afternoon, when the stone glows. The cooler months from October to March are the most comfortable; the summers are hot and humid. A small museum run by the Archaeological Survey of India displays sculptures and fragments recovered from the site, and the beach of Chandrabhaga is a short distance away.

Visitors should respect the rules of the site and not climb on the structures. Licensed guides are available at the entrance and add much to the experience, explaining the iconography of the carvings. Travellers should check opening times, ticket prices and local conditions before visiting.

Reflection

The Sun Temple at Konârak is a monument to a strange kind of confidence: the confidence of builders who believed that stone could carry the sun across the sky. The tower is gone, the god has departed, and the sea that once lapped near the walls has drawn far back; yet the wheels still turn in the imagination, and the horses still strain forward. In the late afternoon, when the shadows of the stone spokes sweep across the platform and the carved dancers seem to step out of the wall, one understands that this was always a temple of time as much as of light. It records an hour that has not yet ended.

✦ Conclusion

The Sun Temple of Konârak is one of the great achievements of Indian architecture and a work of art on a heroic scale. Its design, which turns a building into a chariot of the sun, is among the boldest in the history of religious architecture, and its sculpture is a vivid record of the life, beliefs and imagination of medieval Odisha.

It is also a monument that has survived against the odds. Fallen, buried and nearly forgotten, it has been rescued and restored, and it continues to inspire dancers, scholars and pilgrims. To stand before its great wheels at sunrise is to feel the continuing power of an idea: that the sun, the giver of light and time, deserves a temple that moves.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

1. Where is the Konârak Sun Temple?

It is at Konark in the Puri district of Odisha, eastern India, on the Bay of Bengal coast, about 35 kilometres north-east of Puri and 65 kilometres from Bhubaneswar.

2. Who built the temple and when?

King Narasimhadeva I of the Eastern Ganga dynasty built it around 1250 CE, during his reign of 1238 to 1264.

3. Why is it shaped like a chariot?

It represents the chariot of the sun god Surya, with twenty-four carved wheels and seven horses. The wheels also work as sundials.

4. When was it inscribed on the World Heritage List?

It was inscribed in 1984 under criteria (i), (iii) and (vi).

5. When is the best time to visit?

October to March is the most comfortable period, and early morning or late afternoon gives the best light.
