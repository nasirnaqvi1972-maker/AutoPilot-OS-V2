A Town Named for Black Gold

There are towns that are built beside rivers, towns that are built beside harbours, and there is Ouro Preto, which was built on a mountainside because that is where the gold was. In the closing years of the seventeenth century, prospectors pushing into the rugged interior of Brazil discovered in the streams of what would become Minas Gerais a quantity of metal so great that it redrew the economy of the Portuguese empire. Within a generation a scatter of rough mining camps clinging to the slopes beneath the peak of Itacolomi had become one of the largest and richest settlements in the Americas, and the name that the Portuguese gave it, Vila Rica, the Rich Town, was no exaggeration. Later it would be renamed Ouro Preto, Black Gold, after the dark crust of iron oxide that coats the nuggets of the district and gives the metal its sombre skin.

What survives today is the most complete urban portrait of that golden age anywhere in Brazil. The streets still follow the contours of the original mining tracks, rising and falling so steeply that the pavements turn into stairways, and so crookedly that no two squares are quite on the same level. White houses with heavy timber balconies press shoulder to shoulder down the slopes; fountains of carved stone stand at the corners where water carriers once gathered; and above the roofs, at intervals that seem almost musical, rise the soapstone fronts and slim towers of churches that were paid for in gold and carved by artists who had no equivalent in Europe. The whole town is a mountain made habitable, and walking it is an exercise in both thigh and imagination.

The World Heritage Committee recognised this unique inheritance in 1980, when the Historic Town of Ouro Preto was inscribed as reference 124 under criteria (i) and (iii). The listing was a historic moment in its own right, for Ouro Preto was the first Brazilian cultural property to enter the World Heritage List. The inscribed area covers 167.8 hectares, surrounded by a buffer zone of 2,036.9 hectares, and it embraces not simply a collection of monuments but an entire townscape: the squares, public buildings, residences, bridges, fountains and churches that together make a cohesive ensemble of Baroque architecture, laid over a landscape that has never been tamed by a grid.

The Rush for Gold

The Discovery in the Mountains

The story begins in 1695, when the bandeirante Manuel Borba Gato found gold on the Rio das Velhas, the discovery that opened the Brazilian gold rush. The bandeirantes, adventurous and often brutal men from the São Paulo region of mixed Indigenous and European descent, had spent generations probing the interior in search of captives and treasure, and in the mountains of what the Portuguese would call the General Mines they finally found the second. The Crown moved quickly to control the new wealth. In 1697 it established the Estrada Real, the Royal Road, which carried the gold down from the highlands to the coast, and in 1698 the first settlements that would become Vila Rica were founded in the hills. In 1711 several of these camps were united into a single municipality, and the town began to take on the form of a city.

The numbers that followed are almost unbelievable. By around 1730 the population of the district was in the order of forty thousand, and by 1750 some eighty thousand people were living in and around the town, which made it one of the largest cities in the Americas at the time. Officially, some eight hundred tons of gold left Brazil for Portugal over the course of the eighteenth century, and that figure does not include the gold that was smuggled, nor the gold that stayed behind to be hammered into leaf and spread across the altars of the churches. In 1720 the Crown recognised the new importance of the region by making Vila Rica the capital of the captaincy of Minas Gerais, a status it would keep for almost two hundred years, until 1897.

The Faces Behind the Fortune

A gold rush is always told as a story of discovery, but it was also a story of labour, and the labour was overwhelmingly that of enslaved Africans. Many hundreds of thousands of people were carried in chains to the mining regions of Brazil, and it was their hands that dug the shafts, washed the gravel and carved the channels that carried water to the workings. The splendour of Ouro Preto cannot be separated from that human cost, and the most thoughtful visitors are those who carry the fact with them as they look at the gilded altars. Folk memory preserves the legend of Chico Rei, an African king said to have been enslaved and then to have bought his freedom and that of his people with gold gathered in secret, a tale that endures because it expresses a truth the documents often hide: that the people who made the town were not only its masters but also those whom the masters held in bondage.

The Town That Gold Built

A City Without a Plan

Unlike the Spanish towns of the Americas, which were laid out by decree on regular grids, Ouro Preto was not planned at all. It grew as gold was found, with each camp attaching itself to a seam and each seam drawing its own cluster of houses, chapels and market stalls. The result is a town of irregular squares, abrupt corners and sudden views, in which the lines of the streets follow the original mining tracks and the topography itself. UNESCO describes the original winding road and its irregular layout as an expression of the landscape, and it is exactly this quality that makes Ouro Preto so compelling to walk. Nothing is straight, and everything is framed.

For visitors who like to compare colonial cities, the contrast with Historic City of Sucre, Bolivia, the formal white city of the Spanish Andes, within reach of the silver of Potosí, is instructive, while Old Havana and its Fortification System, Cuba shows how a very different Iberian colony expressed its wealth through harbour walls and merchants' mansions.

Aleijadinho and the Mineiro Baroque

The Little Cripple

If one name stands above all others at Ouro Preto, it is that of Antônio Francisco Lisboa, known to history as Aleijadinho, the Little Cripple. Born in Vila Rica in 1738, the son of a Portuguese master builder, Manuel Francisco Lisboa, and an enslaved African woman named Isabel, he was freed by his father at his baptism and trained in the workshops of the town. He worked as architect, sculptor and carver at a time when those trades were not sharply divided, and UNESCO calls him the greatest artist of colonial Brazil. Around 1777 he began to suffer from a disease that gradually deformed his hands and feet; its exact nature is still debated, with rheumatism, syphilis and porphyria among the diagnoses proposed. Accounts say that he worked on, carving with tools strapped to his wrists, and he lived until 1814.

Aleijadinho's reputation has sometimes grown into legend, yet the central fact remains. In the churches, fountains and altarpieces of Ouro Preto, and in the twelve prophets that he carved between 1800 and 1805 for the sanctuary of Bom Jesus de Matosinhos at nearby Congonhas, there is an artistic voice that is unmistakably Brazilian.

A Church Carved in Soapstone

Nowhere is this voice louder than at the Church of Saint Francis of Assisi, begun in the mid-1760s as the chapel of the lay Third Order of Saint Francis. The documents tie Aleijadinho to the building from 1772, and they record his work on the pulpits, the chancel arch, the portal and the design of the main altar; the traditional attribution of the entire design to him is questioned by some scholars, but few doubt that his hand shaped its character. The façade is its most memorable feature, with the curved lines and round towers that give the building a sense of movement, and above the door a great medallion in which Saint Francis receives the stigmata, surrounded by angels and garlands. The material is as important as the design: the local soapstone, soft when quarried and hardening in the air, lent itself to the delicate carving that gives the façade its lace-like quality. UNESCO notes the combination of gneiss and soapstone in the sculptures of the town as a feature that sets them apart from contemporary European art.

Inside, the ceiling of the nave is the work of the painter Manuel da Costa Athaide, a native of Mariana who worked across the region in the late colonial period. His great ceiling shows the Virgin of the Porciúncula borne up to heaven by a choir of musical angels, and it is a lesson in how the Baroque adapts to its environment: the faces of the angels, and even of the Virgin, are drawn from the people of Minas, and the vegetation and light are those of the tropical uplands rather than of Lisbon or Rome. The effect is of a heaven that has been moved, gently and without apology, to the mountains of Brazil.

Gold in the Churches

Brotherhoods and Their Chapels

One of the reasons the churches of Ouro Preto are so numerous and so splendid lies in a decision made far away in Lisbon. The Portuguese Crown, anxious that the religious orders would smuggle gold and drain the colony of its wealth, forbade them from establishing convents in Minas Gerais. The result was that religious life in the mining region was organised not around monasteries but around lay brotherhoods, the irmandades, which raised the money, commissioned the artists and built the churches, each according to its own social standing and devotion. There were brotherhoods of the wealthy and brotherhoods of the poor, of white mine owners, of mixed-race craftsmen and of Black and enslaved Africans, and in their rivalry for splendour the brotherhoods filled the hills with some of the most ambitious churches in the Americas.

One of the most moving results is the oval church of Nossa Senhora do Rosário dos Pretos, built by and for the town's Black population, which shows that the Baroque was not only an art of the rich but also a means by which the marginalised made their presence visible.

The Weight of the Altar

The great parish church of Nossa Senhora do Pilar is perhaps the most astonishing demonstration of what gold could do. Completed in 1786, according to the usual account, it is a building in which the carving seems to have no end: cherubs, garlands, columns and scrolls climb every surface, and everything is covered in gold leaf. Visitors' guides routinely state that more than four hundred kilograms of gold were used in its decoration, a figure that is difficult to verify but that conveys the sheer scale of the effect. Entering the church is a vertiginous experience, and one that makes immediate sense of the idea that the Baroque is an art of excess, an art that wishes to overwhelm the senses so that the faithful may glimpse paradise. Below, in the basement, a small museum of sacred art now preserves vestments, silver and sculptures from the churches of the region.

The Dream of Independence

Vila Rica Under Pressure

By the 1780s, the gold of Minas was beginning to decline, but the demands of the Portuguese Crown were not. The colony was required to deliver a fixed quantity of gold each year in taxes, and when it failed to do so, the Crown could impose the derrama, a forced levy designed to make up the shortfall. It was a measure that hung over the heads of the inhabitants of Vila Rica, and in its shadow a group of intellectuals, officers, priests and poets began to talk about a different future. They were influenced by the ideas of the Enlightenment and by the example of the American Revolution, and their plan, as far as it is known, was to rise in revolt on the day the derrama was announced and to found an independent republic.

The conspiracy of 1789, known as the Inconfidência Mineira, never came to pass, for it was betrayed and its members arrested before it could begin. Among them were the poets Cláudio Manuel da Costa and Tomás Antônio Gonzaga, the officer Francisco de Paula Freire de Andrade, and the man who would become the movement's symbol, Joaquim José da Silva Xavier, nicknamed Tiradentes, the Tooth-Puller, after the dentistry he practised among his other trades. After a long trial, eleven were condemned to death, but Queen Maria I commuted most of the sentences to exile in Africa. The exception was Tiradentes. He was hanged in Rio de Janeiro on 21 April 1792, his body was dismembered, and the parts were sent to Vila Rica to be displayed as a warning to others.

The Martyr of the Republic

The Crown's intention was to wipe out the memory of the conspiracy, but it achieved the opposite. Tiradentes became the most celebrated martyr of Brazilian independence, and the day of his execution is now a national holiday. In Ouro Preto his memory is preserved in the Museu da Inconfidência, which occupies the former Casa de Câmara e Cadeia, the town hall and prison, a late Baroque building commissioned by Governor Luís da Cunha Meneses in the 1780s and designed by José Fernandes Pinto Alpoim. Its pantheon, completed in 1942, houses the remains of conspirators who died in exile, and the museum was declared a National Museum in 1990.

The Long Sleep and the Student Town

Stagnation as Preservation

As the mines ran dry in the nineteenth century, the fortunes of Ouro Preto declined. The name Ouro Preto was formally adopted on 20 May 1823, when the town was raised to the status of a city, but its importance was already fading, and when the seat of government of Minas Gerais was moved in 1897 to the newly planned city of Belo Horizonte, the old capital was left behind with its churches, houses and memories. This was a misfortune for its inhabitants, but a blessing for the heritage. UNESCO notes that the economic stagnation of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries helped to preserve the colonial buildings and urban pattern of the town. Where other cities were rebuilt in the fashionable styles of the age, Ouro Preto was too poor to change, and the result is that its Baroque skyline survives almost untouched.

When the Committee inscribed Ouro Preto in 1980, it did so with the view that the urban core of the colonial period remained intact and that its authenticity of form, design and materials had been preserved by controlled growth and limits on the scale of new building.

Republics, Rocks and Revelry

Ouro Preto is also very much a living town. In 1876 the Escola de Minas, the School of Mines, was founded here, and the Federal University of Ouro Preto that grew from it now enrols about ten thousand students, many of whom live in the town's famous repúblicas, shared student houses modelled on the traditions of Coimbra. Their presence keeps the old streets lively in the evening, and the carnival, led by blocos such as the Zé Pereira dos Lacaios, founded in 1867 and described as the oldest active carnival block in Brazil, is a street party in a Baroque museum.

A Landscape of Mountains and Mists

Under the Peak of Itacolomi

The setting of Ouro Preto is inseparable from its meaning. The town lies in the Serra do Espinhaço at an altitude of roughly 1,100 to 1,200 metres, beneath the peak of Itacolomi, which rises to 1,722 metres and which, in the eighteenth century, served as a landmark for the gold seekers. The climate is cool tropical highland, with a long dry season and a rainy season from November to February that shrouds the hills in mist, and the morning fog that gathers in the valleys and clears around the church towers makes the town seem to sit halfway between earth and sky.

The modern economy of the region still relies on mining and metallurgy as well as tourism, and UNESCO acknowledges that the property faces pressure from urban growth, traffic, industry and tourism, with expansion onto unstable hillsides posing a risk to its setting.

Reflection

To stand on the steps of São Francisco at dusk, when the soapstone turns the colour of honey and the bells of the town begin to answer one another across the valleys, is to feel the strange double character of Ouro Preto. It is a place of astonishing beauty, and it is a place built from suffering. The gold that gilded the altars was dug by enslaved hands, the art that rose from that gold was made by artists who often had no rights, and the independence that the town dreamed of was first imagined by men who were willing to die for it. Few heritage sites express so honestly the entanglement of wealth, faith, art and exploitation that lies at the root of colonial America.

And yet Ouro Preto also shows what art can do with such a legacy. Out of the mines came not only wealth but a new way of seeing, a Baroque that gave Brazil its first great national art.

✦ Also Read

If the dialogue between colonial wealth and sacred art fascinates you, continue to Historic City of Sucre, Bolivia, where the silver of the Andes paid for a very different kind of colonial city, white, ordered and scholarly, and where the Spanish Baroque was grafted onto an Andean world. Read alongside Ouro Preto, the two towns show how the great mining economies of Iberian America produced architecture of striking individuality in remote highland settings. For a still larger Baroque capital of the Spanish Americas, see Historic Centre of Mexico City and Xochimilco, Mexico, where the wealth of the colonial empire reshaped an ancient lake city.

✦ Conclusion

The Historic Town of Ouro Preto is one of the great monuments to the colonial history of the Americas, a town that grew out of a gold rush and left behind an art that outlived the gold. Its irregular streets, its fountains and bridges, its civic buildings and above all its churches form a cohesive ensemble of the Brazilian Baroque, in which the genius of Aleijadinho and Athaide, and of the many carvers, painters and builders whose names are now forgotten, found its most complete expression. It is for this reason that the World Heritage Committee recognised the town in 1980 under criteria (i) and (iii) as a masterpiece of human creative genius and a testimony to a society shaped by mining and colonial rule.

For the traveller, Ouro Preto offers something that few places can match: a historic town that is still a lively community, climbed on foot, tasted in the cafes of its squares, and heard in the bells of its churches. It asks the visitor to look at gold with both admiration and sorrow, and to remember the miners, the artists and the rebels who made the town what it is. To walk through Ouro Preto is to see how a mountain became a city, and how a city, against all odds, became a work of art.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

1. When was the Historic Town of Ouro Preto inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List?

It was inscribed in 1980 as reference 124 under criteria (i) and (iii), and it was the first Brazilian cultural property to be placed on the List. The inscribed area is 167.8 hectares, with a buffer zone of 2,036.9 hectares.

2. Why is Ouro Preto called Black Gold?

The name Ouro Preto means Black Gold and refers to the dark coating of iron oxide that covers the gold found in the district. The town was known as Vila Rica until it was renamed Ouro Preto in 1823.

3. Who was Aleijadinho?

Antônio Francisco Lisboa, known as Aleijadinho, was born in Vila Rica in 1738 and died there in 1814. The son of a Portuguese master builder and an enslaved African woman, he became the greatest sculptor and architect of colonial Brazil, and his works include carvings at the Church of Saint Francis of Assisi in Ouro Preto and the prophets at Congonhas.

4. What was the Inconfidência Mineira?

It was a conspiracy of 1789 by intellectuals and officers in Vila Rica who hoped to establish an independent republic in Brazil. The plot was uncovered, and Tiradentes, its most famous member, was hanged in Rio de Janeiro on 21 April 1792. His death is commemorated as a national holiday.

5. When is the best time to visit Ouro Preto?

The dry season from May to September offers sunny days and cool nights that are ideal for walking, while the summer months from December to February are greener but bring heavy afternoon rain. Holy Week and carnival are lively but crowded, so book accommodation well in advance.

🧳 Visitor's Guide

Getting There — Ouro Preto lies about 100 km from Belo Horizonte, roughly two to two and a half hours by road, with regular buses from the Belo Horizonte bus station; most international visitors fly into Belo Horizonte or travel overland from Rio de Janeiro

Entry — The streets, squares and exteriors are open to all; the churches, museums and mines have their own opening hours and charge separate admission, so confirm current times and prices locally before visiting

Time Needed — Two full days for the main churches, museums and squares; three or more to include the mines, hiking and nearby Mariana

Best Season — May to September for dry, sunny days and cool nights; December to February is greener but rainy

Best Time of Day — Early morning for fog and soft light on the churches, and late afternoon when the soapstone glows

Combine With — Mariana, the sanctuary of Bom Jesus de Matosinhos at Congonhas, the Mina do Chico Rei and the Itacolomi peak

Practical Tip — Wear sturdy shoes for the steep and uneven cobbles, bring layers since the altitude makes nights cool, and carry some cash for small shops and cafes
