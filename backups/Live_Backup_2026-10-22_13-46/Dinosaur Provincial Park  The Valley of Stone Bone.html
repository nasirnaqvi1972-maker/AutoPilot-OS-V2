A Prairie That Falls Away

The Canadian prairie is a landscape of horizons. For hours the road runs straight across fields of wheat, canola and native grass beneath a sky so large that it seems to press the land flat, and nothing in the view prepares the traveller for what lies ahead. Then, near the Alberta town of Brooks, the ground simply opens. The plain breaks off at a rim, and below it lies a sunken world of striped hills, rounded domes, narrow canyons and fantastic columns of rock, a maze of eroded valleys that seems to belong to another planet.

This is Dinosaur Provincial Park, a protected area of some 7,493 hectares along the Red Deer River in southern Alberta. The badlands here are among the most extensive in Canada, but their fame rests on something beneath the surface: a vast, continuous accumulation of fossils from the Late Cretaceous period, about seventy-five million years ago, that makes the park one of the richest dinosaur graveyards ever discovered. Skeletons, skulls, eggshells, footprints and the remains of countless smaller creatures lie exposed in the hillsides, released by the same erosion that carved the valley.

UNESCO inscribed the park on the World Heritage List in 1979 under two natural criteria: (vii), for the superlative beauty of its badland landscape, and (viii), for its outstanding record of the history of life on earth. Few sites in the world satisfy both criteria so convincingly. A visit here is at once an aesthetic experience and a lesson in deep time, in which the visitor walks across the floor of an extinct world and looks, quite literally, at the bones of its inhabitants.

The Land Seventy-Five Million Years Ago

To understand what the fossils represent, one must picture a very different Alberta. In the Late Cretaceous, a shallow inland sea known as the Western Interior Seaway divided North America in two, stretching from the Arctic to the Gulf of Mexico. Along its western shore lay a broad, low coastal plain, warm and humid, crossed by meandering rivers that carried sediment eastward from the rising Rocky Mountains. Forests of conifers, ferns and flowering plants covered the floodplains, and lagoons, swamps and river channels teemed with life.

This was the environment preserved in the rocks of the Dinosaur Park Formation, part of the Belly River Group, which forms the exposed layers of the park. Rivers regularly flooded, burying carcasses under fresh sand and mud before scavengers and decay could destroy them, and this rapid burial is the secret of the extraordinary preservation. Over millions of years the sediments hardened into sandstone, siltstone and mudstone, and the entire sequence was later buried deeper under younger rocks.

The present landscape is far younger than the rocks. At the end of the last Ice Age, around fifteen thousand years ago, enormous volumes of glacial meltwater poured across southern Alberta and cut a deep valley through the plains, exposing the ancient layers and sculpting them into the badlands seen today. The process continues: every rainstorm washes fresh material from the slopes, uncovering bones that have lain buried for millennia and, if unnoticed, carrying them away.

The Great Canadian Dinosaur Rush

The first European to recognise the palaeontological wealth of the Red Deer River was Joseph Burr Tyrrell, a geologist of the Geological Survey of Canada who in 1884 found the skull of a large carnivorous dinosaur near what is now Drumheller. His discovery, later named Albertosaurus, was among the earliest dinosaur finds in Canada, and it prompted expeditions to explore the river in search of more. In 1898, Lawrence Lambe of the Geological Survey began collecting fossils in the area that would become the park, and over the following years the quality and quantity of the fossils revealed the extraordinary potential of the region.

The decisive moment came in the early years of the twentieth century, when the American Museum of Natural History in New York sent its celebrated fossil hunter Barnum Brown to Alberta. In 1910, Brown and his team built a flat-bottomed barge, floated down the Red Deer River, and camped along the banks, prospecting the cliffs for bones and shipping crates of fossils downstream to the railway. They were soon joined by a rival party led by Charles H. Sternberg, the veteran collector, and his sons, working for the Geological Survey of Canada. The competition between the two groups, which lasted until about 1917, became known as the Great Canadian Dinosaur Rush.

The results were spectacular. Hundreds of specimens were shipped to museums in New York, Ottawa, Toronto, London and elsewhere, and the skeletons assembled from the Alberta badlands became the stars of dinosaur halls around the world. Many of the fossils that visitors admire in great museums today, from horned dinosaurs to duck-billed giants, originated in a few kilometres of Alberta riverbank. The rush ended when the market for new specimens eased, but the park's potential was far from exhausted, and the modern phase of scientific work, led by the Royal Tyrrell Museum in Drumheller, continues the work with sophisticated new methods.

A Cast of Giants

The fossils of the park represent an entire ecosystem, and its cast of characters is remarkable. The most spectacular are the horned dinosaurs, or ceratopsians, whose heavily armoured skulls carried huge frills and horns. Centrosaurus, with its single large nose horn and hooked frill, was the commonest of them, and its remains occur in vast bonebeds that appear to record mass drownings of herds during river crossings or floods. Styracosaurus bristled with a crown of long spikes around the edge of its frill, while Chasmosaurus had a broad, elaborate shield and long brow horns.

Duck-billed dinosaurs, or hadrosaurs, were equally abundant. Species such as Corythosaurus, with its rounded helmet-like crest, and Lambeosaurus, bearing an unusual hatchet-shaped one, are known from superb skeletons, some of them complete with impressions of skin. Armoured ankylosaurs, including the club-tailed Euoplocephalus, patrolled the floodplains, while smaller dinosaurs, some of them feathered, darted among the undergrowth. Preying on all of these was the tyrannosaur Gorgosaurus, a lithe, powerful hunter whose skeletons have been recovered from the park in numbers.

Dinosaurs, however, are only part of the story. The rocks also yield the fossils of fish, turtles, crocodile relatives, flying reptiles, early mammals, frogs and salamanders, along with the plants and invertebrates of their world. The park's significance is not just the abundance of its dinosaurs but the completeness of its record: it preserves nearly an entire community of living things, from the largest predators to the smallest scavengers, in a single sequence of rock. That completeness allows scientists to study not only individual species but the way an ancient ecosystem functioned.

The Living Valley

The park today is alive in ways its fossils might suggest it should not be. Along the river grows a ribbon of cottonwood forest, one of the largest remaining stands of riparian woodland on the northern plains, and the shade and moisture of this narrow band support a rich community of birds, deer and small mammals. Above the valley, the prairie grassland stretches to the horizon, and on the dry badland slopes the vegetation changes to sagebrush, prickly pear cactus and hardy grasses that cling to the crumbling soil.

Wildlife is abundant. Mule deer and white-tailed deer browse in the coulees, pronghorn graze on the prairie above, and coyotes hunt across the flats. Hundreds of bird species have been recorded, from raptors that nest on the cliffs to songbirds of the cottonwood groves, and the prairie rattlesnake basks in the warm rocks of the slopes in summer. The contrast between the hot, dry badlands and the cool green river corridor gives the park an unusual ecological richness and makes its landscape vivid and varied throughout the seasons.

Climate shapes the visit. Summers are hot and dry, with temperatures that can climb well above thirty degrees Celsius, while winters are cold and windy. Rain presents its own hazard: the clay soils that make up much of the badlands turn into a slippery, sticky paste known locally as gumbo, and flash floods can fill the dry channels in minutes. These conditions are a reminder that the processes that created the valley are still active and that the land demands respect.

Peoples of the Plains and the Making of a Park

Long before palaeontologists arrived, the Red Deer River valley was part of the homeland of Indigenous peoples of the northern plains, including the Blackfoot Confederacy, whose Siksika, Kainai and Piikani nations and their neighbours hunted bison across the prairies and used the river valleys as winter shelter. The coulees and cliffs of the badlands hold places of ceremonial and spiritual significance, and the Blackfoot have long recognised the fossil bones that protrude from the hillsides, which feature in oral traditions about the earth and its ancient inhabitants.

European ranching and settlement followed the arrival of the railway in the late nineteenth century, and the small community of Steveville grew up along the river as a river crossing and trading post. As the fame of the fossil finds spread, however, so did the damage. Collectors, commercial and amateur alike, removed bones from the hills in the early twentieth century, and there were fears that the fossils would be depleted or lost. In 1955, the Province of Alberta established Dinosaur Provincial Park to protect the site and regulate its use, and the park was later designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1979.

Today, protection is strict. Much of the park is a designated natural preserve, accessible only through guided tours and hikes, and visitors may not collect fossils, disturb bones in the ground or remove any material from the site. The rules can seem severe, but they reflect a simple fact: a fossil removed without careful recording loses most of its scientific value, because its position in the rock is part of its meaning.

Science and the Visitor

Modern research in the park is carried out by the Royal Tyrrell Museum of Palaeontology and visiting universities, using techniques that Barnum Brown could hardly have imagined. Mapping of bonebeds, CT scanning of skulls, analysis of the chemistry of teeth and bone, and detailed studies of ancient environments are revealing not only what these animals looked like but how they lived, how they grew, and how herds behaved. The work has transformed the understanding of horned dinosaurs, in particular, showing how their elaborate frills and horns evolved and how different species coexisted in the same environment.

For visitors, the park offers a range of experiences. A self-guided trail network leads through the public area, with interpretive signs and viewpoints over the valley, while guided bus tours and hikes lead into the restricted preserve, where fossils lie visible in the hillsides, sometimes still partly buried in the rock. The visitor centre houses exhibits and displays, and the campground along the river allows travellers to stay overnight in the cottonwoods, watching the badlands change colour as the sun goes down. The nearby Royal Tyrrell Museum in Drumheller, with its huge collection of mounted skeletons, makes an excellent companion visit.

Reflection

Dinosaur Provincial Park confers a particular kind of humility. The human story of the valley is old, but the geology lies on another scale entirely: the fossils at one's feet are tens of millions of years older than the first human beings, and the river that exposes them cut its valley only yesterday in geological terms. To walk across the badlands at dusk, with the cliffs gathering shadow and the first bats flitting along the river, is to feel the strange vertigo of deep time. The bones in the hills belong to animals that lived, fought, bred and died in a world that no longer exists, and yet they remain here, patient and legible, waiting for anyone who has the curiosity to look.

✦ Conclusion

Dinosaur Provincial Park is one of the great natural archives of the planet. Its badlands are beautiful in their own right, but their deeper value lies in the astonishing completeness of the world they record: a Late Cretaceous ecosystem preserved in extraordinary detail, from the largest dinosaurs to the smallest scavengers, and still yielding new discoveries after more than a century of study.

It is also a place of careful stewardship. The strict protection of the fossil beds, the partnership between museums, scientists and Indigenous communities, and the quiet discipline of visitors who leave every bone where they find it have ensured that the park remains a living laboratory rather than an exhausted quarry. In that sense, it offers a model of how a society can honour both the wonder and the responsibility of the deep past, and a promise that the valley's stone bones still have stories to tell.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

1. Where is Dinosaur Provincial Park?

The park lies along the Red Deer River in southern Alberta, Canada, about 48 kilometres northeast of the town of Brooks and roughly two and a half hours by road from Calgary. It covers 7,493 hectares of badlands, prairie and river valley.

2. Why is it a UNESCO World Heritage Site?

It was inscribed in 1979 under criteria (vii) and (viii) for the outstanding beauty of its badland landscape and for its exceptional record of the Late Cretaceous, including some of the richest dinosaur fossil beds in the world.

3. What kinds of dinosaurs have been found there?

Horned dinosaurs such as Centrosaurus, Styracosaurus and Chasmosaurus, duck-billed hadrosaurs such as Corythosaurus and Lambeosaurus, armoured ankylosaurs and tyrannosaurs such as Gorgosaurus, along with many smaller dinosaurs and other animals, have been recovered from the park.

4. Can visitors see fossils or collect them?

Visitors can see fossils in place on guided tours and hikes into the natural preserve, and displays at the visitor centre present finds from the park. Collecting or disturbing fossils is strictly forbidden, because a bone removed without scientific recording loses most of its value.

5. When is the best time to visit?

Late spring to early autumn is the main visiting season, with long days and the full range of tours available. Summer can be very hot, so water, sun protection and early starts are advisable, while rain can make badland trails dangerously slippery.
