A River Older Than the Mountains

Deep in the Mackenzie Mountains of Canada’s Northwest Territories, about five hundred kilometres west of the city of Yellowknife, a river does something that rivers are not supposed to do. Instead of flowing around the mountains, it flows through them. The South Nahanni River cuts straight across the ranges in a series of tight, twisting canyons whose limestone walls rise a thousand metres above the water, and it does so because it was here first. The river existed before the mountains rose, and as the land buckled upward around it, the water kept to its course and sawed its way down, like a blade drawn slowly through a rising loaf.

The result is a landscape of astonishing drama. Canyon walls tower above rapids and whirlpools. Hot springs steam in cold boreal forest. Caves beneath the mountains glitter with ice crystals and glow with stalactites. And at the middle of it all, the whole river drops over a cliff in a single thundering plume, Virginia Falls, which at about ninety metres is roughly twice the height of Niagara. Few places on Earth so vividly illustrate the power of running water to shape the planet.

It is not surprising that Nahanni was among the very first places placed on the World Heritage List, in 1978. But the park is more than a geological curiosity. It is the ancestral land of the Dene, home to grizzly bears, caribou and wolves, and for most of the last century it has been a place of legend, myth and adventure. This post follows the river from its sources to its confluence, through rock, water, memory and the long effort to protect one of the last great wildernesses of North America.

Where and What Nahanni Is

Nahanni lies in the Dehcho Region of the Northwest Territories, near the border with the Yukon, in a part of Canada that has no roads into the interior and few settlements around its edges. The nearest communities are Nahanni Butte, Fort Simpson and Fort Liard, small places that serve as gateways for floatplanes and canoe expeditions. The name comes from the Dene language: the river is the Naha Dehé, the river of the land of the Naha people.

The World Heritage property covers about 476,560 hectares, or 4,766 square kilometres, around the South Nahanni River and part of its tributary the Flat River. Since 2009 it has sat inside a much larger Nahanni National Park Reserve, created when Canada added some 2,500,000 hectares to the original park. The reserve now totals around three million hectares and surrounds more than ninety-five per cent of the World Heritage property, giving the river a protective buffer far greater than its original boundaries.

An Antecedent River and the Geology of Deep Time

The South Nahanni is a rare example of what geologists call an antecedent river, one that predates the uplift of the mountains it crosses. As folding and faulting raised the Mackenzie ranges over millions of years, the river was able to maintain its course by cutting downward as fast as the land rose, producing a series of deep gorges rather than a detour around the heights. The principal canyons are separated by wider valleys, and each presents a different expression of the same process.

UNESCO recognised this under criterion (viii), which honours exceptional examples of ongoing geological processes. At Nahanni the list is long: fluvial erosion, tectonic uplift, folding and canyon development, wind erosion, karst and pseudo-karst landforms, and a variety of hot springs. A single river basin brings together a whole textbook of earth science, and it does so on a scale that is easy to see from a canoe.

Virginia Falls: The Great Plunge

The most famous feature of the park is Virginia Falls, known in the Dene language as Nailicho. Here the river, narrowed and accelerated, plunges about ninety metres over a cliff into a churning plume of spray, and a great pillar of rock at the brink splits the flow into two columns. Parks Canada describes the falls as roughly twice the height of Niagara Falls, though far narrower, and the sound of the cataract can be heard long before it is seen.

The falls are also the point at which most visitors begin their river journeys. Below the falls the river races through a fourth canyon, sometimes called Painted Canyon or Five Mile Canyon, which was created as the waterfall itself eroded the limestone and migrated slowly upstream. Visitors who arrive by floatplane often camp near the falls, portage around them on a trail and then set out on the water below, a transition from the roar of the cataract to the hush of the gorge.

Hot Springs, Tufa and the Underworld of Caves

The beauty of Nahanni is not confined to the river’s surface. Along its banks and tributaries, thermal springs rise from deep within the rock, carrying dissolved minerals that crystallise as they cool. At Rabbitkettle Hot Springs the minerals have built great terraced mounds of tufa over thousands of years, structures that are among the most unusual features of the northern Canadian landscape. Access to such delicate formations is carefully controlled so that their growth is not disturbed.

Beneath the plateaus and mountains lies an even more hidden world. The limestone of the region is riddled with caves, sinkholes and underground streams, a karst landscape in which water has dissolved the rock from within. Parks Canada notes caves lined with ice crystals and others adorned with stalactites and stalagmites. These cave systems are the least accessible part of the park and, for many scientists, the most intriguing.

Life in the North: Grizzlies, Caribou and Boreal Forest

The vegetation of Nahanni is a transitional mosaic. At lower elevations the river valleys carry spruce, poplar and birch forest, while higher up the land opens into alpine tundra and bare rock. The transition between mountain and boreal ecosystems supports a rich fauna, among them grizzly and black bears, wolves, moose, Dall’s sheep, mountain goats and woodland caribou. Bears are a constant presence, and visitors are taught how to camp, cook and store food in ways that keep both people and animals safe.

Birdlife includes raptors that nest on the cliffs and waterbirds that use the quiet reaches of the river. The expanded park reserve also protects wider ranges for the caribou and other wide-ranging animals, and it includes spectacular mountain terrain beyond the original property, such as the granite spires of the Cirque of the Unclimbables, famous among mountaineers.

The Dene and the Land of the Naha

Long before prospectors and canoeists came, the land belonged to the Dene, the Indigenous peoples of the Dehcho region. The name Nahanni itself comes from their language, and the river, its canyons and its falls all carry Dene names and stories. The waterfall Nailicho, the river Naha Dehé and the surrounding territory form part of a cultural landscape that has been used, travelled and understood for countless generations.

The modern park reserve is managed cooperatively with the Dehcho First Nations through a consensus team that includes Parks Canada. Its status as a park reserve rather than a full national park reflects the fact that Indigenous land claims in the region are still being resolved, and the cooperative approach acknowledges the Dene as partners and not merely as neighbours. It is a model of conservation in which people are part of the landscape rather than excluded from it.

Headless Valley: Gold, Legends and the Making of a Myth

The twentieth century gave Nahanni a darker reputation. Prospectors came to the river a century ago in search of gold, and they found little. But in the early years of the century several of them died in unexplained circumstances, some of them reportedly found without their heads, and the stories gave rise to the names Deadmen Valley and Headless Valley. Newspapers seized on the legend, and for decades the Nahanni was imagined as a haunted and perilous place.

The truth is probably more prosaic. The deaths of isolated men in a remote and unforgiving wilderness had natural explanations, from accident and exposure to the hazards of winter, and the legends grew with the telling. But the myth did what myths do: it drew attention to a place that had previously been unknown, and it made the Nahanni a part of the Canadian imagination. Writers, filmmakers and adventurers followed, and the call for protection grew with them.

From Legend to Park: Protection and World Heritage

Canada established Nahanni as a national park reserve in the 1970s, and in 1978 it was among the first places inscribed on the World Heritage List. UNESCO recognised it under two criteria. Criterion (vii) honoured the South Nahanni as one of the most spectacular wild rivers in North America, with deep canyons, huge waterfalls, spectacular karst terrain, cave systems and hot springs. Criterion (viii) honoured its exceptional record of geological processes.

The great expansion of 2009 was a landmark, multiplying the protected area more than six-fold and securing the headwaters and tributaries that feed the river. It brought the wider watershed, on which the health of the original property depends, under the same legal protection. Conservation assessments have described the condition of the property as good, with some concerns, among them climate change, mineral exploration around the boundaries and the pressures of tourism.

Paddling the Nahanni: The Visitor’s Experience

Nahanni is not a place for casual tourism. There are no roads to the park, and the journey usually begins with a floatplane flight from Fort Simpson or Fort Liard to a lake or to the base of the falls. From there visitors can join a guided canoe or raft trip, or travel independently if they have the necessary experience. Journeys down the South Nahanni typically last a week or more and cover long distances through canyons, valleys and boreal wilderness, with camping on gravel bars and riverbanks.

The season is short, running roughly from June to September, and weather can be changeable even in midsummer. Visitors are required to register with Parks Canada and to attend an orientation before travelling, and bear safety, self-reliance and respect for the land are central to the experience. For those who cannot commit to a full expedition, flightseeing tours offer a spectacular overview of the canyons and the falls.

Reflection — The Value of Wildness

Nahanni reminds us of something that modern life makes easy to forget: that some places are valuable precisely because they remain beyond our reach. There is no road to the falls, no viewing platform at the brink, and no cell tower on the canyon rim. The experience of the river depends on distance, effort and uncertainty, and those qualities cannot be reproduced by engineering or improved by development. A wilderness is a kind of silence, and silence can be lost in a single generation.

In this respect Nahanni belongs with the great natural sanctuaries of the world, among them Yellowstone National Park, USA and Te Wahipounamu, South West New Zealand. Like them, it asks a simple question: whether a society can choose to leave something alone. The answer, written in the cooperative management of the park and the great expansion of its protection, is a hopeful one.

✦ Conclusion

Nahanni National Park is one of those places where the land seems to speak in its own voice. The river that cuts through the mountains, the waterfall that falls twice the height of Niagara, the hot springs that steam in the cold and the caves that glitter in the dark are all expressions of forces older and larger than human history. That they survive in a nearly intact state is a tribute to remoteness, but also to the choices of people who decided to protect them.

For the traveller who is prepared to make the journey, Nahanni offers something increasingly rare: an encounter with a wild river on its own terms. Go with respect, with preparation and with time, and the Naha Dehé will repay the effort with a memory that endures long after the paddle is dry.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

Where is Nahanni National Park and why is it a World Heritage Site?

Nahanni National Park lies in the Dehcho Region of Canada’s Northwest Territories, in the Mackenzie Mountains. It was inscribed on the World Heritage List in 1978 under criteria (vii) and (viii) for its spectacular wild river, deep canyons, huge waterfalls, karst terrain, cave systems and hot springs, and for its exceptional record of ongoing geological processes.

How high is Virginia Falls?

Virginia Falls, known to the Dene as Nailicho, drops about 90 metres, roughly twice the height of Niagara Falls. A rock pillar at the brink divides the flow into two plumes.

How do you get to Nahanni?

There are no roads into the park. Most visitors fly by floatplane from Fort Simpson or Fort Liard to the river or the falls, then travel by guided or independent canoe or raft trip. Flightseeing tours are also available.

When is the best time to visit?

The season runs roughly from June to September, when the river is ice-free and conditions are best for canoeing. Weather is changeable, so visitors should prepare for rain, cold and bears.

Who manages the park?

Nahanni National Park Reserve is managed by Parks Canada in cooperation with the Dehcho First Nations. The reserve was expanded in 2009 by 2,500,000 hectares to about three million hectares and now surrounds the World Heritage property.

🧳 Visitor's Guide

Best Time to Visit — Roughly June to September, when the river is open and the weather is most settled

Getting There — Fly by floatplane from Fort Simpson or Fort Liard; no roads enter the park; the communities are reached by road and air from southern Canada and Yellowknife

Time Needed — A day for a flightseeing tour; one to three weeks for a full river expedition

Permits — Visitors must register with Parks Canada and attend an orientation; fees apply and conditions can change, so confirm before planning

What to Bring — Waterproof gear, warm layers, bear-safety equipment and food storage, maps, a satellite communicator and emergency supplies

Safety — Bears, cold water, remote rescue and changeable weather; guided trips are recommended for inexperienced paddlers

Etiquette — Leave no trace, respect Dene cultural sites and follow rules protecting sensitive formations such as the hot springs

Nearby Attractions — Fort Simpson, Nahanni Butte, the Liard River and the Cirque of the Unclimbables in the wider park reserve
