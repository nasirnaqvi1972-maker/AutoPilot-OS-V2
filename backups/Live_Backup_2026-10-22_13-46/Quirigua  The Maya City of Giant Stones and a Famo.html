Quirigua — The Maya City of Giant Stones and a Famous Victory

A Small Classic Maya Capital in the Motagua Valley of Eastern Guatemala, Where Towering Sandstone Stelae and Sculpted Boulders Carved in the Eighth Century Record the Reign of Cauac Sky and the Overthrow of the Rival City of Copan, Forming One of the Largest Collections of Maya Monumental Art | UNESCO World Heritage Site

Giants in a Banana Plantation

The road to Quiriguá runs through banana plantations, mile upon mile of glossy green leaves in the hot, humid lowlands of eastern Guatemala. Then, through a gap in the trees, a clearing opens, and in the middle of it, rising from a field of mown grass, stand the stones. They are slender and immense, some taller than a three-storey house, each one a single block of red sandstone carved on all four sides with a king in magnificent costume and columns of hieroglyphic text. Beneath their thatched shelters they look like giants waiting patiently in a forest clearing.

These are the stelae of Quiriguá, and they are among the most extraordinary monuments of the ancient Maya. The ceremonial architecture of the city is modest, a few platforms and a small acropolis, but its sculpture is superb. The site is home to the tallest stone monumental sculpture ever erected in the New World, and it holds one of the largest collections of carved Maya monuments anywhere. UNESCO describes it as an outstanding example and the largest corpus of Maya art masterpieces.

Quiriguá is also a place with a story. For centuries the city was a subordinate of its mighty neighbour Copán, but in the year 738 its king captured and killed the ruler of Copán and turned a vassal town into a sovereign power. The monuments he raised afterwards are a record of that triumph. This post explores the city, its king and its stones, and the challenge of protecting soft sandstone in a rainforest.

A Small City in a Great River Valley

Quiriguá lies in the lower Motagua River valley in the department of Izabal, in the south-east of Guatemala, a hot, wet lowland close to the Caribbean coast. The valley was a major corridor of trade in the Maya world, the route by which jade, a stone more precious than gold to the Maya, travelled from its sources in the highlands toward the lowland cities and the coast. A settlement that controlled part of this route was well placed for prosperity.

UNESCO records that the site was inhabited from the second century AD, with ruins and vestiges of dwellings dating from about AD 200 to 900. Most of the monuments for which it is famous, however, date from the eighth century. The ancient city covered a modest area, and the World Heritage property, a park of 34 hectares, protects its core: the Great Plaza with its stelae, the ball court and the acropolis.

A Vassal of Copán

For much of its history Quiriguá was overshadowed by Copán, the great Maya city in what is now Honduras, about fifty kilometres to the south. Copán was one of the leading powers of the southeastern Maya region, and its kings ruled with a splendour that is still visible in its monuments. Quiriguá, by contrast, was a smaller centre that appears to have been under Copán’s influence, and its rulers seem to have owed it loyalty.

The relationship changed in the eighth century with the accession of a new king, whose name is written K’ak’ Tiliw Chan Yopaat, often called Cauac Sky in English. He came to the throne in 724 and reigned until 785, and over those sixty years he transformed the city. UNESCO describes how, during his reign, Quiriguá became the capital of an autonomous and prosperous state.

738: The Defeat of Copán

The turning point came in 738. In that year Cauac Sky’s forces captured the ruler of Copán, Waxaklajuun Ub’aah K’awiil, popularly known as 18 Rabbit, one of the most celebrated kings of the Maya world, who had presided over the great flowering of his city. According to the inscriptions, the captive king was sacrificed at Quiriguá, and the victory freed the city from Copán’s dominion.

It was a stunning reversal, and its effects were immediate. Quiriguá’s prosperity rose, apparently through control of the trade route, and its king began a campaign of building and monument-making. The defeat was a blow from which Copán did not quickly recover, and the event marks one of the great dramas of Maya political history.

Cauac Sky’s Building Programme

With independence secured, Cauac Sky set out to build a capital worthy of his achievement. He enlarged the Great Plaza, one of the largest in the Maya lowlands, and began raising a series of stelae in it, each commemorating a period ending in the Maya calendar and celebrating the king’s deeds. He also remodelled the acropolis, where the royal palace stood, and commissioned other sculptures.

The architecture itself was modest. The buildings were of masonry, with platforms, small temples and a ball court, and none rivals the great pyramids of Tikal or the hieroglyphic stairway of Copán. What made Quiriguá exceptional was the quality and scale of its sculpture. The Maya of Quiriguá chose to put their energy into stone monuments that would announce the king’s glory to the world and to eternity.

The Tallest Stelae in the Americas

The most spectacular of the stelae is Stela E, the tallest stone monument of its kind ever erected by the Maya or any other people of the pre-Columbian Americas. Standing about ten metres above ground and extending deeper into the earth, and weighing an estimated sixty-five tonnes, it was quarried from sandstone some distance away and brought to the plaza, where it was raised and carved on all four faces. The achievement required enormous labour and organisation.

The stelae portray the king in lavish costume, wearing elaborate headdresses and carrying symbols of rulership, in front and back views that are carved in deep relief. The sides carry long hieroglyphic inscriptions in the fine, flowing style of the Quiriguá scribes. The stone is soft sandstone, which allowed the sculptors extraordinary freedom, and the monuments display a three-dimensional quality that is rare in Maya art.

Zoomorphs: Boulders Turned Into Monsters

Quiriguá’s sculptors did more than carve stelae. They also transformed huge boulders into extraordinary zoomorphs, sculptures in the form of fantastic creatures with their surfaces covered in intricate relief. The creatures, which blend features of reptiles, birds, jaguars and supernatural beings, are often shown with human figures emerging from their mouths, and they carry long inscriptions that wind across their backs and bodies.

These sculptures belong to a tradition of Maya art that saw stone as a living thing. The monsters were not mere decoration but symbols of the cosmos: the earth as a turtle or crocodile, the sky as a celestial serpent, the surface of the world as a living being. Their carving required remarkable skill and imagination, and they remain among the most unusual works of Maya sculpture.

Reading the Stones: Calendars and Creation

The inscriptions on the monuments are among the most valuable written records of the Maya. UNESCO notes that the site contains an impressive series of carved stelae and sculpted calendars that constitute an essential source for the study of Maya civilisation. The texts record dates in the Long Count, the Maya system for counting time from a mythical starting point, together with the names of kings, their ceremonies, their wars and their genealogies.

Some inscriptions go further, recounting the Maya story of creation, in which the gods set the cosmos in order at the beginning of the present era. The stelae thus placed the king within a cosmic narrative, presenting his reign as part of the order of the universe. For modern scholars, they are an extraordinary resource, allowing historians to reconstruct events and dynasties with a precision rarely possible for ancient societies.

After the Golden Age

Cauac Sky was succeeded by Sky Xul, who reigned from 785 until about 797, and then by Jade Sky, who ruled around the end of the century. The city continued to erect monuments for a time, but it did not long outlast its greatest king. Like other lowland Maya cities, Quiriguá was affected by the wider upheavals of the late Classic period, and its monument-building came to an end in the early ninth century.

The city was not entirely abandoned, and traces of occupation continue into the Early Postclassic, but it never regained its earlier grandeur. The forest slowly covered the plaza and the stones, which stood unnoticed for centuries until their rediscovery by nineteenth-century travellers.

Rediscovery and Excavation

In 1840 the English artist Frederick Catherwood visited the ruins with the American diplomat and writer John Lloyd Stephens and made drawings of two of the stelae, which were published in Stephens’s popular book on his travels in Central America. The drawings brought the monuments to the attention of the world, and in 1854 a longer account was made by a visiting scholar.

In 1910 the United Fruit Company bought the land for banana cultivation, and while it cleared much of the surrounding forest it set aside a small area around the ruins as an archaeological zone. Scientific excavation began in the early twentieth century, and an extensive archaeological project was carried out from 1975 to 1980, sponsored by the University of Pennsylvania, the National Geographic Society and the government of Guatemala. UNESCO inscribed the site in 1981, soon after the project’s completion.

What UNESCO Recognised: Criteria (i), (ii) and (iv)

The park was inscribed under three criteria. Under criterion (i), UNESCO recognised the monuments as an outstanding example and the largest corpus of Maya art masterpieces. Under criterion (ii), it noted that the monuments were carved during the Classic Period, from about AD 250 to 900, a time when ideas and artistic styles circulated widely among Maya cities. Under criterion (iv), it recognised the outstanding eighth-century monuments, with their impressive series of carved stelae and sculpted calendars, as an essential source for the study of Maya civilisation.

UNESCO also confirms that all the attributes expressing the property’s value are protected within the boundaries of the inscribed 34 hectares, which are safeguarded by the Constitution of Guatemala and the national law for the protection of cultural heritage and protected areas.

Protecting Soft Stone in a Wet Land

The stelae are made of sandstone, a stone that is easy to carve but vulnerable to weathering, and the climate of the Motagua valley is hot, humid and rainy. Water, algae and lichens attack the surfaces, and heavy rainfall can erode the carving. For this reason the monuments are protected by roofs, and conservation work concentrates on cleaning, consolidation and the control of drainage.

The park is also vulnerable to natural hazards. In 2020 hurricanes Eta and Iota caused severe damage in the region, flooding the lower Motagua and affecting the archaeological park, which reopened in June 2021. In 2016 the monuments of Quiriguá were also registered in the Memory of the World Register for Latin America and the Caribbean, a recognition of the documentary value of their inscriptions and a spur to their protection.

Visiting Quiriguá

Quiriguá is not on the main tourist circuit of Guatemala, which is part of its charm. The park lies about four kilometres from the main highway that crosses the south-east of the country, roughly 200 kilometres from Guatemala City, and it is easily reached by car, by bus to the nearby junction or as a stop on the way to Lake Izabal, the Río Dulce and the Caribbean coast. Many visitors combine it with the Maya ruins of Copán in Honduras, a few hours away.

A visit can be done in half a day. A broad, shaded path leads from the entrance to the Great Plaza, where the stelae stand under their roofs, and onward to the acropolis and the zoomorphs. The climate is hot and humid year round, so visitors should bring water, insect repellent and light clothing, and the drier months of the year are generally more comfortable. Check opening hours and fees locally, since arrangements can change.

Reflection — Power Written in Stone

Quiriguá is an eloquent reminder that monuments were instruments of politics. Cauac Sky did not simply raise stones; he rewrote his city’s place in the world, announcing in stone that a former vassal had become a sovereign power. The stelae were propaganda in the grand manner, intended to be read across generations: here is our king, here are his victories, here is his place in the order of the universe.

Yet the monuments speak to us now in ways their makers could not have intended. They tell of ambition, rivalry and artistry across the span of centuries, and they show how a small city in a river valley could produce works of enduring power. Like Pre-Hispanic City of Teotihuacan, Mexico, Quiriguá belongs to the great story of Mesoamerica, a world of cities, calendars and stone that flourished long before European arrival.

✦ Conclusion

The Archaeological Park and Ruins of Quiriguá is among the lesser-known jewels of Maya civilisation, but its importance is great. Its stelae and zoomorphs, carved in the eighth century under King Cauac Sky, are an extraordinary achievement of sculpture and writing, and its story of defiance and triumph over mighty Copán gives them human drama.

For visitors, it offers a quiet and rewarding encounter with the Maya past, a clearing in the forest where giants stand in the shade. Come with curiosity, allow time to look closely at the carving and let the stones tell their story of a small city that rose to greatness.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

Why is Quiriguá a World Heritage Site?

It was inscribed in 1981 under criteria (i), (ii) and (iv) for its outstanding eighth-century monuments, which form the largest corpus of Maya art masterpieces and an essential source, through their carved stelae and sculpted calendars, for the study of Maya civilisation.

Who was Cauac Sky?

Cauac Sky, or K’ak’ Tiliw Chan Yopaat, was the king who ruled Quiriguá from 724 to 785. He transformed the city into the capital of an autonomous and prosperous state and, in 738, defeated and captured the ruler of Copán.

How tall is the tallest stela?

Stela E is the tallest stone monument of its kind in the Americas, standing about ten metres above ground and weighing an estimated sixty-five tonnes. It is carved on all four faces.

What are the zoomorphs?

Zoomorphs are large boulders carved into the forms of mythical creatures, with human figures emerging from their mouths and surfaces covered in relief and inscriptions. They are among the most unusual sculptures of the Maya world.

How do I visit Quiriguá?

The park lies about four kilometres from the main highway in south-eastern Guatemala, roughly 200 kilometres from Guatemala City. It can be visited in half a day and is often combined with trips to Lake Izabal, the Río Dulce or the ruins of Copán in Honduras.
