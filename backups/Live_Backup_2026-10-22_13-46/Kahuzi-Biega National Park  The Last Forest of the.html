The Forest of the Largest Ape

On the western side of Lake Kivu, where the highlands of eastern Congo drop toward the great forests of the Congo Basin, a vast green wilderness rises toward two extinct volcanoes. Their names, Kahuzi and Biega, give the park its identity, and their flanks are cloaked in mist, bamboo and tall rainforest. This is Kahuzi-Biega National Park, and in its depths lives the largest primate on Earth: Grauer’s gorilla, a heavyset ape of the eastern lowland forests, whose adult males can weigh more than most human beings and who live nowhere else but in eastern Congo.

UNESCO describes the park as a vast area of primary tropical forest dominated by two spectacular extinct volcanoes, with a diverse and abundant fauna. Straddling the Albertine Rift and the Congo Basin, it is an exceptional habitat for the protection of rainforest and for the eastern lowland gorilla, and it protects the most important world population of this subspecies. It also shelters more mammal species than any other site in the Albertine Rift.

But Kahuzi-Biega is also one of the most threatened World Heritage sites on Earth. It has been on the List of World Heritage in Danger since 1997, a casualty of war, refugee crises, poaching and illegal mining, and its gorillas have declined catastrophically. This post tells both stories: the extraordinary natural treasure and the struggle to save it. It is a story without a settled ending.

Where the Rift Meets the Congo Basin

Kahuzi-Biega occupies a remarkable position. The Albertine Rift, the western branch of the East African Rift system, runs along the eastern edge of the Congo, a line of mountains, lakes and volcanoes that is one of the richest areas for biodiversity in Africa. To the west the land falls away into the lowland rainforest of the Congo Basin, the second-largest tropical forest on Earth. The park straddles this boundary, and its altitude ranges from about 700 metres in the lowlands to more than 3,300 metres on the summit of Mount Kahuzi.

Kahuzi rises to 3,308 metres and Biega to 2,790 metres. The slopes of these dormant volcanoes carry a series of habitats, from lowland rainforest through Afromontane forest and extensive bamboo thickets to small areas of subalpine grassland and heath near the summits. This mosaic of habitats, packed into a relatively small altitude range, explains the park’s extraordinary variety of life.

A Park in Two Parts

The park was established in 1970 on the initiative of the Belgian photographer and conservationist Adrien Deschryver, who had come to appreciate the gorillas of the highland forest, and it was later extended into the vast lowland forest to the west, reaching its present area of about 6,000 square kilometres. The two parts are often distinguished as the highland sector, around the volcanoes near Bukavu, and the much larger lowland sector, which stretches toward the Congo Basin.

The distinction matters for conservation. The highland sector, closer to the city and more accessible, was historically the base for tourism and for the habituation of gorilla families. The lowland sector, vast and remote, contains most of the park’s area and, historically, most of its gorillas, but it has also been the part most exposed to armed groups, mining and poaching.

Grauer’s Gorilla: The Giant of the Forest

The eastern lowland gorilla, known scientifically as Gorilla beringei graueri, is the largest of the gorilla subspecies and the largest living primate. UNESCO notes that it is endemic to the Democratic Republic of the Congo and that the most important world population uses the mosaic of habitats found in the park. Adult males, known as silverbacks for the pale saddle of fur on their backs, lead family groups of females and young and can weigh well over 150 kilograms.

Gorillas are gentle herbivores, feeding on leaves, shoots, fruit and bamboo, and they live in stable social groups held together by the silverback. In the highland sector a small number of families were habituated to the presence of people so that visitors could watch them at close range, an experience that made Kahuzi-Biega one of the great wildlife destinations of Central Africa. The highland gorillas are now only part of a wider population that has suffered severe losses.

More Than Gorillas: A Rift Valley Treasure

Kahuzi-Biega’s value extends far beyond its most famous resident. The park protects 136 species of mammals, which UNESCO identifies as a greater diversity than any other site in the Albertine Rift. Thirteen other primates share the forest with the gorilla, including threatened species such as the chimpanzee, the black-and-white colobus and the monkeys known as L’Hoest’s monkey and Hamlyn’s monkey. The IUCN counts fourteen primate species in all, together with two very rare genets.

The bird list is similarly rich, with some 349 species recorded, of which 42 are endemic to the Albertine Rift, and the forest shelters a large number of rare plants and small animals, some known from only a handful of specimens. IUCN regards the park as the second most important site on the Albertine Rift, after Virunga National Park, for its biodiversity, endemism and threatened species.

What UNESCO Recognised: Criterion (x)

Kahuzi-Biega was inscribed on the World Heritage List in 1980 under natural criterion (x), which recognises the most important and significant natural habitats for the in-situ conservation of biological diversity, including those with threatened species of outstanding universal value. The inscription highlighted the park’s unique rainforest habitat, the diversity of its mammals and, above all, the eastern lowland gorilla.

UNESCO considers the area of the property, about 600,000 hectares, to be large enough to maintain its fauna, which is an important consideration for wide-ranging animals such as gorillas and chimpanzees. The scale of the park is one of its greatest assets, but it is also a challenge, because a protected area this vast is extremely difficult to patrol.

Why the Park Was Placed in Danger

In the 1990s the region around Kahuzi-Biega was engulfed in crisis. The Rwandan genocide of 1994 sent huge numbers of refugees across the border into the Kivu provinces, and their settlements put intense pressure on the forest for firewood, food and land. The wars that followed in Congo, beginning in 1996 and continuing in various forms ever since, brought armed groups into the park and disrupted the work of the rangers. In 1997 the World Heritage Committee placed the park on the List of World Heritage in Danger because of political instability, the influx of refugees and increasing exploitation of wildlife.

The threats have persisted. Poaching by armed groups, illegal mining and the clearing of forest for farming have been recurring problems, and mining has been especially serious. A survey in 2011 estimated that there were 918 mining sites within the park, of which 405 involved armed groups, and the minerals taken from the forest feed supply chains far beyond Congo.

The Collapse of Grauer’s Gorilla

The most alarming consequence has been the decline of the gorillas. A comprehensive report by the Wildlife Conservation Society, Fauna and Flora International and the Congolese conservation authority, released in 2016, concluded that the population of Grauer’s gorilla had fallen by about 77 per cent since the mid-1990s, from around 17,000 individuals to about 3,800, and that the subspecies should be considered Critically Endangered. The report warned that without urgent action the gorilla might disappear from much of its range within a few years.

The decline reflects the combined effect of hunting, often for bushmeat or by armed groups, and the destruction of habitat. For the World Heritage Committee, the loss of gorillas is central, because the value for which the park was inscribed is closely tied to their presence. In 2023 the Committee remained concerned about the low populations of key species, in particular Grauer’s gorilla, compared with the time of inscription.

The People of the Forest

Kahuzi-Biega is not an empty wilderness. The forest has long been home to the Batwa, an Indigenous people who lived by hunting and gathering, and many communities around the park depend on its resources for food, fuel and income. The creation and extension of the park displaced the Batwa from forest they had inhabited, and their claims for recognition, land and compensation have been a long-standing issue in the debate about how the park should be managed.

Modern conservation increasingly recognises that protection cannot succeed without the support of local people. Programmes to involve communities in management, to provide alternatives to forest exploitation and to include Indigenous knowledge are essential, and they are part of the challenge of securing the future of the park in a region where poverty and insecurity are deep.

Efforts to Recover and the Committee’s Decisions

The Congolese Institute for Nature Conservation, ICCN, manages the park with the support of international partners, including the German development bank KfW and conservation organisations. At times security operations by the Congolese armed forces have cleared some armed groups and allowed the reopening of ranger posts in the lowland sector, and wildlife surveys have been undertaken to determine the size of gorilla and chimpanzee populations.

The World Heritage Committee has kept the park under close review. Its decisions have repeatedly asked for the results of a wildlife inventory and for the development of indicators for the Desired state of conservation for the removal of the property from the Danger List. In 2024 it regretted that Congo had not submitted a state of conservation report on its four endangered properties. In 2026, at its 48th session in Busan, the Committee decided to maintain Kahuzi-Biega on the List of World Heritage in Danger, strongly encouraged the international community to support efforts to achieve lasting security and asked for a new report by 1 February 2027.

A Park Within a Conflict

Armed groups remain the greatest threat. The IUCN’s 2025 Conservation Outlook describes armed militia as the most pressing challenge, operating within the park and engaging in poaching, illegal mining and deforestation, while the Congolese army works with park staff and local leaders to track groups that take refuge in the forest. The security situation in the Kivu provinces remains volatile, and the resources available for conservation fall far short of what the park requires.

The park’s story shows how closely biodiversity and security are linked. No amount of scientific planning can protect a forest where rangers cannot work safely. Lasting recovery will depend as much on peace and development in the region as on conservation measures inside the park, and the international community has a role to play in supporting both.

Visiting Kahuzi-Biega: An Honest Note

In calmer times, visitors travelled to the highland sector from Bukavu to track habituated gorilla families in the forest, with guided treks that were among the most memorable wildlife experiences in Africa. Today the situation is very different. The security situation in South Kivu is unstable, many governments advise against travel to the region, and the number of habituated gorilla families has fallen. Anyone considering a visit must consult current official travel advice and local sources, and should not travel without reliable confirmation that access is possible and safe.

For those who want to see wild gorillas, other destinations in the region, such as the mountain gorilla parks of Rwanda and Uganda, offer well-established and safer options, while supporting conservation organisations working in Congo is another way to help Kahuzi-Biega. The park’s future will depend on the support of people who may never visit it.

Reflection — Protection Is a Promise Kept by People

Kahuzi-Biega shows in stark terms what the phrase “in danger” means. It is not a metaphor. The gorillas that once numbered in the tens of thousands have dwindled to a few thousand, and the people who guard them work under threat. Yet the Danger List was never intended as a verdict. It is a call for help, a way of focusing international attention and resources on a place whose loss would diminish the whole planet.

The park belongs with the great wildlife sanctuaries of Africa, such as the Serengeti National Park, Tanzania, but it faces a far harder struggle. The Serengeti has the advantage of relative peace; Kahuzi-Biega must carry its heritage through war. That makes the efforts of its rangers and its supporters all the more remarkable, and the world’s responsibility to them all the greater.

✦ Conclusion

Kahuzi-Biega National Park is a place of extraordinary natural wealth and extraordinary vulnerability. Its rainforests, its volcanoes and above all its giant gorillas make it one of the great natural treasures of Africa, and the sharp decline of those gorillas shows how much is at stake. Its status on the List of World Heritage in Danger is a reflection of the crisis the park faces and a call to action.

There is still hope. The park survives, its rangers continue to work, and the international community has repeatedly renewed its commitment. The future of Grauer’s gorilla and its forest will depend on security, on the support of local communities and on a world that decides this heritage is worth saving.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

Why is Kahuzi-Biega a World Heritage Site?

It was inscribed in 1980 under criterion (x) for its primary tropical rainforest and its biodiversity, including the most important world population of the eastern lowland gorilla and a greater diversity of mammal species than any other site in the Albertine Rift.

Is Kahuzi-Biega on the List of World Heritage in Danger?

Yes. It has been on the Danger List since 1997 because of armed conflict, an influx of refugees, poaching and illegal mining. The World Heritage Committee decided to maintain it on the list at its 48th session in 2026.

What is Grauer’s gorilla?

Grauer’s gorilla, also called the eastern lowland gorilla, is the largest gorilla subspecies and the largest living primate. It is found only in eastern Congo and is classed as Critically Endangered, with a population that fell by about 77 per cent between the mid-1990s and 2016.

How big is the park?

The park covers about 600,000 hectares, or 6,000 square kilometres, and includes the extinct volcanoes Mount Kahuzi (3,308 metres) and Mount Biega (2,790 metres) as well as a vast lowland rainforest.

Can I visit Kahuzi-Biega?

Access has been severely limited by insecurity in eastern Congo, and many governments advise against travel to the region. Check current official advice before considering any visit, and consider supporting conservation groups or visiting other gorilla destinations in Rwanda or Uganda.
