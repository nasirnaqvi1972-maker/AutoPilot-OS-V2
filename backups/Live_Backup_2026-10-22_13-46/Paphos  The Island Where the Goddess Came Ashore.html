The Island Where the Goddess Came Ashore

Few place names carry as much weight in the imagination of the Mediterranean as Paphos. Long before it became a modern harbour town of hotels, banana terraces and charter flights, the name belonged to a goddess. Greek poets sang that Aphrodite, risen from the sea foam, first set foot upon the shore of Cyprus, and that she made her home at Paphos, where her precinct was wreathed in incense and her altars never needed blood to be sweet. In the eighth book of the Odyssey the goddess of love slips away from the scandal of Olympus and returns to Paphos, to be bathed and anointed in her fragrant sanctuary. The island, whose waters were already the road along which copper, wine and ideas travelled between Egypt, the Levant and the Aegean, had found its patron deity, and the western end of the coast became her sacred country.

What the poets named, the archaeologists have been able to touch. The UNESCO inscription of 1980 does not describe a single monument but a serial property made up of three components in two places: the village of Kouklia, where the ancient city of Palaepaphos and the Sanctuary of Aphrodite lie, and Kato Paphos, a coastal quarter of the modern town, where the later city of Nea Paphos and the rock-cut necropolis known as the Tombs of the Kings are preserved. Together they cover some 162 hectares of the old kingdom of Paphos by the figure the World Heritage Centre lists, and they span a timeline that begins in the Bronze Age and drifts, by way of Hellenistic palaces and Roman villas, into the age of Byzantine bishops. It is rare for one inscription to hold both the birthplace of a cult and the capital that outlived it, and rarer still for the two to remain legible on the ground.

To walk through Paphos is therefore to read the long arc of Mediterranean antiquity in three registers. At Kouklia one reads the religion: an open-air altar, a sacred stone, a priest-king. At Nea Paphos one reads the politics and the daily life: a harbour city founded by a last king, governed by Ptolemies and by Roman proconsuls, adorned with mosaics for the dining rooms of its governors and merchants. And among the rocky outcrops to the north one reads death, the cool underground halls in which the Paphian elite chose to be remembered. This post follows those three registers in turn, and asks what a modern visitor, standing in the Cypriot sun with the sea beyond the ruins, can still feel of the world that made them.

The Sanctuary at Kouklia

The Cult That Preceded the City

The oldest of the property's components lies about sixteen kilometres east of modern Paphos, where the village of Kouklia has grown over the ruins of Palaepaphos, "Old Paphos". From about 1200 BC the site was a major religious centre of the eastern Mediterranean, and the worship of a great female deity became especially prominent there from the twelfth century BC, when the island was being resettled by Aegean-speaking newcomers and its older Cypriot traditions were blending with theirs. UNESCO's own account credits the Mycenaeans with the building of the temple in that century and records that it remained in use until the Roman period. Scholars have long suspected that the goddess honoured here, who in time acquired the Greek name Aphrodite, grew out of a local fertility divinity of far greater age, and the property is inscribed under criterion (vi) precisely because that cult, once rooted in Cyprus, spread into a widely celebrated symbol of love and beauty.

What strikes the visitor and the historian alike is the austerity of the original rite. No grand covered temple of the classical type was raised here: the holy altar stood in the open air, and the goddess was worshipped not in the form of a statue but as a conical stone. Ancient writers described it as a white pyramid whose material nobody could identify. The stone that is now displayed in the museum at Kouklia, which was found near the altar, has darkened with the centuries, and it is a curious experience to stand before an object so plain that it could be mistaken for a boundary marker, and to know that pilgrims once crossed the sea to touch it.

Kinyras, the Priest-King

Tradition remembered a king named Kinyras, wealthy and musical, who ruled Paphos and served as priest of Aphrodite, and some sources name him as the first of her priests. Whether or not such a man ever lived, the memory expresses a real feature of the early Cypriot kingdoms, in which royal and sacral authority were fused, and in which the sanctuary served as an instrument of political prestige. Under the Romans the stone was moved into a covered building, and Palaepaphos became the seat of the Koinon Kyprion, the federation of Cypriot cities that controlled the island's bronze coinage. The cult survived even after King Nikokles moved his capital to the coast at the end of the fourth century BC, a reminder that a sanctuary can outlast the dynasty that built it.

The decline came slowly. As Christianity spread, the altars were abandoned from the second century onwards, and the great earthquakes of the fourth century finished what indifference had begun by destroying the holy altar itself. Yet the myth proved stronger than the masonry. A short drive along the coast one finds the sea stack known as Petra tou Romiou, the Rock of the Greek, which may have inspired the legend that the goddess was born from the waves at this very spot, and which today draws travellers who have never heard of Palaepaphos but know exactly who Aphrodite was.

Siege, Palace and Fresh Discovery

Archaeology has not finished with Kouklia. The excavations of the University of St Andrews and the Liverpool City Museum between 1950 and 1955 uncovered the so-called Siege Mound, which produced weapons, architectural fragments and a statue of a priest-king. The Greek historian Herodotus describes a Persian siege of the city during the Ionian Revolt in 498 and 497 BC, and the mound was long read as the very ramp the besiegers built; more recent work by the University of Cyprus, begun in 2006, proposes instead that it is the debris of an acropolis destroyed in the early fifth century BC. A royal palace has been identified on the Hadjiaptoullas plateau, a great storage and industrial complex came to light there in 2016, and towered acropolis walls were revealed in 2025. Palaepaphos, in other words, is not a finished chapter but a living excavation, and each campaign makes the old city a little less mythical and a little more political.

The Last King and the New City

Nikokles Looks to the Sea

By the end of the fourth century BC, the politics of the eastern Mediterranean had been rearranged by the generals of Alexander the Great, and the small kingdoms of Cyprus found themselves pulled between rival successors. Nikokles, the last ruler of the Cypriot city-kingdom of Paphos, who died in 306 BC, is credited with the decision that created the second Paphos. He founded, or at least began, a new city on the coast, a short distance to the north-west of the old sanctuary town, on a low and sheltered shore with a natural harbour. Palaepaphos had a sacred past, but Nea Paphos, "New Paphos", was designed for the future: a place from which ships could be built, launched and defended, and from which a dynasty could trade with Egypt and with the Aegean world.

The new foundation was populated partly by people moved from the city of Marion by Ptolemy I Soter, the Macedonian general who had seized Egypt and who wished to bind Cyprus to his own fortunes. The detail is revealing. Nea Paphos was not a quiet planting of colonists but an act of imperial engineering, in which a native king and a foreign master cooperated to build a port that served both of them. The theatre, which the University of Sydney has been excavating since 1995, belongs to the end of the fourth century BC and shows how quickly the city assumed the cultural trappings of a Greek polis.

A Capital Under Ptolemies and Rome

The Ptolemies made Nea Paphos a major shipbuilding centre, and in the second century BC it replaced Salamis as the administrative capital of the whole island. When Rome took possession of Cyprus in 58 BC and formally constituted it as a province in 22 BC, Nea Paphos remained the seat of government, and it was in these centuries that the city acquired the monuments that make it so rewarding today. The agora, a civic and commercial square of about two and a half hectares enclosed by porticoes, was given its formal layout in the second century BC, and was first excavated by Kyriakos Nicolaou between 1975 and 1978. The Odeon, a small semicircular theatre of the second century AD, offered some twelve hundred seats arranged in twelve rows, and could host recitals and council meetings in a setting that still feels intimate when one sits in the stone.

It is easy to forget, among the columns, that Paphos also held a place in the story of early Christianity. The Acts of the Apostles records that Paul and Barnabas crossed Cyprus to Paphos, where they encountered the Roman proconsul Sergius Paulus; the journey placed the city on the route along which a small Jewish movement began to turn into a Mediterranean religion. A later bishopric and an Early Christian basilica, known today as Limeniotissa, mark the way in which the pagan capital slowly became a Christian one.

The Mosaics of the Villas

Floors That Tell the Myths

If a single reason had to be chosen for the global fame of Kato Paphos, it would be its floors. UNESCO describes the mosaics of the villas as among the most beautiful in the world, and they bring together Greek gods, goddesses and heroes with scenes of everyday life, covering the span from the Hellenistic to the Byzantine age. Criterion (iii) recognises that tradition, together with the villas, palaces, fortresses and rock-cut tombs of exceptional historical value, as a unique testimony to the civilisation of Cyprus. The mosaics are not mere decoration: they are the surviving trace of a society that thought in pictures, and that placed the stories of its heroes beneath its feet where guests would read them as they reclined to dine.

The House of Dionysos, a residence of the third century AD of about two thousand square metres, is the grandest of the group, and takes its name from the wine god who dominates many of its panels in scenes of vintage, revel and rustic abundance. The Villa of Theseus, built in stages from the early third century, is best known for the mosaic in which the Athenian hero grapples with the Minotaur, a scene that crosses from the island of Crete to the island of Cyprus as easily as a trade route. The House of Aion preserves a large central mosaic of the deity of time and eternity, and the House of Orpheus, also of the third century, holds more of this mythic repertoire, although its mosaics are at present not on public view.

The Stones of Civic Life

Theatre, Odeon and Agora

Beyond the villas, the archaeological park of Kato Paphos presents the ordinary and extraordinary infrastructure of an ancient capital. The agora was the commercial and political heart of the city, and its porticoes sheltered merchants, magistrates and the occasional philosopher in the shade; the visitor who walks its perimeter can sense the rhythm of a place where news, grain and gossip changed hands. A structure associated with the healing god Asclepius is described within the agora complex, and the park's formal list of monuments includes an Asklepieion, which gives a hint of the sacred landscape that the Greek city shared with its Roman successors.

The remains that command the most attention from afar are those of Saranta Kolones, the "Forty Columns" castle, whose name is taken from the many granite columns that were once reused in its walls. The fortress, built in the Byzantine period, did not survive the great earthquake that struck the island in the early thirteenth century, and its ruined towers now hold the eye as one approaches the harbour. Near them lies the Odeon, and between the two the visitor feels how the city layered monuments one over another, as a living town does, without ever tidying the past.

The Tombs of the Kings

About two kilometres north of the harbour, the landscape changes from ruins above ground to chambers below it. The Tombs of the Kings, a necropolis of the Hellenistic and Roman ages, were carved into the solid rock of the coastal plateau, and many of them date to the fourth century BC, with burials continuing into the third century AD. The name is a misnomer, since no kings were buried here: the tombs belonged to Paphian aristocrats and high officials, and the title reflects only the magnificence of the monuments, some of which have Doric columns and frescoed walls, and some of which imitate the houses of the living. A publication has connected one of them, Tomb 8, to the Ptolemaic dynasty and to the burial of Cyprus's last king, who is said to have taken his own life when the Romans annexed the island.

The effect is of a city of the dead organised on the model of the city of the living. Stairways descend into open courts, and the court gives access to chambers carved in the likeness of domestic rooms, so that the dead might continue their domestic existence in a form of perpetual home. The Tombs of the Kings recall other rock-cut necropolises of the Hellenistic East, among them the tomb façades of Petra, Jordan, though the Paphian monuments are less a display to the passer-by than a descent into private space. In the same way that the tombs and temples of Ancient Thebes, Egypt enclosed the power of the pharaohs in the west bank cliffs, the tombs of Paphos encase a provincial aristocracy that served the Ptolemies, the heirs of those same pharaohs.

The Fall of a Capital and the Long Afterlife

The great earthquakes of the fourth century AD brought Nea Paphos to a crisis. A series of destructive tremors shattered the city, and the administrative capital of Cyprus was moved back to Salamis on the eastern coast. Paphos was not abandoned. It was rebuilt as an Early Christian bishopric, and it survived into the Byzantine era, a quieter and smaller town that gathered around its basilicas and its harbour. The medieval fortress of Saranta Kolones joined the skyline, and later centuries added the churches and village life of the Cypriot countryside, until the ancient city had become a field of stones beneath fields of crops.

Modern excavation made the discovery. Cypriot and international missions, among them the University of Sydney at the theatre, gradually uncovered the villas, the agora and the necropolis, and the Department of Antiquities has presided over their protection. The meaning of the place changed with each campaign, and the post-war decades, with their expanding tourism and their new understanding of the island's role in the ancient world, made Paphos one of the best-known archaeological landscapes of the Mediterranean. In 2017 the town even bore the title of European Capital of Culture, a distinction that placed its ancient heritage at the heart of a contemporary festival.

Conservation and Guardianship

The property is protected under the national Antiquities Law, and most of the site is owned by the government. There is no formal buffer zone, but the law allows the designation of "Controlled Areas" around the monuments to regulate the height and style of nearby building, a vital provision in a coastline where the pressures of tourism are heavy. In November 2010 the property received the status of enhanced protection under the Second Protocol to the Hague Convention, which places it among the cultural properties of greatest importance to humanity in the event of armed conflict. The Department of Antiquities manages the property, and a Master Plan for Kato Paphos has been in force since 1991, with a corresponding plan for Palaepaphos in the process of implementation.

The mosaics demand constant attention. A conservation project run with the Getty Conservation Institute ended in 2004, and the Department of Antiquities began a broader programme of mosaic conservation in 2011. UNESCO's own assessment is that the property retains a high degree of authenticity in its locations, forms, materials and built fabric, but it also observes that a comprehensive management plan is still needed to coordinate the work of so many hands. That frankness is a useful corrective to the sentimental view of heritage as something simply handed down; here, as in the great sites of Athens, the past survives only because of the daily labour of the living.

Reflection

There is a quiet paradox at the centre of Paphos. It is a place whose fame rests on a goddess and her stone, yet the stone is plain, the altar open to the sky, and the temple that Mycenaeans may have raised is a ruin that requires imagination as much as eyesight. The grandest things in the property are the least sacred, the villas and tombs of men and women who wanted to be remembered, while the holiest object is a pebble-like cone that a careless visitor might pass without a glance. The mistake, perhaps, is to think of that contrast as a puzzle. The ancient world did not distinguish as sharply as we do between devotion and display, between a rite and a banquet, between the gods of the floor mosaics and the goddess of the altar.

✦ Also Read

Readers drawn to the Hellenistic and Roman layers of Paphos will find a natural companion in Ancient Thebes with its Necropolis, Egypt, where the Ptolemaic dynasty that ruled Cyprus presented itself to Egyptian subjects as the heir of the pharaohs. The rock-cut tombs of the Theban west bank offer a revealing contrast with the Paphian necropolis, and the story of the temples at Karnak and Luxor shows how the same royal house worked through religion on the Nile as it did through harbours and governors on the island.

Those who wish to follow the thread of the Greek gods will enjoy Acropolis, Athens, Greece, the sanctuary of Athena on its rock above the city, where the same pantheon that gave Aphrodite her island was honoured in marble.

✦ Conclusion

Paphos endures as one of the great meeting places of myth and archaeology in the Mediterranean. Its three components tell a single story in three voices: the voice of a cult that began in the Bronze Age and became a universal image of love, the voice of a Hellenistic and Roman capital that built its power on harbour and fleet, and the voice of an aristocracy that carved its memory into the rock. UNESCO's recognition of the property under criteria (iii) and (vi) captures that doubleness, a unique testimony to a civilisation and a tradition of belief that travelled far beyond Cyprus.

The challenge of the property today is the challenge of any treasure that lives beside a thriving resort: to keep faith with the past without shutting the present out. The mosaics must be roofed, the tombs must be walked with care, and the sanctuary at Kouklia must be allowed to keep its austere dignity. For the traveller willing to go slowly, to drive the road between Kato Paphos and the sanctuary, to sit in the Odeon and to look at the sacred stone, the reward is a rare feeling of continuity, the sense that the island still holds, beneath its beaches and orchards, the memory of the goddess who is said to have come ashore here.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

1. When did Paphos become a UNESCO World Heritage Site?

Paphos was inscribed on the World Heritage List in 1980 as a cultural property under criteria (iii) and (vi). The reference number is 79, and the property is a serial one comprising three components at two sites, Kato Paphos and Kouklia.

2. What exactly is included in the World Heritage property?

The property consists of ancient Nea Paphos and the Tombs of the Kings necropolis at Kato Paphos, together with the Temple of Aphrodite and the ancient city of Palaepaphos at Kouklia. The most famous features are the Roman mosaics of the Houses of Dionysos, Aion and Orpheus and the Villa of Theseus.

3. Why is Paphos linked with Aphrodite?

Ancient tradition held that the goddess of love came ashore on Cyprus and made Paphos her home. The sanctuary at Palaepaphos, where she was worshipped in the form of a conical stone beside an open-air altar, was in use from about the twelfth century BC into the Roman period, and the cult became a celebrated symbol of love and beauty.

4. Were kings really buried in the Tombs of the Kings?

No. The name reflects the grandeur of the rock-cut tombs, which were used by Paphian aristocrats and high officials from the fourth century BC onwards. Only one tomb has been tentatively linked in a publication with the Ptolemaic dynasty.

5. Is Paphos worth visiting for a day or two?

Yes. The Archaeological Park of Kato Paphos and the Tombs of the Kings can be seen in a single long morning or afternoon, and a second half-day is enough for the sanctuary and museum at Kouklia. Many visitors also combine the visit with a stop at the sea stack of Petra tou Romiou.
