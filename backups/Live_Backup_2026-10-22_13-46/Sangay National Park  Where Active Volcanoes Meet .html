Sangay National Park — Where Active Volcanoes Meet the Amazon

A Wilderness of Glaciers, Moorland, Cloud Forest and Rainforest in the Central Ecuadorian Andes, Dominated by the Perfect Cone of Sangay, One of the World’s Most Continuously Active Volcanoes, and Home to Mountain Tapirs, Spectacled Bears and Andean Condors, Once Listed Among World Heritage in Danger | UNESCO World Heritage Site

Location:Central Ecuador, where the eastern Andes fall toward the Amazon basin, in the provinces of Chimborazo, Tungurahua and Morona Santiago

A Volcano That Never Rests

In the heart of Ecuador, where the eastern wall of the Andes drops toward the vast green basin of the Amazon, a mountain smokes. Sangay is a near-perfect cone of ash and lava, rising to about 5,200 metres, and it has been erupting, with brief pauses, for most of the last century. At night its summit glows red, and its plumes of ash and steam drift east over forests that have never seen a road. It is one of the most active volcanoes on Earth, and it is the centrepiece of one of Ecuador’s great wildernesses.

UNESCO describes Sangay National Park as illustrating, within its 270,000 hectares, the entire spectrum of Ecuador’s ecosystems, with outstanding natural beauty and two active volcanoes. Within a few kilometres, the visitor can pass from glaciers and bare volcanic ash to moorlands, cloud forests and lowland rainforest. It is a landscape of extremes, where fire and ice meet, and where the processes that create new land are visible every day.

Sangay is also one of the few World Heritage sites that has been placed on the Danger List and then successfully removed from it. Between 1992 and 2005 the park was listed as in danger, largely because of a road built through its heart, and its recovery shows how international attention can help change decisions on the ground. This post explores the volcanoes, the wildlife, the road and the lessons of the park.

Where the Andes Meet the Amazon

The park lies in central Ecuador, spread across the provinces of Chimborazo, Tungurahua and Morona Santiago, on the eastern flank of the Andes. It was first protected in 1975 as a wildlife reserve, became a national park in 1979 and was inscribed on the World Heritage List in 1983. The World Heritage property covers 271,925 hectares, and in 1992 the park itself was extended to the south, though the extension was not added to the World Heritage property, so that the property now covers about half of the national park.

The park’s altitude range is extraordinary, from tropical lowland forest at the foot of the eastern slopes to glacier-capped volcanic summits above 5,000 metres. That vertical span, packed into a small area, creates one of the world’s most complex series of ecological habitats, which UNESCO recognises under criterion (vii). Few places on Earth allow one to climb from rainforest to ice in a single day.

Three Volcanoes

The landscape is dominated by three great volcanoes. In the north-west stands Tungurahua, at about 5,000 metres, which has erupted repeatedly since 1999 and has forced the evacuation of the nearby town of Baños more than once. Nearby is El Altar, a massive collapsed volcano whose broken crater holds a lake and whose jagged rim is one of the most spectacular sights in the Ecuadorian Andes. And in the centre of the park rises Sangay.

Sangay is the most remarkable. According to UNESCO it is notable globally for its long period of continuous activity, and the current cycle, which began in the 1930s, shows no sign of ending. Its regular explosions throw ash and incandescent rocks down its steep slopes, and its lava flows have built the cone into a steep, almost symmetrical shape. Because the mountain is so remote, its activity is watched mainly by scientists and by satellites, and by the very few climbers who dare approach it.

The Ecological Staircase

The park’s habitats are arranged like a staircase down the mountains. At the top are glaciers and bare volcanic ash, where only lichens and a few hardy plants survive. Below them stretches the páramo, the cold, windy high-altitude grassland of the northern Andes, a landscape of tussock grass, cushion plants and small lakes that acts as a sponge, soaking up rain and mist and releasing water slowly to the rivers below.

Further down the slopes, the páramo gives way to stunted high-altitude forest, then to cloud forest, in which every branch is draped with mosses, bromeliads and orchids, and finally to the lowland rainforest of the Amazon basin. Each zone has its own species, and the transitions between them are among the richest in biodiversity on the planet. UNESCO also recognises the park under criterion (ix) because the active volcano keeps ecological succession going, as new ash and lava are colonised by plants and animals.

A Botanical Treasure House

The flora of Sangay is poorly known, but it is certainly rich. Studies cited by conservation organisations suggest that at least 3,000 plant species occur in the park, probably with a high degree of endemism, particularly in the páramo and the cloud forests, where isolated mountain slopes have allowed plants to evolve in isolation. Orchids, ferns, bromeliads and the strange giant rosette plants of the páramo are among the glories.

This botanical wealth is not merely a curiosity. The páramo and cloud forests regulate the flow of water to communities and farms in the highlands and the Amazon lowlands, and they store large amounts of carbon in their soils and vegetation. The protection of the park is therefore part of the protection of the water and climate on which millions of people depend.

Tapirs, Bears and Condors

The animals of Sangay match its plants in variety. In the highlands live the Andean mountain tapir, a shy, woolly relative of the lowland tapirs and one of the most endangered large mammals of South America, and the spectacled bear, the only bear species in South America, named for the pale markings around its eyes. Pumas hunt in the páramo and forest, and Andean condors, the great vultures with three-metre wingspans, ride the thermals above the ridges.

The park is also a hotspot for birds, with hummingbirds, tanagers, toucans and many species restricted to the Andean slopes, and for amphibians, insects and small mammals, many of which are still being discovered. In recent years scientists have described a new species of porcupine from the park, an indication of how much remains to be learned about its fauna. The combination of habitats and isolation means that Sangay is among the most important natural treasures of the tropical Andes.

People of the Mountains

Although much of the park is uninhabited, people live around its edges. Highland communities, many of them Indigenous Kichwa, farm and graze animals on the slopes below the park, and in the eastern lowlands other communities depend on the forest. For them the mountains are both a source of water and a place of cultural significance, and the park’s management has to take their needs into account.

Grazing, burning of the páramo and hunting have been persistent pressures. The challenge is to work with communities so that livelihoods are secured without damaging the ecosystems on which they depend. The story of Sangay’s Danger List years shows how essential that cooperation is.

The Guamote–Macas Road

The crisis that placed Sangay on the Danger List was a road. The Guamote–Macas road, intended to link the highlands with the Amazon city of Macas, was built through the park, cutting across its heart. Roads in wilderness areas are notoriously damaging: they open the land to settlers, hunters and loggers, fragment habitats and allow invasive species and fire to spread. In 1992 the World Heritage Committee placed the park on the List of World Heritage in Danger, citing poaching, boundary encroachment and unplanned road construction, as well as overgrazing.

The Committee kept the park on the list for thirteen years, and the listing proved surprisingly useful. In 2000 the Ecuadorian Minister for the Environment told a workshop that the inclusion of Sangay on the Danger List had helped his ministry in its negotiations with the Ministry of Public Works and other government bodies, giving it the leverage to obtain resources to assess the road’s environmental impacts and plan mitigation. It was an example of the Danger List working as a tool for cooperation rather than as a punishment.

Off the Danger List: A Success Story

Slowly, the situation improved. A four-year project funded by the United Nations Foundation and run with UNESCO and IUCN made Sangay one of ten pilot sites for a monitoring system, with indicators and benchmarks that could show when the park was safe enough to be removed from the Danger List. Management improved, patrols increased, and the road was gradually brought under control.

In 2005 the World Heritage Committee decided to remove Sangay from the List of World Heritage in Danger. The State Party reported that the areas affected by human intervention such as hunting, mountain climbing and livestock made up only two or three per cent of the park, and that the factors that had led to the danger listing had either been addressed or affected only a very small part of the property or its buffer zone. The case remains one of the most positive stories in the history of natural World Heritage.

What UNESCO Recognised: Four Natural Criteria

Sangay was inscribed on the World Heritage List in 1983 under all four natural criteria, a distinction shared by only a handful of sites. Criterion (vii) recognised the park as one of the world’s most complex series of ecological habitats and a landscape of outstanding beauty; criterion (viii) recognised the geological importance of Sangay, a perfect cone-shaped volcano notable for its long period of continuous activity; criterion (ix) recognised the ongoing ecological and biological processes driven by volcanic activity; and criterion (x) recognised its importance for biodiversity and threatened species.

The property is a national park of Ecuador, protected by national law and managed by the environment ministry. The 1992 extension to the south brought the total park area to more than 500,000 hectares, though the extra land is outside the World Heritage boundary and includes private properties, a distinction that remains important for management.

Living With Volcanoes

The volcanoes are both the park’s glory and its hazard. Tungurahua has erupted repeatedly since 1999, sending ash over nearby towns and forcing the evacuation of communities, and ash from Sangay has fallen on farms and pastures to the east. Ecuador’s scientists, notably the national geophysical institute, monitor the volcanoes with seismometers and satellite data and issue warnings so that communities can prepare.

For the park, these eruptions are part of the natural process that shapes its ecosystems, burying forests in ash and giving rise to new growth. They also remind visitors that they are in a living landscape. Anyone planning to travel near the volcanoes should check the latest volcanic alerts and follow the guidance of local authorities.

Visiting Sangay

Sangay is one of the least visited World Heritage sites in South America, which is part of its appeal. Access is mostly from the highland city of Riobamba, from the spa town of Baños beneath Tungurahua or from Macas on the Amazon side, and most of the park is reachable only on foot. Trekkers can explore the páramo, the lakes and the slopes of El Altar, and climbers sometimes attempt the remote and dangerous ascent of Sangay itself, which demands experience, a guide and favourable volcanic conditions.

The best time for walking is generally the drier months, though mountain weather is unpredictable at any time of year. Visitors should be prepared for cold, rain and altitude, carry proper equipment and use local guides, who know the routes and conditions. As the volcanoes are active, always check current alerts and park regulations before travelling.

Reflection — A Road, a Listing and a Lesson

The story of Sangay shows that international heritage designation can matter in ways that go beyond prestige. When the World Heritage Committee placed the park on the Danger List, it gave a government minister an argument to use in negotiations with his colleagues, and it brought attention and money to a place that had few advocates. The listing was not a verdict but a lever, and it was used effectively.

It is a reassuring story in a world of environmental bad news, and it reminds us that decisions about roads, dams and mines are made by people who can be persuaded. Ecuador is a country whose natural heritage is among the richest on Earth, from the Galapagos Islands, Ecuador to the Amazon, and Sangay stands beside them as a mainland masterpiece. Its Andean neighbours, such as the Historic Sanctuary of Machu Picchu, Peru, share the same mountain chain and the same need for careful stewardship.

✦ Conclusion

Sangay National Park is a place where the Earth is still at work. Its volcanoes smoke, its ice melts into moorland streams, its forests drip with moss and its rainforests teem with life. UNESCO recognised its importance in 1983 under all four natural criteria, and the years it spent on the Danger List showed how vulnerable even a remote wilderness can be.

Its removal from the list in 2005 is proof that conservation can succeed when science, government and international attention work together. The park still faces pressures, but it also has a record of recovery, and for those who take the trouble to visit, it offers one of the most dramatic natural experiences in the Andes.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

Why is Sangay National Park a World Heritage Site?

It was inscribed in 1983 under all four natural criteria, (vii), (viii), (ix) and (x). UNESCO describes it as illustrating within 270,000 hectares the whole spectrum of Ecuador’s ecosystems, with outstanding natural beauty and two active volcanoes, Sangay and Tungurahua.

Was Sangay on the List of World Heritage in Danger?

Yes. It was listed in danger from 1992 to 2005, mainly because of the construction of the Guamote–Macas road through the park, together with poaching, boundary encroachment and overgrazing. The Committee removed it from the list in 2005.

How active is Sangay volcano?

Sangay is notable globally for its long period of continuous activity. Its current eruptive cycle has lasted since the 1930s, making it one of the most active volcanoes in the world.

What animals live in the park?

The park shelters Andean mountain tapirs, spectacled bears, pumas, Andean condors and a great variety of birds, amphibians and small mammals, including recently described species such as a new porcupine.

Can I visit Sangay National Park?

Yes, but access is mostly on foot from Riobamba, Baños or Macas. Trekking around El Altar and the páramo is popular, while the ascent of Sangay volcano is remote and dangerous. Use local guides and check volcanic alerts before travelling.

🧳 Visitor's Guide

Best Time to Visit — The drier months for trekking; mountain weather is unpredictable year round

Getting There — By road to Riobamba, Baños or Macas, the main gateways to different parts of the park

Time Needed — Several days for a trek around El Altar; longer expeditions for the remote areas

Guides — Use local guides, especially for volcano routes and high-altitude trails

What to Bring — Warm waterproof clothing, sturdy boots, sun protection, a tent for multi-day treks and plenty of water

Altitude — Most trails are above 3,000 metres; allow time to acclimatise to avoid altitude sickness
