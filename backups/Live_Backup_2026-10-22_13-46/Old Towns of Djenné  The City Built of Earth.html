An Island of Earth in the Sahel

Djenné lies on a low island in the floodplain between the Niger and Bani rivers, in the inland delta of central Mali, where the waters spread across the plain each year and the Sahel briefly turns green. Seen from a distance in the dry season, the town appears as a low silhouette of earth-coloured buildings, rising to the three conical towers of its great mosque. Everything here, from the walls of houses to the minarets of the mosque, is built of mud.

This is no humble material in Djenné’s hands. The builders have turned earth into an architecture of sophistication and beauty, with tall façades articulated by pilasters, buttresses and rows of projecting wooden beams, and with delicately worked parapets and doorways. The result is a town that looks as if it were carved from a single block of clay, glowing orange and gold in the light of dawn and dusk.

UNESCO inscribed the Old Towns of Djenné in 1988 under criteria (iii) and (iv), recognising the exceptional testimony of the town’s architecture and its long history as a trading and Islamic learning centre. The property includes the living town and three archaeological sites, Djenné-Djeno, Hambarkétolo and Tonomba, and together they trace urban life in this region over two thousand years.

Djenné-Djeno — An Ancient City Without Walls

Two kilometres from the present town lies a low mound covered with fragments of pottery. This is Djenné-Djeno, or Old Djenné, a settlement that was occupied from about the third century BCE and flourished for more than a thousand years before being gradually abandoned around the fourteenth century. Excavations carried out from the late 1970s by the archaeologists Susan and Roderick McIntosh showed that it grew into a substantial town, with a population possibly of several thousand people, a surrounding wall of mud-brick and a network of satellite settlements.

The discovery was important because it overturned old assumptions. For a long time, scholars had believed that urbanism in sub-Saharan Africa arrived from outside, through trans-Saharan trade and Islam. Djenné-Djeno showed that complex towns developed here earlier and independently, based on rice and millet farming, fishing, iron working and the trade of local goods. Artefacts such as terracotta figurines, pottery, ironwork and glass beads reveal a creative and interconnected society.

The city’s eventual decline is not fully understood, but changes in climate, trade routes and politics, as well as the rise of the new town of Djenné nearby, probably contributed. What remains is a fragile archaeological landscape, threatened by erosion, looting and the passage of time, and a reminder of how much of Africa’s urban history remains to be discovered.

A Trading City and a Centre of Learning

The present town of Djenné was probably founded around the eleventh or twelfth century, and quickly became one of the most important commercial centres of West Africa. Its position, at the head of the navigable waters of the Niger and Bani, made it a natural meeting point for traders bringing gold from the forest regions to the south and salt from the Sahara to the north, and for boats carrying grain, cloth, kola nuts and fish along the river. Camel caravans and canoes met here, and its markets were famous across the region.

Djenné became also a great centre of Islamic scholarship, with Qur’anic schools and a community of scholars whose reputation rivalled that of Timbuktu, with which it was closely linked. Tradition holds that its ruler, Koi Konboro, converted to Islam in the thirteenth century and turned his palace into the first mosque, an act that gave the town its religious and architectural identity. The mosque and the market have remained the twin centres of life in Djenné ever since.

The town passed through the hands of successive powers. It was conquered by Sonni Ali of the Songhai Empire in 1473 after a long siege, by the Moroccans who invaded in 1591, and later by Fulani rulers in the nineteenth century, and then by the French at the end of the nineteenth. Through all these changes, its trading and scholarly traditions endured, and its architecture continued to evolve.

The Great Mosque — A Building Remade Every Year

The mosque seen today dates from 1907, when it was rebuilt under the direction of the master mason Ismaïla Traoré, on the site of earlier mosques. It stands on a raised platform, about seventy-five metres on each side, to protect it from floods, and is built of sun-dried mud bricks and a mud plaster, with a framework of palm wood. Three massive minarets rise above the prayer hall’s eastern wall, and the façade is decorated with vertical buttresses crowned with pointed pinnacles.

The Crépissage Festival and the Masons’ Guilds

The mud walls need constant maintenance. Rain and humidity erode the plaster, and cracks appear, so each year, in the dry season, the entire town takes part in a festival of replastering, known as the crépissage. Teams of masons, organised in hereditary guilds, climb the projecting wooden beams, called toron, which serve as permanent scaffolding, while young men prepare mud and women and children carry water. The festival is part ritual, part celebration and part civic duty, and it embodies the idea that heritage is something a community makes anew, year after year.

Houses, Markets and Everyday Life

The mosque is the most celebrated monument, but the old town as a whole is the real treasure. Djenné contains hundreds of houses in the traditional style, built of mud brick with plastered façades, often two storeys high, with flat roofs, small windows and elaborate doorways. Many are decorated with pilasters and geometric patterns, and the finest, associated with wealthy merchant families, show a clear influence from North African and Moroccan models, as well as from local traditions.

The town’s heart is the market square in front of the mosque, which comes alive each Monday when traders from the surrounding villages arrive by canoe, donkey cart and truck. Fish, millet, cloth, pottery, livestock and every kind of household good are on sale, and the combination of the crowd and the mosque’s towering façade creates one of the most memorable urban scenes in Africa. For most visitors, the market is the best way to feel the town’s living spirit.

The people of Djenné, including Songhai, Bozo, Fulani and Bambara communities, share a long tradition of coexistence, and their families have for generations been bound to particular crafts, among them masonry, weaving and fishing. This social fabric, as much as the buildings, is a vital part of the town’s heritage.

Danger, Conservation and the Future

Earthen architecture is as fragile as it is beautiful. Heavy rains, floods and rising groundwater erode the walls, and the pressure of modern building materials, new construction and changing lifestyles threatens the traditional building tradition. Many old houses have been lost or altered, and conservation efforts have sought to support owners and masons in maintaining them with traditional techniques.

Security and political instability in Mali have added a further layer of risk. Since the crisis that began in 2012, the country’s heritage has suffered, and in 2016 the Old Towns of Djenné were placed on the List of World Heritage in Danger, along with several other Malian sites. Limited access, reduced tourism income and the difficulty of supporting conservation work have made the task of protecting the town more difficult, and readers should check current information about the situation.

Despite these challenges, the community’s commitment remains strong. The replastering festival continues, local masons train new generations, and national and international partners support restoration work. The experience of Djenné shows that heritage survives best when the people who live with it have the means and the will to care for it.

Djenné and Timbuktu — Sister Cities of the Sahel

Djenné’s story cannot be separated from that of Timbuktu, the desert-edge city to the north. Goods flowed between them along the Niger: gold, grain and slaves travelling north, salt, cloth and books moving south, and the cities formed a commercial and intellectual axis that sustained the great West African empires of Mali and Songhai. Scholars, students and manuscripts moved between the two, and the pattern of Islamic learning in the region was shaped by their exchange.

The wealth of the region became known to the wider world in 1324, when the ruler of Mali, Mansa Musa, made his pilgrimage to Mecca with a vast retinue and distributed so much gold in Cairo that it was said to have depressed the price of the metal for years. European mapmakers soon began to depict West Africa as a land of gold, and the names of Timbuktu and Djenné entered the imagination of the Mediterranean world.

Today the two towns share not only a history but a predicament. Both are World Heritage Sites inscribed in the same year, both rely on earthen architecture, and both face the pressures of instability and environmental change. Understanding Djenné fully means seeing it as part of a network of cities, markets and ideas that linked the Sahel to North Africa and beyond.

How Earth Builds a City — The Craft of Banco

The material of Djenné’s architecture is banco, a mixture of local clay, water and organic binders such as rice husks or straw, which is shaped into bricks and dried in the sun. Traditional Djenné bricks were hand-moulded in a distinctive cylindrical form, and later rectangular moulded bricks became common. The bricks are laid in mud mortar and finished with a plaster of similar composition, applied in several coats to protect the wall from rain.

Palm-wood beams, the toron, project from the walls at regular intervals, and they serve several purposes: reinforcing the structure, providing ready-made scaffolding for repairs and, according to some interpretations, offering a decorative rhythm that gives the façades their distinctive texture. The thick walls help regulate temperature, keeping interiors cool during the hot afternoon and retaining warmth in the night, a form of passive climate control that suits the Sahel.

The craft of building in banco is passed down in families and guilds of masons, whose knowledge of the mixture, the weather and the structure is acquired over years of apprenticeship. This human knowledge is as much a part of the heritage as the walls themselves, and efforts to document and transmit it are among the most important aspects of conservation in Djenné.

Reflection — The Wisdom of Impermanence

There is something quietly radical about Djenné. In a world that tends to measure heritage by its permanence, the town honours impermanence: its buildings must be remade every year, and their survival depends not on stone or steel but on the labour and the festivals of the community. Preservation here is not a matter of freezing a monument but of keeping alive a living tradition.

That perspective has much to teach. The Great Mosque is not a relic but a collaborative work of art that has been renewed again and again, a symbol of how culture can persist through care and communal effort. To understand Djenné is to see that the greatest guardian of heritage is not an institution but a people, and that the most enduring monuments may be those that are never finished.

✦ Conclusion

The Old Towns of Djenné form one of the most extraordinary urban landscapes in Africa: a trading city of earth, with a great mosque that is renewed each year by the community that surrounds it, and an ancient predecessor in Djenné-Djeno that tells of urban life reaching back two millennia. Together they illustrate a distinctive Sahelian civilisation of scholars, merchants and masons.

The town’s present challenges, from erosion to instability, are serious, but its resilience is also striking. For those fortunate enough to see it, and for those who learn about it from afar, Djenné stands as a testament to the creativity of African architecture and to the power of community in sustaining heritage.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

Why are the Old Towns of Djenné a UNESCO World Heritage Site?

UNESCO inscribed them in 1988 under criteria (iii) and (iv) for their exceptional testimony to the Sudano-Sahelian architectural tradition, the town’s role as a trading and Islamic learning centre, and the archaeological evidence of early urbanism at Djenné-Djeno.

Is the Great Mosque of Djenné really the largest mud-brick building in the world?

It is widely described as the largest earthen, or adobe, building in the world. The present structure dates from 1907 and stands on a raised platform of about seventy-five metres by seventy-five metres.

What is the crépissage festival?

It is an annual communal event in which the people of Djenné replaster the Great Mosque with fresh mud, using the projecting wooden toron beams as scaffolding. It renews the building and strengthens community bonds, and is accompanied by music and celebration.

Can visitors enter the Great Mosque?

Access to the interior is restricted, and non-Muslim visitors have not generally been admitted since the late 1990s. Visitors can admire the exterior and the market square, but should check current rules and local advice.

Is it safe to visit Djenné?

Security conditions in Mali have been difficult in recent years, and many governments advise against travel to parts of the country. Anyone considering a visit should consult current official travel advice and local sources before making plans, and should treat this post as background rather than a travel advisory.
