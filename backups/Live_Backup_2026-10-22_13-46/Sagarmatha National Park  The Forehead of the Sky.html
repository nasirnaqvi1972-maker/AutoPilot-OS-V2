The Roof of the World

No mountain on earth has so completely seized the human imagination as Everest. Its summit, at 8,848 metres, is the highest point on the planet's surface, and the range of peaks that surround it, Lhotse, Nuptse, Ama Dablam, Pumori and a hundred others, forms a landscape of such scale that photographs struggle to convey it. Yet the park that protects it is more than a mountain. Sagarmatha National Park, in the Solukhumbu district of north-eastern Nepal, is a world of deep gorges, glaciers, high pastures, rhododendron forests and Sherpa villages, a landscape in which geology, ecology and faith have become inseparable.

The park covers 124,400 hectares, spanning an elevation range of some six thousand metres, from forested river valleys at around 2,800 metres to the summit of the world. It was established as a national park in 1976 and inscribed on the World Heritage List in 1979, in the first group of Nepalese sites, under natural criterion (vii) for its superlative natural beauty. The citation praises the dramatic mountains, glaciers and deep valleys, dominated by the highest peak in the world.

The Nepali name for the mountain is Sagarmatha, usually translated as Forehead of the Sky, while the Tibetan name, Chomolungma, means Goddess Mother of the World. The English name Everest was given in the nineteenth century in honour of Sir George Everest, a surveyor general of India who never saw the mountain. Each name carries a different perspective, and together they show how many cultures have looked up at the same peak and seen something sacred.

Mountains Born of Collision

The Himalaya is the product of one of the great collisions in earth's history. About fifty million years ago, the Indian plate, moving northward, crashed into the Eurasian plate, closing the ancient Tethys Sea that lay between them. The immense forces of the collision crumpled and uplifted the crust, raising the sediments of the old seabed into mountains, and the process continues today, with the range still rising by a few millimetres each year.

One of the most astonishing facts about Everest is that its summit is made of marine limestone, rock that formed on the floor of a warm sea hundreds of millions of years ago. Fossils of ancient sea creatures have been found near the top of the mountain, a vivid reminder that the highest place on earth was once beneath the waves. The rocks of the Khumbu also include schists and granites, thrust upward and folded in complex patterns.

Erosion and ice have sculpted this raw material into the landscape seen today. Glaciers have carved great U-shaped valleys and sharp, pyramidal peaks, and rivers such as the Dudh Kosi, fed by meltwater, have cut deep gorges through the rock. The result is a terrain of extraordinary vertical relief, and one of the most dynamic and youthful mountain environments on the planet.

The Sherpa and the Khumbu

The valleys of the Khumbu have been home to the Sherpa people for about five centuries. Their ancestors migrated from eastern Tibet through the high passes, and they settled in the sheltered valleys below the great peaks, where they developed a unique economy based on yak herding, potato farming and trade across the passes. Their Buddhist faith, of the Nyingma tradition, shapes the landscape, with prayer flags fluttering from every ridge, mani walls inscribed with sacred mantras beside every trail and monasteries perched on hilltops.

The spiritual centre of the Khumbu is Tengboche Monastery, founded in 1916 on a ridge at nearly 3,900 metres, with Ama Dablam and Everest rising behind it. The monastery has been damaged by earthquake and fire and rebuilt with the help of the community, and it remains an active place of worship. Visitors who arrive at dawn, as the monks gather for prayers and the horns echo from the walls, often find the experience unforgettable.

The market village of Namche Bazaar, built in a natural amphitheatre of the mountains, is the commercial heart of the region, with shops, lodges and a weekly market. Beyond it, the villages of Khumjung, Pangboche, Dingboche and others cling to the slopes, each with its own history. The Sherpa are known worldwide as mountain guides and porters, and their skill, endurance and knowledge of the mountains have made them indispensable to the climbing of Everest. Their culture is a living part of the park's heritage, and the World Heritage listing recognises the harmony between the people and their environment.

The Mountain and Its Climbers

The first recorded ascent of Everest took place on 29 May 1953, when the New Zealand mountaineer Edmund Hillary and the Sherpa climber Tenzing Norgay reached the summit as members of a British expedition led by John Hunt. The achievement made both men world famous and transformed the Khumbu, drawing the attention of the world to a remote valley and its people. Tenzing, born in the high country, became a national hero in Nepal and India, while Hillary devoted much of his later life to building schools and hospitals for the Sherpa communities.

Since 1953 thousands of climbers have reached the summit, and the mountain has become a commercial enterprise, with guided expeditions, fixed ropes and ladders across the crevasses of the Khumbu Icefall. The risks are real. Avalanches and altitude sickness have claimed hundreds of lives, among them many Sherpa guides and porters, and the tragedies of recent years, including the avalanche in the icefall in 2014 that killed sixteen Nepalese guides, and the avalanche triggered by the 2015 earthquake that killed nineteen people at Base Camp, have sharpened debate about the ethics and safety of mountaineering on Everest.

For most visitors, however, the great experience is not the summit but the journey to its foot. The trek to Everest Base Camp, at about 5,360 metres, is among the most celebrated walks in the world, a journey of some two weeks through villages, forests and moraines that reveals the full scale of the Himalaya without requiring technical climbing skills.

Life Among the Heights

The ecology of Sagarmatha changes dramatically with altitude. On the lower slopes, forests of blue pine, fir, juniper and birch, with a rich understorey of rhododendron, clothe the valleys, and in spring the hillsides flame with the red, pink and white of the flowers. Above the tree line, shrubs and alpine meadows give way to bare rock and ice, and the zone above five thousand metres, which makes up the great majority of the park, is a world of barren stone and glacier.

Wildlife is sparse but remarkable. The Himalayan tahr, a shaggy wild goat, grazes on the steep slopes, while the musk deer, shy and solitary, lives in the forests. The snow leopard, the elusive predator of the high mountains, ranges across the upper valleys, although it is rarely seen, and the red panda, Himalayan black bear and several species of pheasant also live in the park. The Himalayan monal, with its iridescent plumage, is Nepal's national bird and can often be seen on the trails.

Even the extreme heights support life. Yellow-billed choughs have been seen at altitudes of more than eight thousand metres, and a tiny jumping spider has been found at almost 6,700 metres, feeding on insects blown upward by the winds. Over two hundred bird species have been recorded in the park, and the combination of elevation, climate and isolation has created a unique ecological community.

Ice in Retreat

The glaciers of the Khumbu are among the most closely watched in the world, and their message is troubling. Studies have shown that the glaciers of the region are retreating, with the Himalayas warming faster than the global average, and meltwater is filling new lakes at the foot of the ice. Glacial lakes such as Imja Tsho have expanded rapidly, and the danger of a glacial lake outburst flood, in which a moraine dam gives way and releases a torrent of water down the valley, is a serious threat to communities downstream.

Tourism brings its own pressures. Tens of thousands of trekkers and climbers visit each year, and the waste they produce, from human sewage to discarded oxygen cylinders, has become a significant problem. The Sagarmatha Pollution Control Committee, established in 1991 by local people, has worked to manage rubbish in the region, and expedition teams are now required to bring their waste down from the mountain, but the challenge remains substantial.

The park's management has been strengthened over time. The area was extended by the creation of a buffer zone in 2002 and is now part of a larger protected landscape that includes Makalu Barun National Park to the east and the Gaurishankar Conservation Area to the west, as well as the Qomolangma reserve across the border in Tibet. The cooperation of the Sherpa communities, who share in the benefits of tourism and take part in conservation, is the foundation of the park's success.

The Trek to Base Camp

Most trekkers begin with a flight from Kathmandu to Lukla, landing on a short, sloping airstrip at around 2,860 metres, one of the most dramatic airports in the world. From there the trail follows the Dudh Kosi valley upward through Phakding to Namche Bazaar at about 3,440 metres, where most trekkers spend an extra night to acclimatise. Beyond Namche, the path climbs to Tengboche, then continues through Dingboche and Lobuche to Gorak Shep, the last settlement before Base Camp.

Everest Base Camp, at around 5,364 metres, lies beside the Khumbu Glacier, a bleak and spectacular site that is occupied by expedition tents in the spring climbing season. Because the summit of Everest is not visible from Base Camp, many trekkers climb the nearby viewpoint of Kala Patthar, at about 5,545 metres, for a clear view of the mountain's black pyramid rising above the clouds.

Altitude is the principal danger. Acute mountain sickness can strike anyone above 2,500 metres, and the only reliable protection is gradual ascent, rest days and willingness to descend if symptoms develop. The best seasons are spring, from March to May, and autumn, from late September to November, when skies are clearest. Trekkers need a park entry permit and a local permit, and should travel with an experienced guide, check current conditions and carry suitable insurance.

Reflection

There is a stillness in the Khumbu at dawn that no photograph can capture. The prayer flags hang motionless, the smoke rises from the yak-dung fires, and the first sun strikes the summit of Everest, lighting it in gold while the valleys below remain in shadow. In that moment, one understands why the Sherpa have called the mountain a goddess, and why the world has come to look at it. Sagarmatha is not only a place of records but a place of reverence, where the largest forces of the planet are visible in the shapes of the land, and where human beings, small and persistent, have made their homes and their faith in the shadow of the sky.

✦ Conclusion

Sagarmatha National Park is the highest landscape on earth and one of its most inspiring. Its peaks and glaciers are among the great spectacles of the natural world, its Sherpa communities are a living example of harmony between people and mountains, and its history of exploration is one of the great stories of human endurance.

It is also a landscape under pressure. Climate change, tourism and the risks of mountaineering all weigh on the park, and its future depends on the cooperation of the Nepali government, the Sherpa people and the international community. To visit Sagarmatha is to see the world at its highest and its most fragile, and to understand why it is worth protecting.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

1. Where is Sagarmatha National Park?

It lies in the Solukhumbu District of north-eastern Nepal, on the border with Tibet, and contains Mount Everest and the Khumbu region.

2. When was it inscribed on the World Heritage List?

It was inscribed in 1979 under natural criterion (vii), and was one of the first two Nepalese sites on the list, along with the Kathmandu Valley.

3. How high is Mount Everest?

UNESCO gives the height as 8,848 metres. A joint measurement by Nepal and China announced in 2020 gave 8,848.86 metres.

4. Who are the Sherpa?

The Sherpa are a Buddhist people who migrated from Tibet some five centuries ago and settled in the Khumbu. They are renowned as mountain guides and farmers.

5. When is the best time for the Base Camp trek?

March to May and late September to November offer the clearest weather. Allow time to acclimatise to avoid altitude sickness.
