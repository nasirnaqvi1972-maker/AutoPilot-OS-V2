Petra — The Rose-Red City Half as Old as Time

The Lost Nabataean Capital Carved into the Sandstone Cliffs of Southern Jordan

Petra is one of the most extraordinary ancient cities in the world. Carved directly into the rose-red sandstone cliffs of southern Jordan by the Nabataeans more than two thousand years ago, it was a thriving trading hub that connected Arabia, Egypt, and the Mediterranean. Today it is a UNESCO World Heritage Site and one of the New Seven Wonders of the World.

The city was lost to the Western world for centuries, known only to local Bedouin tribes, until a Swiss explorer named Johann Ludwig Burckhardt rediscovered it in 1812. Since then, Petra has captured the imagination of travellers, archaeologists, and filmmakers alike.

The Nabataeans and the Rise of Petra

The Nabataeans were a nomadic Arab people who settled in the region around the 4th century BCE. They quickly grew wealthy by controlling the trade routes that carried incense, spices, and silk between Arabia and the Mediterranean. Petra became their capital, and by the 1st century BCE it was home to an estimated 20,000 people.

The Nabataeans were master engineers. They built an elaborate system of dams, cisterns, and aqueducts that allowed the city to thrive in an otherwise arid desert. They also developed a distinctive architectural style that blended Hellenistic, Egyptian, and Mesopotamian influences — visible in the monumental facades that still stand today.

Petra's wealth attracted the attention of the Roman Empire. In 106 CE, the last Nabataean king, Rabbel II, died without an heir, and the kingdom was peacefully annexed by Rome. Petra continued to flourish under Roman rule for another century before trade routes shifted north to Palmyra, and the city began its slow decline.

The Siq — The Dramatic Entrance

Visitors enter Petra through the Siq, a narrow gorge that winds for over a kilometre between towering sandstone cliffs. The walls rise up to 80 metres on either side, and the path narrows to just a few metres in places. The colours of the rock change as the light shifts — deep reds, oranges, and purples bleeding into one another along the curve of the canyon.

At the end of the Siq, the Treasury (Al-Khazneh) appears suddenly, framed by the gorge — one of the most dramatic reveals in the ancient world. The passage was deliberately designed this way. The Nabataeans understood that the approach mattered as much as the destination, and they engineered the entrance to create a moment of pure revelation.

The Siq is not just a passage; it was also part of the city's water management system. Channels carved into the rock walls carried water from the spring at Wadi Musa into the heart of Petra, sustaining the city through the dry desert months. Small terracotta pipes and settling basins still line the walls, reminders of the engineering genius that made Petra possible.

The Treasury and the Monastery

The Treasury (Al-Khazneh) is Petra's most iconic monument. Despite its name, it was probably a royal tomb rather than a treasury. Its facade is 39 metres high and 25 metres wide, decorated with Corinthian columns, statues, and intricate carvings. The urn at the top was long believed to contain treasure, and Bedouin legend says it was shot at by travellers hoping to break it open — the pockmarks of their bullets are still visible.

The Monastery (Ad-Deir) is even larger — 47 metres high and 48 metres wide. It sits at the top of a long staircase of 800 steps, and its scale dwarfs everything else at Petra. Unlike the Treasury, it is largely undecorated, giving it a more austere, monumental feel. The climb is steep, but the reward is a view that stretches across the entire valley of Petra and out towards the desert beyond.

The Royal Tombs and the Theatre

Beyond the Treasury, Petra opens up into a vast valley filled with hundreds of carved structures — tombs, temples, houses, and a Roman-style theatre that could seat 8,500 people. The Royal Tombs, cut into the eastern cliffs, are among the finest examples of Nabataean architecture, with facades that rival the Treasury in scale and detail.

The most famous of these is the Urn Tomb, which was later converted into a Byzantine church in the 5th century CE — a reminder that Petra was not a single-period site but a place that evolved over centuries. The Corinthian Tomb, the Palace Tomb, and the Silk Tomb are all worth exploring, each with its own architectural character.

The theatre, carved directly into the rock, was probably used for both dramatic performances and public gatherings. It was later expanded by the Romans after they annexed the Nabataean Kingdom in 106 CE, increasing its capacity and adding a stage building behind the performance area.

Petra by Night and Modern Preservation

Three nights a week, Petra hosts a candlelit event called Petra by Night. Visitors walk through the Siq guided only by the light of hundreds of candles placed along the path, and arrive at the Treasury to find it lit by firelight, with Bedouin musicians playing traditional music. The event lasts about two hours and is one of the most atmospheric experiences at any ancient site in the world.

Modern Petra is carefully managed by the Jordanian Department of Antiquities and the Petra Development and Tourism Region Authority. Visitor numbers are capped, and conservation work is ongoing to protect the sandstone facades from erosion. The site faces challenges from wind, water, salt crystallisation, and the sheer volume of visitors — but the Jordanian government and international partners continue to invest in preservation.

The Bedouin of Petra — From Wadi Musa to the World

The Bedouin tribes of the Petra region — particularly the Bdoul — lived inside the ancient caves and tombs for centuries before the site was developed for tourism. In 1985, when Petra was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site, the Bedouin were relocated to the nearby village of Umm Sayhoun. Many still work at the site as guides, donkey handlers, and souvenir sellers.

Their connection to Petra is not just economic but cultural. The Bdoul have their own oral traditions, poetry, and knowledge of the site's hidden paths and caves that no archaeologist could replicate. A Bedouin guide will often show visitors places that are not on any map — a hidden tomb, a secret viewpoint, or a natural spring that has been used for centuries.

Visiting Petra — What to Expect

Petra is a large site — you cannot see all of it in a single day. Most visitors spend two to three days exploring, using the main trail on the first day to see the Treasury, the Theatre, and the Royal Tombs, then climbing to the Monastery on the second day and to the High Place of Sacrifice on the third.

The walking is demanding. The site involves long distances over uneven stone paths, with steep climbs to the major monuments. A donkey or mule can be hired to carry visitors up to the Monastery, but animal welfare organisations recommend walking if you are physically able.

The best time to visit is in spring (March to May) or autumn (September to November), when temperatures are mild. Summer afternoons can exceed 40°C, and winter nights can be cold. Regardless of season, start early — the site opens at 6:00 AM, and the first hours of daylight are the most beautiful.

Conclusion

Petra is more than a collection of ruins. It is a monument to the ingenuity, ambition, and artistic vision of a people who turned a desert valley into one of the great cities of the ancient world. Walking through the Siq and emerging at the Treasury is one of the most unforgettable experiences any traveller can have — a moment when two thousand years of history open up in front of you.

The Nabataeans are gone, but their city remains. And as long as travellers keep walking through the Siq, Petra will continue to be what it has always been: a place of wonder at the edge of the desert.

Frequently Asked Questions

Where is Petra located?
Petra is in the Ma'an Governorate of southern Jordan, about 240 kilometres south of Amman and 130 kilometres north of Aqaba.

When was Petra built?
Petra was built by the Nabataeans beginning around the 4th century BCE. It reached its peak in the 1st century BCE and was annexed by Rome in 106 CE.

Why is Petra a UNESCO World Heritage Site?
Petra was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1985 for its outstanding cultural value, including its monumental rock-cut architecture and its role as a major ancient trading hub.

How do you get to Petra?
Petra is reached from the town of Wadi Musa. Visitors typically fly into Amman or Aqaba, then travel by road to Wadi Musa. The site itself is entered on foot through the Siq.

What is the best time to visit Petra?
The best time to visit is between March and May or between September and November, when temperatures are mild. Summer afternoons can be extremely hot, and winter can bring cold nights and occasional rain.

Visitor's Guide

Getting there: Petra is accessed from the town of Wadi Musa, about 3 hours by road from Amman or 2 hours from Aqaba.

Entry: The site is open daily from 6:00 AM to 6:00 PM in summer and 6:00 AM to 4:00 PM in winter. A one-day ticket covers entry to the main site.

What to wear: Comfortable walking shoes are essential — the site involves a lot of walking on uneven terrain. Dress modestly and bring a hat and sunscreen.

Where to stay: Wadi Musa has a wide range of hotels, from budget guesthouses to luxury resorts, most within walking distance of the site entrance.

What to bring: Water, sunscreen, a hat, and comfortable shoes. A headlamp is useful if you plan to visit the Treasury at night for the Petra by Night event.
