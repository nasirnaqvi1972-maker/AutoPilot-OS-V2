A Staircase of Lakes

Few natural scenes in Europe seem as unreal as Plitvice. In a forested valley in the hills of central Croatia, a series of sixteen named lakes steps down from higher to lower ground, each spilling over into the next through curtains of waterfalls and over low barriers of rock. The water is astonishingly clear, and its colour changes from turquoise to emerald, blue-grey and even milky green depending on the light, the minerals and the season.

The lakes are divided into two groups: twelve Upper Lakes, in a dolomite basin, and four Lower Lakes, in a limestone canyon. The largest is Kozjak, about four kilometres long and more than forty metres deep, while the most spectacular waterfall is the Veliki Slap, or Great Waterfall, which plunges nearly eighty metres, making it the highest in Croatia. Wooden boardwalks and footpaths weave across and around the water, letting visitors walk practically among the cascades.

UNESCO inscribed Plitvice in 1979 under natural criteria (vii), (viii) and (ix), for its outstanding beauty, its geological and hydrological processes, and the ongoing biological evolution of its travertine barriers. The park was extended in 2000, and today the protected area covers roughly 29,500 hectares of lakes, karst, meadows and forest.

How the Lakes Are Built — Tufa and Living Stone

The lakes of Plitvice are not simply holes in the ground filled with water; they are constructions, built continuously by living organisms. The rivers that feed the lakes flow through limestone and dolomite and are rich in dissolved calcium carbonate. As the water tumbles over rocks and through moss, algae and bacteria, the carbonate precipitates out as tufa, also called travertine, a porous rock that grows around the plants and gradually forms barriers across the valley.

These barriers, which can grow at a rate of about a centimetre per year, hold back the water, and the lakes form behind them. Because the process continues, the barriers keep growing and changing, and the lakes keep shifting in shape and level over centuries. It is a rare example of a landscape that is still actively building itself, with life itself as the engineer.

This delicate system depends on clean, flowing water, moderate temperatures and the right balance of minerals and organisms. Pollution, changes in water flow or damage to the moss can interrupt tufa growth and cause barriers to erode, which is why the park’s management places such emphasis on protecting the quality of the water and limiting human impact on the barriers.

Karst, Caves and the Hidden Plumbing of the Land

Plitvice sits in the Dinaric karst, one of the great limestone regions of Europe, a landscape in which water dissolves rock and carves underground channels, caves and sinkholes. The surface rivers that feed the lakes disappear into the ground, flow underground and reappear at springs, in a hidden plumbing system that is still incompletely understood. Across the area, more than a hundred caves have been recorded, some of them containing remarkable formations.

The geology explains the abundance of water and the peculiarities of its behaviour. Rain that falls on the surrounding uplands sinks quickly into the porous rock and is released slowly, which helps maintain a steady flow in the lakes even in dry periods. Yet the same porosity makes the system vulnerable to pollution, as contaminants can travel quickly through the rock without being filtered.

The word karst itself comes from a region near the Slovenian and Croatian border, and the Dinaric karst is the type area for the study of this kind of landscape. Plitvice thus offers not only beauty but also a living lesson in the earth sciences, one that has contributed to the understanding of karst processes worldwide.

Forests, Bears and the Wildlife of Plitvice

The lakes lie within a landscape of dense forest, mainly beech and fir, with spruce and a variety of other trees, which covers much of the park and forms one of the most extensive areas of old forest in Central Europe. Under the canopy, ferns, mosses and wildflowers thrive in the damp air, and more than a thousand plant species have been recorded, including numerous orchids.

The forests and meadows shelter a rich fauna. Brown bears, wolves and lynx, all rare in much of Europe, live in the park and the wider Dinaric region, and visitors may occasionally see signs of them. Roe deer, wild boar, otters and many species of bats also live here, while the lakes are home to trout and other fish. Birdlife is abundant, with owls, woodpeckers, kingfishers and dippers among the species to be seen.

Butterflies and insects are also plentiful. The park includes more than three hundred species of butterfly and moth, many of them dependent on its meadows, which are maintained by traditional mowing. This reminder that traditional land use helps sustain biodiversity underlines the importance of continuing to manage the landscape thoughtfully rather than simply leaving it alone.

Legends, Early Visitors and the Making of a Park

Local legend tells of a Black Queen, Crna Kraljica, who brought rain to a land suffering a terrible drought, her prayers answered by the creation of the lakes. Such stories reflect a long familiarity with the place, and the lakes have been known to local people for centuries. From the nineteenth century, travellers began to visit, and early guest houses and a regime of guided walks laid the foundations for tourism.

In 1949, Plitvice was declared a national park, the first and largest in Croatia, and it has since become the country’s most visited natural attraction. The development of boardwalks, trails and electric boats on Lake Kozjak has allowed millions to enjoy the lakes while protecting the sensitive barriers. Swimming, picking plants and leaving the paths are forbidden, rules that may seem strict but that are vital to the survival of the tufa.

The park has also attracted film-makers, notably in the 1960s, when a series of adventure films based on the novels of Karl May were shot here, using the lakes as a backdrop for the American West. These films made Plitvice famous across Germany and Central Europe, and helped establish its image as a place of almost fairy-tale beauty.

War, Recovery and the Pressures of Popularity

Plitvice’s modern history has not been free from conflict. In March 1991, at the start of the Croatian War of Independence, the park was the scene of one of the first armed clashes of the war, and for several years the area was held by Serbian forces. The park was damaged and mined, and UNESCO placed it on the List of World Heritage in Danger in 1992, where it stayed until the area was reintegrated and cleared in the later 1990s.

Since then, the park has recovered and become one of the great tourist destinations of Croatia, attracting well over a million visitors a year. Such popularity brings pressure. Crowds on the boardwalks, traffic, litter and demand for accommodation have raised concerns about the capacity of the park, and management has introduced measures such as timed tickets, capacity limits and regulation of visitor flows, particularly in the busiest summer months.

The balance between access and protection is an ongoing challenge. Visitors who come early in the morning, in spring or autumn, or in winter when the waterfalls freeze into sculptures of ice, will find a quieter and in many ways more rewarding experience, and help ease the strain on this fragile system.

Four Seasons at Plitvice — Spring Floods, Autumn Gold and Winter Ice

Plitvice changes dramatically with the seasons. In spring, snowmelt and rain swell the streams, and the waterfalls thunder at full strength, throwing spray across the boardwalks and filling the forest with the sound of water. Wildflowers carpet the meadows, the beech leaves are a fresh pale green, and the lakes are at their brightest, making this one of the most rewarding times to visit.

Summer brings warmth and long days, along with the largest crowds. The shade of the forest and the cool mist of the falls are welcome, and the lakes reflect the deep green of the trees. Autumn, by contrast, turns the beech woods to copper and gold, and the colours reflected in the water give the park an almost painterly quality, while the air is crisp and the paths are quieter.

In winter the park enters a different world. The waterfalls freeze into curtains of ice, snow outlines the branches, and the lakes become still and silvery. Some paths and services close, and boardwalks can be icy, but those who visit in this season see Plitvice in an austere and magical light, with a silence that is rare in summer.

Walking the Park — Routes, Viewpoints and Boats

The park is designed to be walked, and its network of trails and boardwalks is well organised, with colour-coded routes of varying length. The shorter routes concentrate on the Lower Lakes and the Great Waterfall, while longer routes include the Upper Lakes and the forested hills. Visitors often begin at one entrance and finish at the other, using the electric boat and the panoramic train to link the two sections.

The most famous viewpoint looks down from the cliff edge over the Lower Lakes toward the Veliki Slap, showing the waterfall in its full height and the lakes below in layers of turquoise. Elsewhere, boardwalks pass within an arm’s length of cascades, and short detours lead to caves and quiet corners where few people go. Each turn reveals a new composition of water, stone and green.

Planning helps. Arriving early in the day, choosing a route that suits one’s stamina and leaving time to pause at viewpoints makes the experience more enjoyable and reduces crowding. Keeping to the paths and respecting the rules is not only a matter of courtesy but a direct contribution to the preservation of the barriers and the habitats around them.

Reflection — A Landscape That Builds Itself

What makes Plitvice so haunting is that it is alive in a way few landscapes are. The lakes and waterfalls are not static features but the product of countless tiny organisms working in the water, depositing stone grain by grain, building walls that hold the lakes in place. To walk over a boardwalk above a barrier is to stand on a structure that is literally growing beneath one’s feet.

That awareness changes how one sees the beauty. The turquoise water is the sign of a delicate balance, and the roar of the falls is the sound of a process that can only continue if the water stays clean and the forest remains healthy. Plitvice teaches that beauty and fragility often go together, and that the most lovely places are often those most dependent on our care.

✦ Conclusion

Plitvice Lakes National Park is one of the most exquisite natural landscapes in Europe: a series of turquoise lakes and cascades, formed and maintained by living tufa barriers, set in ancient beech and fir forest and sheltered by the mountains of the Dinaric karst. Its geology, ecology and sheer beauty make it a site of exceptional value.

For the visitor, the park offers the chance to walk among waterfalls on carefully designed boardwalks and to experience a landscape that seems almost too beautiful to be real. With careful management and respect for its rules, it will continue to enchant for generations to come.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

Why is Plitvice Lakes a UNESCO World Heritage Site?

UNESCO inscribed it in 1979 under natural criteria (vii), (viii) and (ix) for its outstanding natural beauty, its geological and hydrological processes, and the ongoing formation of travertine barriers by living organisms. The site was extended in 2000.

How many lakes are there?

There are sixteen named lakes, arranged in a group of twelve Upper Lakes and four Lower Lakes, connected by waterfalls and cascades. The largest is Kozjak.

Why are the lakes so many different colours?

The colour depends on the mineral content of the water, the presence of microorganisms, the angle of sunlight and the season. The lakes can appear turquoise, green, blue or grey, and the colours shift through the day.

Can I swim in the lakes?

No. Swimming is not permitted, because it can damage the delicate tufa barriers and disturb the ecosystem. Visitors must stay on the marked paths and boardwalks.

When is the best time to visit?

Spring and early autumn offer a good balance of comfortable weather, full waterfalls and fewer crowds. Summer is busiest, while winter brings snow and frozen waterfalls, though some paths and boats may be closed.

🧳 Visitor's Guide

Getting There — The park lies about two hours by road from Zagreb and from Zadar; buses and tours run from both cities

Best Time to Visit — April to June and September to October for good water flow and fewer crowds

Tickets — Entry tickets vary by season and are often sold with timed entry; book online ahead in summer

Entrances — There are two main entrances; Entrance 1 is closer to the Great Waterfall and Entrance 2 to the Upper Lakes

Getting Around — Boardwalks, electric boats on Kozjak and a panoramic train link the main areas; allow a full day

Rules — No swimming, no leaving the paths, no picking plants and no feeding animals

What to Bring — Comfortable shoes, rain protection and water; boardwalks can be slippery

Accommodation — Hotels and guest houses are available inside and near the park; book early in high season
