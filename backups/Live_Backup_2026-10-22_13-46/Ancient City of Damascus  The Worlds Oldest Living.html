The City That Has Always Been

Damascus is one of those places whose age exceeds the capacity of ordinary historical imagination to fully absorb. Human settlement at the site of Damascus dates to approximately the seventh millennium BCE — the Neolithic period, approximately 9,000 years ago — making it one of the oldest continuously inhabited urban sites on Earth. By the second millennium BCE it was already an established city and a significant political centre. It appears in the records of the Egyptian pharaoh Thutmose III, who campaigned in the region around 1450 BCE. It was the capital of the Aramean kingdom of Aram-Damascus in the first millennium BCE, a major centre in the Persian Empire, a city visited by Alexander the Great, a Roman provincial city of considerable importance, the capital of the Umayyad Caliphate — the first great Islamic empire — in the seventh and eighth centuries CE, and subsequently the capital or the major city of the Abbasid, Tulunid, Ikhshidid, Hamdanid, Fatimid, Seljuk, Zengid, Ayyubid, and Mamluk polities that succeeded each other in Syria across the medieval period.

In each of these successive civilisations, Damascus was built and rebuilt — its temples converted to churches, its churches converted to mosques, its Roman street plan overlaid with the organic growth of the medieval Islamic city, its Byzantine mosaics incorporated into the Umayyad mosque that rose on the site of the cathedral that had risen on the site of the Roman temple. The result is a city of extraordinary historical density — a place where the layers of human occupation are not merely metaphorical but literally present in the archaeology of the buildings, in the reused Roman columns of the mosque's colonnades, in the Byzantine mosaics of the Umayyad courtyard, in the Crusader-era fortifications incorporated into the medieval citadel.

UNESCO inscribed the Ancient City of Damascus on the World Heritage List in 1979, under five criteria, recognising it as a masterpiece of human creative genius, an outstanding example of the interchange of human values across cultures and civilisations, an outstanding testimony to multiple vanished civilisations, an outstanding example of an architectural ensemble illustrating multiple significant periods in human history, and a site directly associated with events of outstanding universal significance. It is one of the most comprehensively significant cultural World Heritage Sites on the entire List.

The situation of Damascus at the time of writing is one of profound concern. The Syrian civil war that began in 2011 caused severe damage to multiple UNESCO World Heritage Sites across Syria, and while the Ancient City of Damascus was spared the worst of the destruction suffered by Palmyra, Aleppo, and other Syrian heritage sites, it has been affected by the conflict and by the economic collapse that has followed. The resilience of the city and its heritage is a matter of ongoing international concern, and the work of documentation, monitoring, and eventual restoration is a priority for UNESCO and the international conservation community. Readers who have explored our post on Babylon, Iraq will recognise in Damascus a parallel situation — a great ancient city of the Middle East whose heritage carries the accumulated weight of millennia of human civilisation and whose protection in conditions of conflict represents one of the most urgent challenges in the field of cultural heritage conservation.

The Umayyad Mosque — The Fourth Holiest Site in Islam

The Umayyad Mosque — al-Jami' al-Umawi al-Kabir, the Great Mosque of the Umayyads — is the most important building in Damascus and one of the most important in the Islamic world. Built between 705 and 715 CE by the Umayyad Caliph al-Walid I, on the site of a Byzantine cathedral of John the Baptist (which had itself been built on the site of a Roman temple of Jupiter), the mosque is both the oldest surviving monumental mosque in the world after the Mosque of the Prophet in Medina and one of the most architecturally magnificent religious buildings of any tradition.

The site on which the mosque stands has been sacred in an unbroken succession for more than three thousand years. The Roman temple of Jupiter was built in the first century CE on a site that had been used for religious purposes from the Bronze Age. The Byzantine cathedral of John the Baptist was built within the Roman temenos (sacred precinct) in the fourth century, reusing the Roman precinct walls and incorporating Roman columns into its structure. When the Arab Muslim armies captured Damascus in 636 CE, the cathedral and the mosque were initially shared between the Christian and Muslim communities — the Muslims praying in the eastern portion, the Christians in the western. In 705 CE, al-Walid I purchased the Christian share of the building and demolished the cathedral to build the current mosque on the site of the entire precinct.

The mosque covers an area of approximately 157 metres by 97 metres — one of the largest mosques in the world. Its courtyard — a vast marble-paved open space surrounded by arcaded galleries — is dominated by three structures: the Dome of the Treasury (an octagonal structure on columns, originally used to store the mosque's funds), the Dome of the Clock (housing the mosque's timekeeping instruments), and the Prayer Hall at the southern end. The prayer hall is divided into three aisles by rows of reused Roman and Byzantine columns, its interior decorated with Byzantine mosaics of extraordinary quality — large-scale landscape compositions depicting rivers, trees, and architectural fantasies of the earthly paradise, their gold tesserae glowing in the light that filters through the windows of the drum above.

The Street Called Straight — Via Recta

The Ancient City of Damascus preserves, in fragmentary but legible form, the street plan of the Roman city — a grid of streets oriented to the cardinal directions, its main east-west axis the Via Recta — the Straight Street, Darb al-Mustaqim in Arabic. This is the street mentioned in the New Testament — the street to which the blinded Paul of Tarsus was led after his conversion on the road to Damascus, the street where he was told to go to the house of Judas and wait for Ananias. The Street Called Straight of the Acts of the Apostles is a real street, still existing, still running from east to west through the ancient city, its Roman colonnades visible in fragmentary form above the later buildings that line its edges.

The straight street is approximately 1.5 kilometres long, running from the Eastern Gate (Bab Sharqi, still largely intact in its Roman form) to the area of the Great Mosque, with a slight deflection at its midpoint that reflects the joining of two different urban grids of different periods. Walking the length of the Via Recta — from the Eastern Gate through the Christian quarter, through the bazaar district, to the Great Mosque — is to walk through the full depth of Damascus's historical layers: Roman, Byzantine, Umayyad, medieval, and Ottoman architectural evidence visible in the buildings that line the street, the Roman columns embedded in the walls, the Crusader-era gates, the Ottoman khans and hammams and merchant houses of extraordinary character.

The Souks — A Medieval Economy in a Modern City

The souks of Damascus — the covered markets of the ancient city — are among the oldest and most continuously operating commercial centres in the world. The Souk al-Hamidiyye — the main covered market, its iron roof riddled with circular bullet holes from a French artillery bombardment in the nineteenth century that now admit shafts of dramatic light into the bazaar below — runs from the citadel in the west to the Umayyad Mosque in the east, a covered arcade approximately 600 metres long lined with shops selling textiles, silver, gold, brocade, spices, and the full range of Damascus's traditional craft production.

Beyond the Souk al-Hamidiyye, the ancient city is threaded with a network of specialised markets — the copper market, the textile market, the gold market, the spice market — each concentrated in its own quarter in the same organisational pattern that characterises the traditional Islamic city, the same pattern visible in our post on the Medina of Fez, Morocco. The Damascene craft tradition — the damask weaving that gave the fabric its name, the Damascene metalwork inlaid with silver and gold, the blown glass of characteristic blue and amber, the woodwork inlaid with mother-of-pearl — are among the most celebrated in the Islamic world, and the craftsmen who produce them still work, in many cases, in the same workshops and the same districts in which their ancestors worked for centuries.

The Khan Assad Pasha — The Ottoman City

The Ottoman period — 1516 to 1918 — left an extraordinary architectural legacy in Damascus: khans (caravanserais for merchants and their goods), hammams (bathhouses), mosques, and private houses of great architectural quality that are distributed throughout the ancient city and that represent some of the finest examples of Ottoman provincial architecture in the Levant.

The Khan Assad Pasha — built in 1751 by the Ottoman governor Assad Pasha al-Azm and regarded as the finest khan in Damascus — is a structure of extraordinary spatial quality: a large square courtyard covered by a central dome and eight smaller surrounding domes, its black and white marble floor and its walls of alternating black basalt and white limestone creating a space of great calm and great beauty. The khan originally served as a caravanserai for merchants arriving in Damascus along the trade routes from Anatolia, Persia, and the Arabian Peninsula. It now houses a craft market.

The private houses of the Christian quarter of Damascus — the Beit Wakil, the Beit Nizam, and others — preserve the traditional Damascene domestic architecture of the Ottoman period: inward-facing courtyard houses of great refinement, their plain exterior walls giving no indication of the elaborately decorated interiors within, where painted wooden ceilings, carved plaster alcoves, and marble-floored courtyards with central fountains create private worlds of extraordinary beauty and tranquillity.

Damascus Today — Heritage Under Pressure

The Ancient City of Damascus was inscribed on the UNESCO List of World Heritage in Danger in 2013, following the outbreak of the Syrian civil war. While the ancient city itself was not subjected to the systematic destruction suffered by Palmyra, Aleppo's citadel and souks, or Crac des Chevaliers, it has been significantly affected by the conflict — through the displacement of its resident population, the closure and damage of its traditional crafts workshops, the deterioration of buildings whose maintenance has been interrupted by the economic collapse accompanying the war, and the general disruption of the social and economic fabric that sustains a living historic city.

The international heritage community has responded with documentation projects, monitoring missions, and planning for eventual restoration — the work made more urgent by the scale of what stands to be lost if the deterioration of the ancient city continues unchecked. The resilience of Damascus — a city that has survived multiple conquests, earthquakes, fires, and political upheavals across eight thousand years of continuous occupation — is not merely a historical fact but a living reality that the international community has an obligation to support.

Reflection — Eight Thousand Years of Continuity

Damascus is, among other things, a lesson in the durability of human habitation — in the extraordinary persistence of a city across the full range of the historical changes that have transformed the Middle East across eight millennia. The Aramaeans, the Persians, the Macedonians, the Romans, the Byzantines, the Arab Muslims, the Crusaders, the Mongols, the Mamluks, the Ottomans, the French — all of them passed through Damascus or ruled it or fought over it, and Damascus survived all of them. The city absorbed their buildings and their cultures and their languages and their religions and went on being itself — the oasis city at the edge of the Syrian Desert, the city where the Barada River emerges from the Anti-Lebanon mountains into the fertile Ghouta plain, the city that has been a meeting point of the routes between the Mediterranean and the Arabian Peninsula and the Iranian plateau since before recorded history.

What survives in the Ancient City of Damascus — the Umayyad Mosque, the Street Called Straight, the medieval souks, the Ottoman khans and courtyard houses — is not merely the heritage of Syria or of the Arab world or of the Islamic civilisation. It is the heritage of all the civilisations that have left their mark on one of the oldest inhabited places on Earth. UNESCO was right to inscribe it in 1979 under five criteria. The world is right to be concerned about its current condition. And the eight thousand years of continuous human habitation that Damascus represents are right to command the respect and the protection that the international community can and should provide.

✦ Conclusion

The Ancient City of Damascus is one of the most historically significant places on Earth — a city whose eight thousand years of continuous habitation make it one of the longest-running experiments in urban civilisation that the human species has conducted, and whose Umayyad Mosque, medieval souks, Roman street plan, and Ottoman courtyard houses together constitute a layered archaeological and architectural record of outstanding universal value.

The current situation of Damascus — affected by conflict, economic collapse, and the displacement of the population that sustains its living heritage — makes the preservation of the ancient city one of the most urgent tasks in the field of cultural heritage conservation. The international community, through UNESCO and through the support of national conservation agencies, has a responsibility to ensure that what has survived eight thousand years of human history survives the present crisis and is passed on, intact, to the generations who will inherit it.

The Ancient City of Damascus has outlasted every challenge that history has thrown at it. The task of ensuring that it continues to do so falls, in the present moment, to all of us.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: Is Damascus the oldest city in the world? Damascus is one of the oldest continuously inhabited cities in the world — human settlement at the site dates to approximately the seventh millennium BCE, making it approximately 9,000 years old. Whether it is the absolute oldest is debated, with other sites including Jericho and Byblos also claiming this distinction. What is certain is that Damascus has been continuously inhabited for an extraordinarily long period.

Q2: What is the Umayyad Mosque? The Umayyad Mosque — the Great Mosque of the Umayyads — is one of the oldest and most magnificent mosques in the Islamic world, built between 705 and 715 CE by the Umayyad Caliph al-Walid I on the site of a Byzantine cathedral and a Roman temple. It is regarded by Muslims as the fourth holiest site in Islam after Mecca, Medina, and Jerusalem.

Q3: What is the Street Called Straight? The Street Called Straight — the Darb al-Mustaqim in Arabic, the Via Recta in Latin — is the main east-west street of the Roman city of Damascus, mentioned in the New Testament's Acts of the Apostles as the street where Paul of Tarsus was taken after his conversion. It is approximately 1.5 kilometres long and still in use as a commercial street.

Q4: Is the Ancient City of Damascus a UNESCO World Heritage Site? Yes. The Ancient City of Damascus was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1979, under five criteria — (i)(ii)(iii)(iv)(vi) — as one of the most comprehensively significant cultural World Heritage Sites on the List. It was inscribed on the List of World Heritage in Danger in 2013 due to the impact of the Syrian civil war.

Q5: Is it safe to visit Damascus? The situation in Syria has been extremely dangerous since the outbreak of civil war in 2011, and most governments advise against travel to Syria including Damascus. Visitors should consult their government's current travel advice before making any plans to visit. The heritage community hopes for a peaceful resolution of the conflict and the eventual safe reopening of Syria's extraordinary cultural heritage sites to international visitors.
