The Island of Gold

In 1331, the great Moroccan traveller Ibn Battuta, who had seen the cities of half the world, landed on a small island off the coast of East Africa and described what he found as one of the most beautiful and well-constructed towns in the world. The island was Kilwa Kisiwani, and the town was the capital of a powerful sultanate that controlled the gold trade between the interior of southern Africa and the markets of Arabia, Persia, India and China. Ibn Battuta admired the sultan's generosity and piety, and the elegance of the coral stone houses, and it is clear that Kilwa was at that moment at the height of its wealth.

Six centuries later, the ruins of Kilwa and its sister city on the neighbouring island of Songo Mnara are among the most important archaeological sites of the African coast. They lie off the shore of southern Tanzania, in the Lindi Region, some 330 kilometres south of Dar es Salaam, on low islands fringed with mangroves and baobabs. Their mosques, palaces and houses, built of coral stone and lime, are the remains of a civilisation that is too often overlooked: the Swahili culture of the East African coast.

UNESCO inscribed the ruins on the World Heritage List in 1981 under criterion (iii), recognising them as an exceptional testimony to the Swahili civilisation and its prosperous commerce between the twelfth and sixteenth centuries. The property consists of two small sites, and its significance is out of all proportion to its size.

A Coast Joined by the Monsoon

The Swahili coast, which stretches from Somalia to Mozambique, was shaped by the monsoon. For centuries, from about the first millennium AD, ships sailing on the seasonal winds carried goods and people across the Indian Ocean, riding the north-east monsoon southward in winter and the south-west monsoon back in summer. The coast of East Africa was one terminus of this network, and over time its towns grew into cosmopolitan ports where African, Arab, Persian and Indian traditions mingled.

The Swahili language, which is Bantu in grammar and rich in Arabic loanwords, is the best expression of that mingling. The people of the coast were Muslim from an early date, and their cities were centres of Islamic learning and trade, yet they were also deeply African, with strong links to the peoples of the interior. The merchants of Kilwa grew rich as intermediaries, collecting the products of the African interior, gold, ivory, ambergris, iron, timber and slaves, and exchanging them for cloth, glass, beads and Chinese porcelain from the ships of the north.

Kilwa's special advantage was its position. It lay near the southern limit of the monsoon routes, and close to the great gold-producing regions of the interior, whose products were shipped through the port of Sofala, to the south, and then carried north to Kilwa. In the late Middle Ages, the Kilwa sultans controlled this trade, and the gold that passed through their hands made the city one of the richest on the coast.

Sultans, Shirazis and Coins

The origin of the city is told in an old chronicle, the Kilwa Chronicle, written in the sixteenth century, which says that it was founded by a prince from Shiraz in Persia named Ali ibn al-Hasan, who bought the island from its African ruler. Archaeology shows a more complex picture, with a settlement that grew from a small village of mud and thatch in the ninth or tenth century into a town of stone by the twelfth. The idea of a Persian founder, however, reflects the importance that the Swahili elite attached to their Islamic and Persian heritage.

The city was ruled by a succession of dynasties. In the thirteenth century, power passed to a family known as the Mahdali, who made Kilwa the leading city of the coast, and whose sultans built the great monuments that survive. Their rule reached its height in the fourteenth century, under rulers such as Sultan al-Hasan ibn Sulaiman, whose reign saw the building of the palace of Husuni Kubwa and the enlargement of the Great Mosque.

Kilwa was also the first city of sub-Saharan Africa to mint its own coins. Excavations have found small copper coins struck at Kilwa from about the twelfth or thirteenth century, bearing the names of the sultans, as well as foreign coins including Chinese coins of the Song dynasty, evidence of the extent of the city's trade connections and of its sophisticated monetary economy.

The Great Mosque

The most important building at Kilwa Kisiwani is the Great Mosque, a monument of exceptional interest. It was founded in the twelfth century as a modest building, with a prayer hall of coral stone slabs set in a core of puddled clay, and it was enlarged in the fourteenth and early fifteenth centuries into the largest mosque on the East African coast. The extension added a vast southern prayer hall, roofed with a series of domes and vaults supported by rows of octagonal and square columns, an architectural form without parallel in the region.

The domes and vaults, made of coral rubble set in lime mortar, are the technical wonder of the building. They were built without centring, using local techniques, and the ranks of arches and domes give the interior a rhythmic beauty, even in ruin. Some of the vaults are still standing, and visitors can walk through the long arcades, imagining the sound of prayers and the play of light from the sea.

Close to the Great Mosque stand other mosques, the remains of the town, with houses, public squares and cemeteries, and the small, elegant Husuni Ndogo, a lesser palace. Together they present a picture of an Islamic city built by Africans with the means of the coast, in a distinctive architectural language that combined imported ideas with local materials and skills.

Husuni Kubwa — The Palace on the Cliff

Overlooking the sea from a headland to the east of the main town stand the ruins of Husuni Kubwa, a palace built in the early fourteenth century, probably between about 1310 and 1333, by Sultan al-Hasan ibn Sulaiman. It was the largest single building in sub-Saharan Africa at the time, a vast complex of courtyards, halls, chambers, storerooms and terraces, covering several acres and including more than a hundred rooms.

Its position was dramatic. The palace stood on the edge of the cliff, looking out over the Indian Ocean, and its terraces descended in steps to the water. Among its features were a great court, with a sunken area and an audience hall, private apartments and a magnificent octagonal swimming pool, probably used for ceremonies as well as pleasure, with steps leading down into it and a view toward the sea.

Husuni Kubwa was both a palace and a symbol. It proclaimed the wealth of Kilwa and the sultan's status, and its design, with its carefully ordered spaces and its use of carved stone and fine plaster, shows an architecture of confidence. It was apparently never completed, or was occupied only briefly, and its abandonment has contributed to the mystery that surrounds it.

Songo Mnara — A Town Preserved

A short sail to the south lies the island of Songo Mnara, where another Swahili city flourished between the fourteenth and sixteenth centuries. In contrast to Kilwa, where the ruins are scattered and overlaid by later buildings, Songo Mnara preserves the plan of a medieval Swahili town with remarkable clarity. Within an enclosing wall are the remains of five mosques, a number of large houses and a palace, grouped around open spaces.

The houses, of which about thirty-three have been identified, are particularly interesting. They were built of coral stone and lime, with long, narrow rooms arranged around a courtyard, and they were decorated with niches in the walls, carved doorways and plastered surfaces. The arrangement of the rooms, with an entrance room for public use and inner rooms for family life, reveals a society with strong ideas of privacy and hierarchy.

Songo Mnara was a smaller town, but it seems to have been wealthy, and the finds of Chinese porcelain, Indian beads and other imports show that it shared in the trade of its neighbour. The two sites, one a great capital and the other a refined provincial town, provide a vivid picture of the range of Swahili urban life.

The Portuguese, Oman and the End

The fortunes of Kilwa changed dramatically with the arrival of the Portuguese. In 1502, Vasco da Gama, on his return from India, forced the sultan to pay tribute, and in 1505 a large Portuguese fleet under Francisco de Almeida attacked and sacked the city and built a fort, the first Portuguese fort on the East African coast. The garrison was soon withdrawn, but the damage was done: the Portuguese took control of the gold trade, which they diverted to Sofala and Mozambique, and the prosperity of Kilwa declined.

The city never recovered its former glory. In the following centuries, it was ruled by local sultans, then by Omani Arabs, who in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries established control over much of the coast and built a fort, the Gereza, on the foundations of the Portuguese fort. Kilwa became a centre of the slave trade, supplying captives from the interior for the markets of Zanzibar and the French islands, a dark chapter in its history.

In the nineteenth century, the town moved to the mainland, and the island was abandoned to the ruins. The buildings, left to the sea and vegetation, gradually decayed, and it was only in the twentieth century that archaeologists began to study them, led by figures such as the British scholar Neville Chittick, whose excavations in the 1960s transformed understanding of the Swahili coast.

Danger, Conservation and Visiting

The ruins are fragile. They are built of coral stone and lime, which are vulnerable to salt, humidity, wind and water, and the low-lying islands are exposed to erosion by the sea. In 2004, the World Heritage Committee placed Kilwa Kisiwani and Songo Mnara on the List of World Heritage in Danger because of damage by sea erosion, collapsing monuments, vegetation and encroachment, and they remained on the list until 2014. A joint programme by Tanzania, Japan, France, Norway and UNESCO supported emergency conservation, including repairs to the walls, sea defences and management planning.

Visiting is possible but requires planning. The ruins are reached from the town of Kilwa Masoko, on the mainland, by a short crossing by dhow or motorboat, usually of twenty to thirty minutes, and the site is managed by the national antiquities authorities, with local guides available. The drive to Kilwa Masoko from Dar es Salaam is long, around 330 kilometres, and the road conditions vary. The dry season, from about June to October, is the most convenient time to visit.

Visitors should respect the site and its community, wear sun protection, carry water and follow the guidance of local guides. Travellers should check current conditions, boat schedules and access before they travel. In an age of tourism, Kilwa remains quiet, and the pleasure of walking through its ruins, with only the sound of the sea and the baobabs, is a rare one.

Reflection

The Swahili cities are a reminder of how much of the human story is told in coral stone and lime rather than in marble. The merchants of Kilwa had no great empire behind them, only a harbour, a monsoon and a talent for trade, and from that they built mosques, palaces and a coinage. Walking among the broken arches of the Great Mosque, with the tide sounding on the shore, one senses that civilisation is not the preserve of any one place or people, but something that arises wherever people meet, exchange and dream. The sea that eroded Kilwa also made it, and in its ruins one can still hear the monsoon wind.

✦ Conclusion

The Ruins of Kilwa Kisiwani and Songo Mnara preserve the memory of one of the great trading civilisations of the medieval world. Their mosques and palaces, made of coral stone, testify to the wealth, sophistication and cosmopolitan character of Swahili society, and to the importance of Africa in the networks of the Indian Ocean.

They are also a heritage in need of care. Sea, weather and neglect continue to threaten them, and their survival depends on sustained conservation and the commitment of the local community. To visit them is to discover a chapter of African history that deserves to be far better known.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

1. Where are Kilwa Kisiwani and Songo Mnara?

They are on two small islands off the coast of the Lindi Region in southern Tanzania, about 330 kilometres south of Dar es Salaam, reached from Kilwa Masoko.

2. When were they inscribed on the World Heritage List?

In 1981 under criterion (iii). They were on the List of World Heritage in Danger from 2004 to 2014.

3. What are the main monuments?

At Kilwa Kisiwani, the Great Mosque, the Husuni Kubwa palace and the Gereza fort; at Songo Mnara, five mosques, a palace and a number of houses within a walled enclosure.

4. Why was Kilwa important?

It controlled the gold trade from southern Africa to the Indian Ocean world in the thirteenth to fifteenth centuries and minted the first coins in sub-Saharan Africa.

5. When is the best time to visit?

The dry season, from about June to October. Check boat schedules and local conditions before travelling.
