Polonnaruwa — The Garden City of the Sinhalese Kings

A City Among the Trees

There is a particular pleasure in arriving at Polonnaruwa by bicycle. The paths run quietly between tall trees, with the occasional toque macaque watching from a wall, and then, around a bend, a vast brick palace or a circular shrine of carved stone appears from the greenery. Beyond the ruins stretches a shining expanse of water, the great reservoir of Parakrama Samudra, whose far shore is barely visible in the haze. The city of Polonnaruwa, once the thriving capital of a Sinhalese kingdom, has been silent for eight centuries, but its monuments are among the best preserved and most beautiful in South Asia.

Polonnaruwa lies in the North Central Province of Sri Lanka, in the dry zone of the island, and served as the royal capital from the eleventh to the thirteenth century. It succeeded Anuradhapura, the first great Sinhalese capital, and its short golden age, under kings who combined military power with a devotion to Buddhism and a passion for irrigation, produced some of the finest architecture and sculpture of the island.

UNESCO inscribed the Ancient City of Polonnaruwa in 1982, alongside Anuradhapura and Sigiriya, under criteria (i), (iii) and (vi). It is recognised as a masterpiece of creative genius, an exceptional testimony to a civilisation and a place directly associated with the traditions of Theravada Buddhism. The monuments are the work of a kingdom that understood its landscape and shaped it to serve both its people and its faith.

From Chola Conquest to Royal Capital

The story of Polonnaruwa begins with a catastrophe. In 993, the Chola emperor Rajaraja I, ruling from southern India, invaded Sri Lanka and destroyed Anuradhapura, the capital of the Sinhalese kings for more than a thousand years. The Cholas made Polonnaruwa, until then a modest royal residence, their provincial capital, calling it Jananathamangalam, and they built Hindu temples, notably to Shiva, whose remains can still be seen among the ruins.

The Sinhalese fought on for decades. In 1070, King Vijayabahu I finally drove the Chola garrison from the island after a long campaign, and rather than return to the hot and exposed ruins of Anuradhapura, he chose to rule from Polonnaruwa. The new capital had advantages: it lay closer to the fertile Mahaweli River, it was easier to defend and it stood at the centre of an area where large-scale irrigation was possible.

The city reached its peak under Parakramabahu I, who ruled from 1153 to 1186. A great king in the mould of the Buddhist rulers of old, he reunited the island after years of civil war, built an impressive palace, restored temples and monasteries, and, above all, embarked on a massive programme of irrigation works that made the dry zone bloom. His reign is remembered as the high point of medieval Sinhalese civilisation.

The Sea of Parakrama

The greatest of the king's creations was the reservoir that bears his name. The Parakrama Samudra, or Sea of Parakrama, was formed by joining several smaller tanks with enormous earthen embankments, creating a body of water that covers more than 2,500 hectares. Its scale was such that it appeared to early observers as an inland sea, and it remains in active use today, supplying water to the surrounding fields of rice.

The reservoir was not merely a technical triumph; it was the foundation of the kingdom's prosperity. In a land where rainfall is seasonal and uncertain, the capacity to store water and distribute it through canals determined the harvests, and therefore the strength of the state. The Sinhalese had been masters of hydraulic engineering for more than a thousand years, and the tanks of Polonnaruwa represent the culmination of that tradition.

The city itself was planned as a garden city, with a royal citadel at its heart, surrounded by gardens, ponds and parks, and protected by earthen ramparts and moats. The pattern of the outer city was laid out along the cardinal directions, with the monasteries and temples strung out along the northern part. The idea of a capital as a harmonious composition of buildings, water and greenery, rather than a crowded, walled town, remains one of the city's most appealing features.

The Sacred Quadrangle

The religious heart of Polonnaruwa is the Sacred Quadrangle, a raised, walled terrace that holds a cluster of the finest shrines of the city. Its centrepiece is the Vatadage, a circular relic house, built to shelter a stupa, with concentric terraces and low brick walls faced in stone. Four entrances, each with a flight of steps, lead to the centre, where four seated Buddha figures face the cardinal directions around the small stupa.

The artistry of the Vatadage lies in the details. The entrances are guarded by finely carved guardstones, and the steps are decorated with semicircular moonstones, intricately carved with bands of animals and floral motifs, which rank among the most beautiful in Sri Lanka. The stone pillars that once supported a wooden roof add to the elegant geometry of the building. It is thought to have been built by Parakramabahu I, or perhaps by his successor Nissanka Malla, to house the sacred tooth relic of the Buddha.

Around it stand other important monuments: the Hatadage, a relic shrine built by Nissanka Malla; the Atadage, an older shrine attributed to Vijayabahu I; the Thuparama, a vaulted image house; and the Nissanka Latha Mandapaya, a small, elegant pavilion with stone pillars carved to resemble lotus stalks. Nearby, the Gal Potha, or Stone Book, a massive slab inscribed with a record of the king's achievements, testifies to the pride of the rulers.

Palace and Council Chamber

A short distance from the Quadrangle stand the remains of the royal palace of Parakramabahu I, known as the Vejayanta Prasada. Its surviving walls, built of massive brick and rising to a considerable height, show the scale of the original, which tradition says was seven storeys high and contained a thousand rooms. The roof and upper floors, which were of wood, have long since vanished, but the thick walls and the deep sockets for beams give a vivid impression of the building's size.

Close to the palace is the Council Chamber, a raised stone platform on which stood a timber-roofed hall where the king met his ministers. Its stone steps and balustrades are decorated with lions and elephants in the best tradition of Sinhalese carving, and the pillars, with their elegant bases, suggest the splendour of the lost hall. Inscriptions on the pillars record the seating arrangements for the officials, according to rank.

The royal bath, the Kumara Pokuna, with its stone-lined pool and carved spouts, and the beautiful lotus-shaped pond, the Nelum Pokuna, are further reminders of the refinement of court life. Together, the buildings of the citadel convey the image of a ruler who lived with dignity and magnificence, but who also devoted much of his wealth to the welfare of his subjects and the support of the Buddhist order.

The Gal Vihara — Stone That Breathes

No monument in Polonnaruwa is more moving than the Gal Vihara, a rock temple at the northern end of the city, where four colossal figures of the Buddha have been carved from a single long outcrop of granite. Dating from the reign of Parakramabahu I, they were once housed within brick shrines, of which only traces remain, and they now stand open to the sky, glowing in the morning light.

On the left is a seated Buddha in meditation, carved into a shallow cave in the rock, beside a smaller seated figure in a separate niche. In the centre stands a tall figure, about seven metres high, with arms crossed over the chest, in an unusual pose that has been much debated. To the right lies the immense reclining Buddha, some fourteen metres long, shown at the moment of his passing into final nirvana, his head resting on a pillow, one hand supporting his head.

What distinguishes these sculptures is their serenity and technical mastery. The granite is shaped with subtle modelling, the folds of the robes ripple with a sense of movement, and the faces, with their half-closed eyes and gentle expression, radiate calm. The sculptors had no hope of correcting errors in so hard a stone, and the flawless proportions of the figures reveal an extraordinary confidence. The Gal Vihara is widely regarded as the finest achievement of Sinhalese rock sculpture.

Many Faiths, Many Ruins, and the Fall of the City

Polonnaruwa is mainly a Buddhist city, but it also bears the traces of other traditions. The Chola temples to Shiva, built of stone in the style of south India, stand near the Sacred Quadrangle, and bronze images of Hindu deities found in the ruins show the cultural exchange between Sri Lanka and the Tamil lands. The Lankatilaka, a gigantic image house with brick walls rising some seventeen metres on either side of a colossal Buddha figure, and the Tivanka image house, with its surviving frescoes, show the breadth of the city's religious architecture.

At the northern end of the city stands the Rankot Vihara, a huge stupa, over fifty metres tall, built by Nissanka Malla, and close by the Kiri Vehera, a stupa that is still white and still in use, which has been preserved with remarkable completeness. Both display the engineering skill of the builders and the continuing devotion of the Buddhist community.

The city's decline was rapid. After the reigns of Parakramabahu and Nissanka Malla, the kingdom was weakened by dynastic disputes, and in 1215 an invasion from the Kalinga region led by Magha laid waste to the capital. Later rulers moved southwest, away from the dry zone, and the abandoned city was swallowed by the forest. It was rediscovered by British officials in the nineteenth century and excavated and restored from the early twentieth, a process continued in recent decades under the Cultural Triangle project.

Visiting Polonnaruwa

The ancient city lies in the dry zone of north-central Sri Lanka, about five hours by road from Colombo and close to Sigiriya, Dambulla and Anuradhapura, which together form the island's Cultural Triangle. The archaeological park is entered through ticket offices run by the Central Cultural Fund, and the main monuments are spread over several kilometres, so most visitors hire bicycles, tuk-tuks or cars to see them all.

The best time to visit is early in the morning, before the heat builds and the crowds arrive, when the light is soft and the wildlife is active. Bring water, sun protection and a hat. At all religious sites, visitors must dress modestly, covering shoulders and knees, and remove shoes and head coverings in sacred areas. The dry season, from roughly May to September, is generally the most comfortable.

The Archaeological Museum near the entrance gives an excellent introduction, with models, sculptures and the famous Chola bronzes. A boat ride on the Parakrama Samudra at sunset offers a pleasing contrast to the day's exploration. Travellers should check current ticket prices, opening hours and conditions before visiting.

Reflection

Polonnaruwa is a place for slowness. There is no single overwhelming view, as at Angkor or Machu Picchu; the pleasure comes in fragments, a carved step in the shade, the pitch of a brick wall, a smile in granite. The kings who built the city understood that power is brief and that what endures is the care with which water, stone and devotion are shaped. Standing before the reclining Buddha in the early light, with the village bells beginning in the distance, one feels the whole weight of that understanding. The city fell, as cities do, but its calm survives.

✦ Conclusion

The Ancient City of Polonnaruwa preserves the memory of a medieval capital in which religion, engineering and kingship combined to remarkable effect. Its reservoir, its palaces, its relic shrines and its colossal Buddhas form a coherent picture of a society that understood the importance of water and faith.

For the visitor, it offers an unforced and generous experience: monuments of lasting beauty in a natural setting of trees, water and birdsong. And for Sri Lankans, it remains a source of pride, a reminder of a time when their island was a centre of Buddhist civilisation and a model of the harmony between people and landscape.
