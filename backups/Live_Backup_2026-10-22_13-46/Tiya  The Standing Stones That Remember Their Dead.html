A Field of Stones South of the Capital

Most travellers who come to Ethiopia come for the north: the rock-hewn churches of Lalibela, the obelisks of Aksum, the castles of Gondar. The south is quieter, more agricultural, more easily overlooked, and it is here, on the edge of the Rift Valley a short drive from Addis Ababa, that one of the strangest and most evocative prehistoric monuments in Africa lies half-hidden in pasture and cropland. Tiya is a field of standing stones, and to stand among them on a bright highland morning is to feel an immediate and slightly unsettling sense of company.

There are thirty-six monuments at Tiya, of which thirty-two are carved stelae, tall, narrow slabs of local stone set upright in the ground. Their surfaces are covered with shallow incised designs: sword shapes, human-like figures, geometric patterns, and symbols that resemble plants or stylised faces. Some stand taller than a person, some have toppled or lean at angles, and several cluster together in small groups that give the field the look of a cemetery, which is precisely what it is. These are tombstones in the oldest and most literal sense, markers set above the buried dead by a people whose names and language are lost to us.

UNESCO inscribed Tiya in 1980 under criteria (i) and (iv), recognising both the artistic quality of the carving and the site’s value as a representative example of a funerary tradition that once stretched across much of southern Ethiopia. Small as it is, perhaps no more than three hectares, Tiya is the best known and best preserved of hundreds of such stelae fields, and its meaning extends far beyond its modest boundaries.

Reading the Land — The Rift Valley Setting

To understand Tiya, one must first understand the landscape in which it sits. The Ethiopian Rift Valley is a great structural trench cutting through the highlands, a northern arm of the East African Rift, dotted with lakes and volcanic cones and bordered by high escarpments. The area around Tiya lies on the margin of the highlands, where fertile volcanic soils support farming and herding, and where the climate is mild and well watered. It is the sort of country in which human communities have lived for a very long time.

Ethiopia as a whole is one of the oldest zones of human occupation on earth. The Rift Valley farther north has yielded some of the most celebrated fossil hominids ever discovered, and nearby sites such as Melka Kunture document stone-tool traditions reaching back more than a million years. Against that immense timescale Tiya is a late arrival, a medieval monument raised by farming and pastoral peoples, but it belongs to a continuous history of human settlement in the Rift that has lasted, in one form or another, since our species began.

The siting of the stelae is also telling. They stand not in a wilderness but in the midst of cultivated land, close to the routes and communities that must have given rise to them. This is a memorial landscape rather than a ritual retreat, and its position beside farms and villages hints that the dead of Tiya were meant to remain present among the living.

The Stelae and Their Language of Symbols

The extraordinary feature of Tiya is its carving. Each stele is a flat or slightly rounded slab, usually between one and five metres high, and its decorated face is covered with a repertoire of shapes that seems at once familiar and impenetrable. The most prominent are the sword-like designs, narrow vertical forms with a crossguard and a pointed blade, sometimes grouped in rows. Alongside them appear anthropomorphic figures, circles, and branching motifs, and some stelae are crowned with a rounded or flattened upper section that can look uncannily like a head.

Swords, Figures and Plants

Interpretation of these signs has long been debated. Some researchers have seen the swords as references to warriors killed or to the military standing of the dead; others have suggested that the number of symbols may relate to the number of individuals buried or to events in their lives. The plant-like motifs have been read as emblems of fertility or lineage, and the human-like figures as representations of the deceased or of ancestors. None of these readings is certain, and the honest scholarly position is that the code remains unbroken. What is clear is that the carvers were working within a shared, deliberate system of signs, and that this was a literate memorial culture in the broad sense, even if its writing was in stone rather than in script.

The stones themselves are carved with notable skill. The designs are incised with consistent depth and with a confident sense of proportion, and the arrangement of motifs across the surface shows an intention to compose rather than merely to decorate. In recognising the work as an outstanding creative achievement, UNESCO’s inscription under criterion (i) acknowledges that these are not merely archaeological artefacts but works of art.

Graves Beneath the Stones — What the Excavations Revealed

For a long time the stelae of southern Ethiopia were a puzzle, and early European visitors noted them with curiosity but without a clear understanding of their purpose. Scholars working in the first decades of the twentieth century recorded the carved stones, and later archaeological investigation in the 1970s, including excavations led by the French archaeologist Roger Joussaume, finally brought the funerary function into focus. Beneath and beside the stelae were found human burials, in which the dead had been laid in a flexed or foetal position, accompanied in some cases by modest offerings.

These burials matter because they transform the stelae from mysterious monuments into the visible half of a funerary practice. The stone above the ground and the body below it formed a unit, a memorial that announced the presence of the dead to everyone who passed. Some graves contained multiple individuals, which suggests family or communal burial, and the variation in the number and arrangement of symbols across the stelae may well reflect variations in status, identity or the circumstances of those commemorated.

The dating of the stelae at Tiya is generally placed within the medieval period, often given as around the twelfth to thirteenth centuries, though the wider tradition of erecting carved stones in southern Ethiopia seems to have lasted for several centuries. This situates Tiya in the same broad era as the great rock-hewn churches of Lalibela in the north, and it is a humbling thought that while the kings of the highlands were carving churches from the living rock, communities in the south were quarrying and decorating a very different kind of stone monument.

A Wider Megalithic World in Southern Ethiopia

Tiya is not alone. Across a broad swathe of southern Ethiopia, particularly in the regions inhabited today by the Gurage, Hadiya, Sidama, Gedeo and other peoples, stand many hundreds of stelae sites, some with a handful of stones and some with thousands. The tradition is not uniform: in some places the stelae are plain, in others they are phallic in form, in others, as at Tiya, they are flat slabs bearing sword and figure motifs. This variety suggests a long-lived cultural phenomenon with many local expressions rather than a single event or a single people.

The southern Ethiopian stelae belong, in broader terms, to a pan-African and global tradition of megalithic memorial, which includes the stelae of Aksum in the north, the standing stones of the Senegambian region, and the monoliths of Europe. Yet they have their own distinct character. In choosing Tiya as the one site from this tradition to include on the World Heritage List, UNESCO effectively selected a representative of a wider heritage, a decision that carries both honour and responsibility, because the protection of a single site cannot alone preserve the countless others that remain unrecorded.

Indeed the larger stelae fields have faced pressure from agriculture, erosion, quarrying and the simple passage of time. Many stones have been moved, broken or reused, and the knowledge of who raised them has often been lost within living memory. This is why the preservation and documentation of Tiya is important not only for its own sake but as a window into a heritage that is otherwise perilously fragile.

Conservation, Community and the Future of Tiya

Preserving a field of standing stones is a different challenge from preserving a building. There is no roof to repair or wall to prop; the threats are weather, erosion, soil movement, vegetation and human traffic. At Tiya, the stelae stand in an open site where grazing animals, farmers and visitors all move through the same space, and the carved surfaces are slowly worn by wind, rain and lichen. Conservation efforts have therefore had to focus on protecting the site’s boundaries, stabilising the stones and managing access.

The involvement of local communities is central to this effort. Tiya sits in an inhabited landscape, and the people who live around it are its natural guardians. Guides drawn from the neighbouring villages now lead visitors around the site, and tourism, modest as it remains, offers some income and a reason for communities to take pride in the monuments. Sustainable interpretation, with good signage, trained guides and a respect for local beliefs, will be vital if Tiya is to endure without becoming a victim of its own recognition.

There is also an enormous amount left to discover. The writing on the stones has not been deciphered, the full extent of the surrounding archaeology has not been mapped, and the cultural context of the people who raised the stelae remains to be understood. Every new survey and excavation has the potential to illuminate a chapter of Ethiopian history for which written records are scarce, and Tiya remains one of the most promising sites for such research.

Walking Among the Stones — Visiting Tiya Today

The experience of visiting Tiya is quiet and unhurried. The site is approached along a rural road, and the stelae appear first as a cluster of grey shapes above the grass, seeming almost to hover at the edge of the fields. A low enclosure marks the protected area, and a local guide will usually meet visitors at the gate, leading them from stone to stone and pointing out the details that an unaided eye might miss: the faint outline of a sword, the double line that may represent a figure’s arms, the bevelled edge where a slab was once dressed.

It is worth moving slowly. The carvings are shallow and the light matters; in the flat glare of midday many designs seem to vanish, while in the early morning or late afternoon they stand out in crisp relief. Visitors with time can circle individual stones to see how the decoration wraps around the slab, and can sit for a while at the edge of the field to take in the whole assembly. Because there are few distractions, Tiya rewards attention in a way that crowded monuments often cannot.

The site is small enough to see in an hour or two, which makes it an excellent addition to a day in the Rift Valley rather than a destination in itself. Combined with nearby churches, lakes and village markets, it offers a glimpse into a side of Ethiopia that few foreign visitors see, one in which the deep past is not set apart from daily life but woven into it.

Reflection — The Dignity of the Unknown

There is something deeply affecting about a monument whose makers cannot be named. At Lalibela, one can read the story of kings and saints; at Aksum, the inscriptions of rulers. At Tiya, the voices are silent, and what remains is a field of stones with signs whose meaning we can only guess. Yet the silence itself is eloquent. These stones were raised by people who cared that their dead should be remembered, who spent labour and skill in cutting, carving and erecting them, and who believed that a name or a deed deserved a lasting form.

In an age that tends to measure heritage by scale and spectacle, Tiya reminds us that a small site can carry great weight. It is not grand, and it asks for no awe. It asks instead for patience and for a willingness to stand in a quiet field and consider that the people who lie there once felt exactly what we feel: that to be remembered is one of the oldest of human desires. That the stones have survived, carrying their unread messages across the centuries, is a small miracle and a quiet rebuke to forgetting.

✦ Conclusion

Tiya is one of the quietest and most absorbing sites on the World Heritage List. Its thirty-two carved stelae, set in an unassuming field in the Ethiopian Rift Valley, mark graves made some eight centuries ago and speak in a symbolic language that archaeology has so far been unable to read. Yet even in its mystery the site conveys a clear message: that the peoples of southern Ethiopia possessed a rich, deliberate and sophisticated culture of memory, expressed in stone.

For the traveller, Tiya offers something rare: a heritage site that is intimate rather than overwhelming, rooted in the everyday landscape and still waiting to be fully understood. It is easy to reach from Addis Ababa on a day trip, and rewarding to combine with other sights of the Rift Valley. Those who take the time to walk among its stones will leave with a lasting impression of a vanished people, and of the strange and tender power of a memorial that has outlasted its makers’ names.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

What is Tiya, and why is it a UNESCO World Heritage Site?

Tiya is an archaeological site in southern Ethiopia, about 90 kilometres south of Addis Ababa, containing thirty-six monuments including thirty-two carved stelae that mark medieval graves. UNESCO inscribed it in 1980 under criteria (i) and (iv) as an outstanding example of a distinctive funerary tradition and a remarkable artistic achievement.

What do the carvings on the stelae mean?

Nobody knows for certain. The most common motifs are sword-like shapes, human-like figures and plant or geometric designs. Researchers have suggested they may refer to warriors, to the number or status of the dead, or to lineage and fertility, but the symbolic system has not been deciphered and interpretations remain tentative.

How old are the stelae at Tiya?

They are generally thought to date from the medieval period, often placed around the twelfth to thirteenth centuries, although the broader tradition of carved stelae in southern Ethiopia appears to have lasted for several centuries. Dating estimates have varied between studies.

How do I get to Tiya from Addis Ababa?

Tiya lies roughly 90 kilometres south of the capital, along the main road toward Hawassa, and is usually visited as a day trip by car. Many visitors combine it with other Rift Valley sights, such as Adadi Mariam rock-hewn church and the lakes of the region. Hiring a local driver or joining a tour is the simplest option.

When is the best time to visit Tiya?

The dry season, roughly October to February, offers the most comfortable conditions and the easiest travel on rural roads. The rainy season, from about June to September, turns the grass green and the ground muddy, which can make the site more picturesque but more difficult to reach.
