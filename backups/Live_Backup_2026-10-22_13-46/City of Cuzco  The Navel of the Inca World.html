The Navel of the World

In the language of the Incas, Quechua, Qosqo meant the navel, and for the people who ruled the largest empire of the pre-Columbian Americas, it was exactly that: the centre of the world. From this city in a high Andean valley, at an altitude of about 3,400 metres, the Inca emperors governed Tawantinsuyu, the Land of the Four Quarters, a realm that at its height stretched from what is now Colombia to Chile and Argentina. Four great roads radiated from its central plaza to the four corners of the empire.

The Spanish conquerors who arrived in 1533 destroyed the Inca state but not the city. They built their own colonial capital on its foundations, adopting the Inca stonework as the base for churches, convents and mansions, and the result is a city in which two civilisations are stacked one upon another. Perfectly fitted Inca walls support baroque façades, and Quechua is still spoken in the streets beneath the bells of colonial churches.

UNESCO inscribed the City of Cuzco in 1983 under criteria (iii) and (iv), recognising it as the former capital of the Inca Empire and an outstanding example of urban fusion of Inca and Spanish traditions. It is also the gateway to Machu Picchu and the Sacred Valley, and for many visitors it is the starting point of an Andean journey.

The Inca Capital — Pachacuti and the Puma City

Inca tradition attributed the founding of Cuzco to Manco Cápac, the first Inca, who emerged from a cave or lake and followed a golden staff until it sank into the earth in the Cuzco valley. Historians suspect that the settlement was occupied for centuries before the Incas came to power, but it was in the fifteenth century, under the ninth ruler, Pachacuti, that Cuzco was transformed from a regional centre into an imperial capital. Pachacuti is credited with rebuilding the city on a grand scale and launching the conquests that created the empire.

According to a long-held tradition, the city was designed in the shape of a puma, a sacred animal of the Incas, with the fortress of Sacsayhuamán as its head, the central plazas as its body and the confluence of two rivers as its tail. While historians now debate how literally this should be understood, the layout of the city does show a deliberate plan, with a ceremonial centre of temples and palaces surrounded by residential districts of stone and adobe.

The heart of the city was the great plaza, Huacaypata, where festivals, ceremonies and gatherings were held, and which was twice as large as the present Plaza de Armas. Around it stood the palaces of the emperors, each ruler building his own, and the houses of the nobility. Cuzco was also a religious centre, with temples dedicated to the Sun, the Moon, the Thunder and other deities, and shrines called huacas, set along invisible lines, called ceques, radiating across the landscape.

Qorikancha and the Perfection of Inca Stone

The most sacred site in Inca Cuzco was the Qorikancha, the Golden Enclosure, or Temple of the Sun. According to Spanish chroniclers, its walls were covered with sheets of gold, its courtyard had a garden of golden plants and animals, and its chambers held mummies of past rulers and statues of the gods. The Spaniards, dazzled and greedy, stripped it of its treasures, melting down the gold for ransom and plunder.

On its foundations, the Dominican friars built the Convent of Santo Domingo, preserving the lower Inca walls, which are among the finest examples of Inca masonry. They are built of carefully shaped blocks of andesite, fitted together so precisely that no mortar is needed, and sometimes without even a gap for a knife blade. The walls slope slightly inward and have trapezoidal doors and niches, features that give them stability in earthquakes. In the earthquake of 1950, the colonial superstructure of Santo Domingo was badly damaged while the Inca walls stood intact.

Elsewhere in the city, on streets like Hatun Rumiyoc, one can see the same craftsmanship in the walls of Inca palaces. The famous twelve-angled stone, a large block with twelve corners fitted exactly into its neighbours, is the best-known example, and visitors gather around it to marvel. The techniques used to cut, move and fit such stones without iron tools or wheels remain a subject of fascination.

Sacsayhuamán — The Fortress Above the City

High on a hill above Cuzco stands the great ceremonial and military complex of Sacsayhuamán. Its most striking feature is a triple line of zigzag walls, built of gigantic limestone blocks, some of which weigh well over a hundred tonnes, fitted together with the same precision as the walls of Cuzco’s palaces. The zigzag form, which may have been intended to represent lightning or the teeth of the puma, also provided a clever defensive geometry.

Sacsayhuamán was more than a fortress. It was a ritual space as well, where ceremonies connected with the Sun were held, and where the annual festival of Inti Raymi was celebrated. Excavations have revealed the foundations of towers, storerooms and water channels, and the great open esplanade in front of the walls was used for gatherings and military displays.

In 1536, during the great Inca rebellion led by Manco Inca, Sacsayhuamán became the scene of a fierce battle, as Inca warriors seized the fortress and besieged the Spaniards in Cuzco. The Spaniards eventually recaptured it after a bloody assault, and the fortress, once the pride of the empire, was later quarried for stone to build the colonial city, which explains why so much of it has vanished. What remains is only a fraction of the original, but it is awe-inspiring.

A Spanish City on Inca Foundations

Francisco Pizarro and his small force entered Cuzco on 15 November 1533, after the capture and execution of the Inca ruler Atahualpa. The Spaniards, impressed by the city’s size and splendour, declared it the capital of their new domain, though Pizarro later founded Lima on the coast as the seat of government. Cuzco remained the centre of the Andean world, and its Spanish settlers began to remake it according to their own ideas.

They built churches and convents on the sites of Inca temples, and mansions of Spanish nobles on the foundations of Inca palaces, so that in many places the Inca stone base and colonial superstructure are visible together. The Cathedral, begun in 1560 on the site of the palace of the Inca Viracocha and completed nearly a century later, was built partly from stones taken from Sacsayhuamán. Beside it stands the Jesuit church of La Compañía, begun in the 1570s and rebuilt after an earthquake in 1650, one of the finest examples of Andean baroque.

The Spanish colonial city, with its arcaded plazas, balconied houses and tiled roofs, took on a character of its own. Streets named after Inca nobles and Spanish saints cross one another, and the entire historic centre forms a compact and harmonious ensemble that blends two worlds in a way found nowhere else in the Americas.

The Cusco School and the Art of the Andes

The art of colonial Cuzco is as distinctive as its architecture. In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, a school of painting developed in the city, known as the Escuela Cusqueña, in which indigenous and mestizo painters, trained in European techniques, created devotional works for churches and convents. Their paintings combine Spanish and Flemish models with local imagery, using rich colours, gold leaf and a flattened, decorative style that departs from European realism.

Among the best-known artists were Diego Quispe Tito, a Quechua painter who worked in the early seventeenth century, and Marcos Zapata, whose paintings from the eighteenth century include the celebrated Last Supper in Cuzco Cathedral, in which the apostles share a meal featuring roast guinea pig, or cuy, a traditional Andean dish. Such details reveal the process of cultural translation by which Christian imagery was adapted to Andean tastes and realities.

The cathedral is also home to the Señor de los Temblores, the Lord of the Earthquakes, a dark crucifix venerated as the protector of Cuzco from tremors, and carried in procession each Easter Monday. The mix of piety, ritual and local colour in these customs shows how Catholicism took root in the Andes, absorbing older beliefs and forms of devotion and creating a distinctive Andean Christianity.

Earthquakes, Rebellion and Reconstruction

Cuzco lies in an active seismic zone, and its history has been punctuated by earthquakes. A major tremor in 1650 destroyed much of the colonial city, which was rebuilt over the following decades in the baroque style that now dominates the historic centre. Another severe earthquake on 21 May 1950 devastated the city again, killing dozens of people, destroying churches and houses, and exposing the Inca foundations beneath.

The reconstruction that followed was guided by an effort to restore the colonial character of the city, while also revealing and preserving Inca remains. Many of the Inca walls that visitors admire today were uncovered at that time, and the experience reinforced the lesson that Inca engineering, with its tight-fitted, slightly inclined stones, was better suited to the Andean environment than the heavier colonial structures built on top.

Cuzco was also a centre of resistance and rebellion. In 1780 to 1781, José Gabriel Condorcanqui, who took the name Túpac Amaru II, led a great uprising against Spanish rule. He was captured and executed in the Plaza de Armas in May 1781, but his revolt became a symbol of indigenous resistance and is still remembered in Peruvian history and culture.

A Living City — Festivals, Markets and Quechua Culture

Cuzco is not only a monument but a living city of around four hundred thousand people, many of whom speak Quechua as well as Spanish, and whose culture still draws on Inca roots. The most spectacular expression is the Inti Raymi, the Festival of the Sun, held each year on 24 June, the winter solstice in the southern hemisphere. A re-creation of the Inca ceremony, with processions, music, costumes and ritual, it draws thousands of spectators to Sacsayhuamán and the plaza.

Other festivals and traditions fill the calendar, from the great Corpus Christi processions, in which statues of saints are carried through the streets, to local celebrations in the surrounding villages. The San Pedro Market, with its stalls of fruit, potatoes, coca leaves, herbs and handicrafts, and the artisans’ quarter of San Blas, with its steep lanes and workshops, offer an immediate sense of everyday Andean life.

This vitality brings challenges. Tourism, which has grown enormously with the popularity of Machu Picchu, has put pressure on the historic centre, with crowding, traffic and the rapid conversion of houses into hotels and restaurants. Balancing the needs of residents with those of visitors, and preserving the authenticity of the city, is one of the main tasks facing its guardians.

Reflection — Two Worlds in One Wall

There is a wall in Cuzco, on one of the lanes below the Plaza de Armas, where the Inca masonry ends and the colonial masonry begins. The lower courses are of beautifully fitted stone, dark and exact; above them rise the uneven courses and plaster of the Spanish period. To touch the point where they meet is to touch the whole history of the Andes, of conquest and survival, of destruction and continuity.

It is a reminder that cities are never entirely made by one people or one era. Cuzco was shaped by the Incas, rebuilt by the Spaniards and sustained by generations of Quechua and mestizo families, and each left their imprint. The result is a city that does not hide its past but wears it openly, in every wall, every arch and every festival, and that invites us to read it with attention and respect.

✦ Conclusion

The City of Cuzco is one of the great historic cities of the Americas, a place where the splendour of the Inca capital and the grandeur of Spanish colonial Peru exist side by side. Its walls, plazas, churches and festivals tell the story of two worlds that clashed and then intertwined, creating a culture that is uniquely Andean.

To explore it is to climb through layers of history, from the perfect stonework of Qorikancha to the baroque gilding of La Compañía, and to feel the pulse of a city that is still alive with tradition. Few places in the world offer so rich or so moving an encounter with the past.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

Why is Cuzco a UNESCO World Heritage Site?

UNESCO inscribed it in 1983 under criteria (iii) and (iv) as the former capital of the Inca Empire and as an outstanding example of an urban ensemble in which Inca and Spanish colonial traditions are combined.

What is Qorikancha?

Qorikancha was the Temple of the Sun, the most sacred site of the Inca Empire. The Spanish built the Convent of Santo Domingo on its foundations, preserving the Inca walls, which are among the finest examples of Inca masonry.

How high is Cuzco, and will altitude affect me?

Cuzco lies at about 3,400 metres above sea level. Many visitors experience altitude sickness, with headache, nausea and breathlessness. It helps to rest on arrival, drink plenty of water, avoid heavy meals and alcohol, and drink coca tea, as locals do. Consult a doctor if you have health concerns.

What is the best time to visit?

The dry season from May to September offers clear skies and cold nights. The rainy season from December to March brings heavy rain and fewer tourists. The Inti Raymi festival on 24 June is the most famous event, but it is very crowded.

How do I visit Machu Picchu from Cuzco?

Most visitors travel by train or road to Ollantaytambo or Aguas Calientes, then take a bus to Machu Picchu. Tickets and train places must be booked well in advance, and rules on circuits and entry times change, so check current requirements.

🧳 Visitor's Guide

Getting There — Fly to Alejandro Velasco Astete International Airport from Lima or other cities; buses and trains connect Cuzco with the Sacred Valley and Puno

Best Time to Visit — May to September for dry weather; book ahead for Inti Raymi in late June

Altitude — Allow one or two days to acclimatise before strenuous activity

Tickets — A tourist ticket covers many archaeological sites around Cuzco; check current options and prices

Must See — Plaza de Armas, Cathedral, La Compañía, Qorikancha, Hatun Rumiyoc street, San Blas and Sacsayhuamán

Sacred Valley — Pisac, Ollantaytambo and Moray are popular excursions that can be combined with Machu Picchu

Safety — Beware of pickpockets in crowded areas and use licensed taxis

Respect the Culture — Ask before photographing people, particularly in traditional dress, and respect religious ceremonies
