Everglades National Park — The River of Grass Under Threat

A Vast Subtropical Wetland at the Southern Tip of Florida, Where a Slow Sheet of Fresh Water Flows Through Sawgrass Marshes, Cypress Domes and Mangrove Forests to Florida Bay, Sheltering Alligators, Crocodiles, Manatees and Wading Birds, and Listed Among World Heritage in Danger | UNESCO World Heritage Site

Location:Miami-Dade, Monroe and Collier counties, southern tip of the Florida peninsula, United States of America
Year of UNESCO Inscription:1979 (on the List of World Heritage in Danger 1993 to 2007 and again from 2010 to the present)
Category:Natural
UNESCO Reference:76
Criteria:(viii)(ix)(x)
Coordinates:Approximately 25°19′N 80°56′W
Area:567,017 hectares (about 5,670 square kilometres)

A River You Cannot See Flowing

Most rivers announce themselves with banks, bends and the sound of moving water. The Everglades has none of these. Standing at the edge of the great sawgrass prairie in southern Florida, you see only a flat horizon of green and gold grass, scattered clumps of trees rising like islands and a sky so large that clouds seem to occupy half the world. The water is there, a thin shining sheet around the stems of the grass, but it moves so slowly, perhaps only tens of metres in a day, that it is almost impossible to perceive. This is the famous river of grass.

UNESCO describes the site at the southern tip of Florida as a river of grass flowing imperceptibly from the hinterland into the sea, and notes that the exceptional variety of its water habitats has made it a sanctuary for a large number of birds and reptiles, as well as for threatened species such as the manatee. It is the largest subtropical wilderness in the United States and, with an area of more than 567,000 hectares, one of the great wetlands of the world.

But the Everglades is also a place of warning. It is one of very few World Heritage sites in a wealthy country to appear on the List of World Heritage in Danger, a status it has held for most of the last three decades. Its story is one of extraordinary natural beauty, of an almost reckless history of drainage and development and of one of the largest ecosystem restoration efforts ever attempted. It is a story that is not yet finished.

A River Wide as a Peninsula

The key to understanding the Everglades is its hydrology. Historically, rain falling in the Kissimmee River valley in central Florida flowed south into Lake Okeechobee, the great shallow lake at the heart of the peninsula. When the lake overflowed in the wet season, the water spilled southward as a broad sheet, perhaps fifty to eighty kilometres wide, across a landscape so flat that it drops only a few centimetres per kilometre. This slow sheet flow carried water through the sawgrass to the mangrove coasts and out into Florida Bay and the Gulf of Mexico.

The writer Marjory Stoneman Douglas gave the system its most enduring name in her 1947 book, The Everglades: River of Grass, and the Seminole people called the land Pa-hay-okee, meaning grassy water. The system runs on a rhythm of seasons: a wet summer, when thunderstorms flood the plains and animals disperse, and a dry winter, when water retreats and life concentrates in pools, creeks and the deep channels called sloughs.

Habitats Within Habitats

The Everglades is not one environment but many, arranged in intricate bands that depend on small differences in elevation and water depth. The sawgrass marsh is the most famous, a sea of sharp-edged sedge that can reach three metres in height. Within it rise cypress domes, circular stands of bald cypress growing in solution holes, and hardwood hammocks, raised islands of tropical trees such as mahogany, gumbo-limbo and strangler fig, which stand just above the high-water line.

Along the higher limestone ridge in the east are pine rocklands, open forests of slash pine over a sharp limestone floor. At the coast the marsh gives way to mangroves, whose tangled prop roots form what the National Park Service describes as the largest contiguous stand of protected mangrove forest in the Western Hemisphere. Beyond them lies Florida Bay, with its shallow seagrass meadows that nurture fish, shrimp and sea turtles. Each habitat supports its own community, and together they form one of the most varied wetlands on Earth.

Alligators, Crocodiles and Panthers: The Wildlife

The Everglades is the only place in the world where alligators and crocodiles live side by side. The American alligator inhabits the freshwater marshes, digging gator holes that hold water through the dry season and shelter fish, turtles and birds, making it a keystone species. The American crocodile, a shyer and more endangered relative, favours the brackish waters of the coast and Florida Bay.

Other residents include the West Indian manatee, grazing in the warm shallows, the bottlenose dolphin, sea turtles, snook and tarpon, and the elusive Florida panther, a critically endangered subspecies of the cougar that survives in small numbers in South Florida. The list of animals is long, and for many of them the Everglades is among the last refuges in the United States.

The Birds of the Glades

Birds are the glory of the Everglades. Wading birds such as great blue herons, great egrets, white ibises, wood storks and roseate spoonbills gather in the thousands where falling water concentrates fish. At the height of the dry season a single pool may hold hundreds of birds feeding together, a spectacle that has drawn naturalists for more than a century.

The wading birds also tell a conservation story. In the late nineteenth century the plumes of egrets and other birds were fashionable on women’s hats, and plume hunters slaughtered them in vast numbers, bringing some species close to extinction. The outrage that followed helped spark the American bird protection movement. Today the Everglades remains a stronghold for wading birds, and even rarities occasionally appear, such as a flock of 125 flamingos reported in recent years, the largest seen in a decade.

First Peoples of the Glades

People have lived in and around the Everglades for thousands of years. The Calusa and Tequesta peoples of the southern Florida coasts built shell mounds and fished the estuaries, while later the Seminole and Miccosukee peoples, many of whom took refuge in the Everglades during the nineteenth-century Seminole Wars, learned to live on the tree islands, poling dugout canoes through the sawgrass. Their descendants remain in the region, and their knowledge of the land is part of its story.

For the Seminole and Miccosukee the Everglades is not a wilderness in the sense of a place without people but a homeland that sustained them, and their relationship with it continues in the present. Any account of the park must acknowledge that the land was lived in, named and used long before it became a protected area.

Draining the Swamp

To the settlers who arrived in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, the Everglades was a wasteland to be conquered. Beginning in the 1880s and accelerating in the early twentieth century, canals were dug to drain the marsh for farming and towns. The Tamiami Trail, a road across the southern Everglades completed in 1928, acted like a dam across the sheet flow, and after severe floods in the 1940s the federal government built a vast network of canals, levees and pumps known as the Central and Southern Florida Project, designed to control the water for agriculture and flood protection.

The works succeeded in making South Florida habitable for millions of people and for a rich agricultural industry, but they dismantled the natural plumbing of the Everglades. Water was diverted to the sea, flows were cut off and polluted with fertiliser runoff, and about half of the original wetland was lost to farms and cities. What remained was a fragment dependent on a plumbing system built for other purposes.

Creating the Park and Winning World Recognition

Even as the drainage proceeded, a movement grew to protect what remained. Everglades National Park was dedicated in December 1947, the same year that Douglas published her book, making it the first national park in the United States created to protect a fragile ecosystem rather than scenic grandeur. The park was later recognised as an international Biosphere Reserve in 1976, inscribed on the World Heritage List in 1979 and designated a Wetland of International Importance under the Ramsar Convention in 1987.

UNESCO inscribed it under three natural criteria. Criterion (viii) recognises outstanding examples of ongoing geological and hydrological processes, criterion (ix) recognises ongoing ecological processes in the evolution of ecosystems, and criterion (x) recognises the most important habitats for biodiversity, including threatened species. Few sites in the world satisfy all three so completely.

The Danger List: 1993 to 2007 and Since 2010

In 1992 Hurricane Andrew struck South Florida with devastating force, and the following year, concerned about the deterioration of the park’s ecological integrity, the World Heritage Committee placed Everglades National Park on the List of World Heritage in Danger. The decision acknowledged damage from hurricanes, but also the deeper problems of altered water flows, nutrient pollution and the loss of habitat.

The United States made progress, and in 2007 the park was removed from the Danger List at the request of the American government. But conditions did not improve as hoped, and in 2010 the Obama administration itself asked the Committee to place the park back on the list, arguing that doing so would underline the country’s commitment to restoring the ecosystem. UNESCO’s records show the park has been on the Danger List from 1993 to 2007 and from 2010 to the present.

The Biggest Restoration Ever Attempted

The response to the crisis has been one of the most ambitious restoration programmes in history. The Comprehensive Everglades Restoration Plan, approved by the United States Congress in 2000, aims to redirect water to restore more natural flows, improve water quality and re-establish the flow of fresh water to Florida Bay. Projects include storage reservoirs, treatment marshes, the removal of obstructive canals and levees and the bridging of sections of the Tamiami Trail so that water can flow beneath it.

Progress has been real but slow, hindered by cost, politics and the sheer complexity of an engineered system. Meanwhile new problems have emerged: invasive species such as Burmese pythons, which have devastated populations of small mammals; excess phosphorus and other nutrients from agriculture and urban runoff; saltwater intrusion and sea level rise, which are pushing salt farther into the freshwater marshes; and increasingly intense storms. Restoration is a race between the pace of repair and the pace of change.

The Dolphin Expressway Debate

A recent flashpoint is a proposal to extend the SR 836 Dolphin Expressway in Miami-Dade County into the Greater Everglades wetland ecosystem. In 2025 the World Heritage Committee expressed its utmost concern about the potential adverse impacts of the extension, urged the United States to identify alternatives that avoid harm to the property, and asked for an environmental impact assessment before any decisions that would be difficult to reverse. A legal challenge to the project in state courts was reported as ongoing.

In 2026, at its 48th session in Busan, the Committee decided again to retain the park on the Danger List. It reiterated its request that the United States invite a joint World Heritage Centre and IUCN Reactive Monitoring mission to assess the corrective measures, asked for an updated report by 1 December 2026 and noted that a full report will be examined at its 49th session in 2027. The park’s status, in other words, remains under active international review.

Visiting the Everglades

The park has several distinct entrances. The main one, near Homestead in the south, leads to the visitor centre and the Anhinga Trail, a boardwalk where alligators, anhingas and herons are often seen at close range. Shark Valley, off the Tamiami Trail, offers a tram and bicycle loop to an observation tower with long views over the sawgrass. The Gulf Coast area at Everglades City provides access to the Ten Thousand Islands by boat, and Flamingo, at the southern end of the main road, is a base for canoeing, kayaking and fishing in Florida Bay.

The best time to visit is the dry season, from about December to April, when wildlife concentrates, temperatures are mild and mosquitoes are fewer. The summer wet season is hot, humid and buggy, though storms bring dramatic skies. Visitors should bring sun protection and insect repellent, keep a safe distance from alligators and never feed wildlife, and check current conditions and fees with the National Park Service before travelling.

Reflection — Can a Nation Undo a Mistake?

The Everglades poses an uncomfortable question: what do we owe to a landscape we have damaged, and how much of the damage can be repaired? Few places illustrate more vividly the tension between development and nature. The same engineering that made South Florida habitable for millions broke the slow circulation on which the Everglades depended, and restoring it means unpicking some of that engineering while a growing population still relies on it.

The park’s place on the Danger List is not an insult but a spotlight. Like Yellowstone National Park, USA, the Everglades was born of an American conviction that some places should be kept for everyone, and like the Great Barrier Reef, Australia, it shows that even the wealthiest nations struggle to protect ecosystems against pollution and a changing climate. The question is whether the same ingenuity that drained the marsh can now be used to heal it.

✦ Conclusion

Everglades National Park is a place of subtle grandeur. It has no mountains or waterfalls, only water, grass and sky, yet it supports a richness of life that few landscapes can match. Its alligators and wading birds, its mangroves and sloughs and its slow, silent river of grass form one of the great natural systems of the world.

It is also a place where the future is still being decided. The restoration of the Everglades will take decades and require patience, money and political will, and the World Heritage Committee’s repeated decisions to retain the park on the Danger List are a reminder that the work is incomplete. For the visitor, it is both a pleasure and a responsibility to see it.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

Why is Everglades National Park a World Heritage Site?

It was inscribed in 1979 under criteria (viii), (ix) and (x) for its outstanding hydrological and geological processes, its ongoing ecological processes and its importance for biodiversity. UNESCO describes it as a river of grass and a sanctuary for birds, reptiles and threatened species such as the manatee.

Is Everglades National Park on the List of World Heritage in Danger?

Yes. UNESCO records show it was on the Danger List from 1993 to 2007 and again from 2010 to the present. The World Heritage Committee decided to retain it on the list in 2024, 2025 and 2026.

What is the river of grass?

It is the name for the Everglades’ slow, shallow sheet of fresh water that flows from Lake Okeechobee southward through sawgrass marshes to the sea. The phrase was popularised by Marjory Stoneman Douglas’s 1947 book and echoes the Seminole name for the land, meaning grassy water.

What animals live in the Everglades?

The park is home to American alligators and American crocodiles, which live side by side nowhere else, as well as manatees, Florida panthers, sea turtles and many wading birds such as herons, egrets, ibises, wood storks and roseate spoonbills.

When is the best time to visit?

The dry season from about December to April has the best wildlife viewing, mild temperatures and fewer mosquitoes. The summer wet season is hot and humid, so plan accordingly and check current conditions with the National Park Service.
