Los Glaciares National Park — Where the Ice Age Still Breathes at the End of the World

A Vast Patagonian Wilderness of Calving Glaciers, Turquoise Lakes and Granite Spires, Where the Perito Moreno Glacier Dams an Arm of Lake Argentino and Bursts Its Own Wall, and Where Fitz Roy and Cerro Torre Rise above the Remnant of the Last Great Glaciation | UNESCO World Heritage Site

Year of UNESCO Inscription:1981 (no extensions)

Where the Ice Meets the Steppe

At the southern end of the Americas the land seems to lose its nerve. The Andes, which for seven thousand kilometres have held their ranks like an army on the march, break into islands, fjords and rags of rock, and over the last of the great peaks there settles a quilt of ice that has outlasted the age that made it. Patagonia's Argentine edge, in the far south-west of Santa Cruz Province, is the home of Los Glaciares National Park, a wilderness of 726,927 hectares in which the Ice Age is not a chapter in a textbook but a weather report. Wind comes off the ice with the smell of cold stone. Icebergs the colour of old porcelain drift on lakes whose water has the opacity of milk. And somewhere beyond the next ridge, with a report like artillery, a wall of ice the height of a twenty-storey building shears away and falls into the water.

The park was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1981, under two of the natural criteria. Criterion (vii) recognises the superlative natural beauty of a landscape in which granite peaks of more than three thousand metres stand above glaciers and glacial lakes, and criterion (viii) recognises a living laboratory of Earth history, in which the advances and retreats of the Pleistocene and the Holocene are written in lake basins, moraines and valleys that are still being carved. The two judgments belong together, for the beauty of Los Glaciares is not decorative; it is the visible consequence of processes that are still at work, and a visitor who stands before the ice is looking not at a monument to the past but at a machine that continues to run.

What makes the place unusual, even among the great mountain parks of the world, is the abruptness of its contrasts. Within a few hours of travel one may pass from the dry, wind-scoured Patagonian steppe, where guanacos graze among tussocks and the horizon is a ruled line, into a dark and mossy forest of southern beech, and from there to a lake-shore from which the great glaciers descend almost to the waterline. The ice field that feeds them lies to the west, on the Chilean frontier, and it is the engine that explains everything else: the lakes, the rivers, the forests, the spires of the north, and the peculiar fame of one glacier in particular, which has become a pilgrimage for travellers from every continent.

The Last Great Ice Field

A Relic of the Glaciations

Los Glaciares takes its name from the Southern Patagonian Ice Field, known in Spanish as the Hielo Continental, a body of ice about 350 kilometres long that stretches along the Andes between roughly 48 and 51 degrees south. Sources differ slightly on its area, but it covers on the order of sixteen thousand square kilometres, of which the great majority lies in Chile and a smaller portion in Argentina. It is the second largest contiguous ice field outside the polar regions, behind only the great icefield complex of south-eastern Alaska and the Yukon, which is itself protected as Kluane / Wrangell-St. Elias / Glacier Bay / Tatshenshini-Alsek. UNESCO describes the Patagonian field as a relict of the Quaternary glaciation, a surviving fragment of the mantle of ice that once smothered the entire southern Andes, and the description is exact. When the great ice sheets of the Pleistocene retreated, they left behind a sculpted landscape and a few last strongholds of the cold. This is the largest of those strongholds in the Southern Hemisphere outside Antarctica.

Because the field is so large, and because it sits in a latitude of storms, its glaciers behave differently from those of lower and drier ranges. Most mountain glaciers begin high on the peaks, at altitudes of around two and a half thousand metres. Here the ice begins at about fifteen hundred metres and descends to as little as two hundred, so that the tongues of the glaciers reach into forest and lake rather than stopping on bare rock. The Wikipedia account of the park counts forty-seven large glaciers fed by the ice field, thirteen of which flow toward the Pacific, and estimates that roughly thirty per cent of the park is covered by ice, while UNESCO's own text speaks of about half of the property being glaciated.

The Giants of the Argentine Side

On the Argentine side the ice drains eastward into two great lakes. In the south lies Lake Argentino, which with an area of about 1,466 square kilometres is the largest lake in the country, and in the north lies Lake Viedma, of about 1,100 square kilometres. Both discharge into the Santa Cruz River, which carries the meltwater across the steppe to the Atlantic at Puerto Santa Cruz, so that ice formed in the mountains of the Pacific watershed ends its journey in the South Atlantic. Into Lake Argentino calve the Upsala, Spegazzini, Onelli and Perito Moreno glaciers; to the north the Viedma Glacier, which covers some 978 square kilometres on the Argentine side, pours into the lake that bears its name. Upsala, at about 765 square kilometres on the Argentine side, is reached only by boat, and excursion vessels carry visitors through fields of floating ice to its front and to those of its neighbours.

The Glacier That Bursts Its Own Wall

Anatomy of a Giant

Of all the glaciers of Los Glaciares, one has outshone the others, and it is not the largest. The Perito Moreno Glacier covers about 250 square kilometres, runs for some thirty kilometres from its source in the ice field, and presents to Lake Argentino a front about five kilometres wide and, on average, some seventy-four metres above the water, with a total ice depth that has been measured at around 170 metres. UNESCO, with a different measuring convention, describes the face as up to sixty metres high. It is a sheer, shattered cliff of white and blue, riven with crevasses and spires, and what has made it famous is the combination of its beauty and its accessibility. At Perito Moreno, a system of walkways has been built on the opposite peninsula, so that one may stand facing the ice at close range, in safety, on dry land, and listen to the glacier as it speaks.

The sounds are the real marvel. Ice does not merely fall; it groans, cracks and detonates, and then a tower of ice leans, hesitates and drops into the lake, throwing up a plume of spray and a surge of water that rolls across the bay. It is an experience that stirs the awe people feel before things that are vastly larger and slower than they are, and that are nonetheless, at certain moments, startlingly alive.

The Dam and the Rupture

The glacier's most remarkable behaviour, however, is a drama that unfolds over years. The front of the Perito Moreno does not simply end in the lake; it pushes across it. The glacier advances until it touches the opposite shore of the Magallanes Peninsula and, in so doing, divides Lake Argentino into two parts, the main body of the lake and a side-arm known as the Brazo Rico. The narrow channel linking the two, the Canal de los Témpanos, is then sealed by a wall of ice. With the outflow blocked, meltwater and rain continue to pour into the Brazo Rico and its level rises, over months, by as much as thirty metres above that of the main lake. The water seeps into the ice and begins to carve a tunnel under the pressure, the tunnel becomes an arch, and the arch, at last, collapses, in the event known as the rupture, one of the most celebrated natural spectacles in Argentina.

The first rupture recorded in the literature occurred in 1917, after the glacier closed the channel for the first time, and many have followed, among them those of 1935, 1940, 1953, 1956, 1980, 1984 and 1988, and in this century those of 2004, 2006, 2008, 2012, 2016 and 2018, with a smaller event in 2019. The intervals have varied, and the rupture is famously impossible to schedule; the one in 2008 was the first to occur in winter. For years the Perito Moreno also puzzled glaciologists because it stayed close to equilibrium while so many glaciers elsewhere shrank, gaining about as much mass as it lost. The reasons for that long stability are still debated, and reports indicate that since about 2020 its northern front has been retreating, a change that scientists interpret with caution and that may signal the end of an anomaly rather than the beginning of a crisis.

Explorers, Names and a Boundary of Ice

The Perito and His Mountain

The name by which the glacier is known belongs to Francisco Pascasio Moreno, the Argentine explorer and naturalist who surveyed these regions in the nineteenth century and who is known by the honorific Perito, meaning expert, because he served as an expert adviser to Argentina in its boundary dispute with Chile. The glacier had been known by other names, among them Francisco Gormaz, after a Chilean naval official, and Bismarck, before an Argentine lieutenant, Iglesias, named it Perito Moreno in 1899, and the 1902 arbitral award that settled much of the frontier question supported positions Moreno had argued. The renaming is a reminder that in Patagonia the naming of mountains and ice was never a neutral act. In a land whose borders were still being drawn, a name was a claim, and an explorer's map could be an instrument of diplomacy.

The same Moreno saw, in 1877, a great spire of granite in the north of the region and named it after Robert FitzRoy, the captain of HMS Beagle, who had charted much of the Patagonian coast in the 1830s. It is a connection that gives a faint, pleasing echo to the study of the natural world: the same ship that carried the young Charles Darwin through these southern waters also carried him on to the archipelago described in Galapagos Islands, Ecuador, and the name of her captain now crowns one of the most dramatic peaks in the Americas. The Tehuelche people had long had their own name for it. They called it Chaltén, a word usually translated as smoking mountain, because a cloud so often clings to the summit that the peak seems to be exhaling.

The Line That Was Never Finished

The ice itself has been a subject of diplomacy. In 1998 Argentina and Chile agreed to redraw parts of their boundary across the Southern Patagonian Ice Field, in the stretch between Mount Fitz Roy and Cerro Daudet, but the remaining section, running from Fitz Roy to Cerro Murallón, was left undefined until a detailed map could be completed and further negotiations held. It is described as the last unconcluded section of the border between the two countries, a frontier drawn across ice that flows and changes shape faster than any surveyor can fix it.

The Granite Spires of the North

The Mountain That Smokes

North of Lake Viedma the park changes character. The ice remains, but now it is overshadowed by a skyline of bare granite needles, towers and walls that seem designed to challenge the climber and humble the walker. At their centre stands Fitz Roy, which tourism sources give as 3,405 metres, and which was first climbed on 2 February 1952 by the French alpinists Lionel Terray and Guido Magnone, by the south-east ridge that is now known as the Franco-Argentine route. Among the members of the party that climbed the south-west ridge in 1968 was Yvon Chouinard, who later founded the outdoor clothing company Patagonia.

The base of the mountain is reached from El Chaltén, a small settlement about 220 kilometres north of El Calafate, at an elevation of around 400 metres. It owes its existence to the same diplomatic history as the glaciers: it grew from Argentina's effort to establish a presence in a disputed zone, which was awarded to it in 1985, and it has become the national capital of trekking, with a 2022 census population of 1,861. There, a few steps from the last houses, the trails begin, and among them are the routes to the Laguna de los Tres, at the very foot of Fitz Roy, and the Laguna Torre, beneath the spire of Cerro Torre.

The Tower and the Compressor

Cerro Torre, a spire of granite and ice that reaches 3,128 metres, has a history that reads as a parable of mountaineering ethics. In 1959 the Italian climber Cesare Maestri claimed that he and the Austrian Toni Egger had reached the summit, and that Egger had been swept away by an avalanche on the descent, taking with him the camera that held the only photographic proof. Most mountaineers have doubted the claim, given the inconsistencies in his account and the absence of pitons, bolts or fixed ropes on the route he described, and in 2005 another party that climbed the face he had named found a route that differed markedly from his description. In 1970 Maestri returned with a gas-powered compressor and drilled a ladder of bolts some 350 metres up the south-east face, stopping about a hundred metres below the summit. The act was widely condemned.

The first undisputed ascent was made in 1974 by the Italian climbers known as the Ragni di Lecco, and in January 2012 two young climbers, Hayden Kennedy and Jason Kruk, made a fair-means ascent of the south-east ridge and, on the way down, removed some 125 of Maestri's bolts. The climbing community has largely accepted the removal as a restoration of the mountain's character, a reminder that these peaks are places whose difficulty is part of their value.

Forests, Steppe and Wild Creatures

The Belt Between Ice and Wind

The climate of Los Glaciares is cool, moist and capricious. Mean temperatures range from a little above freezing in winter to about thirteen degrees Celsius in summer, and annual precipitation averages about 500 millimetres in the west and 900 in the east. The mountains catch the moisture from the Pacific, so that the steppe on the Argentine side is arid. Between them lies a belt of Magellanic subpolar forest, composed principally of lenga and guindo, two species of southern beech, with some ñire. Beyond it the forest gives way to the West Patagonian steppe, a landscape of tussock grass and cushion plants that seems at first glance sparse and which turns out, on closer acquaintance, to be rich and resilient.

Condor, Huemul and Puma

The fauna is correspondingly distinctive. UNESCO notes the presence of the puma, the Andean cat, and the huemul, a rare deer of the southern Andes, and records important breeding populations of the Andean condor and of Darwin's rhea, known locally as the choique. Guanacos, which have declined under pressure from livestock grazing, still wander the steppe, and the more than one hundred bird species recorded include the torrent duck, which lives in the fast streams of the forest. The park suffers from introduced species, among them cattle, European hares and trout, and from the perennial problems of overgrazing, forest fires and the pressures of tourism, which brought some 688,837 visitors in 2023. Beauty, in a place like this, is a responsibility as well as a gift.

Ice in a Warming World

A Record of Climate Change

The second criterion on which Los Glaciares was inscribed, the record of Earth's history, has acquired a new urgency. UNESCO notes that the site is valuable for research into climate change, and the numbers that have emerged from the study of the Southern Patagonian Ice Field are sobering. Measurements of the field's mass balance indicate a loss of roughly twenty gigatonnes of ice per year between 2000 and 2012, and the figure for the late 1990s was higher still. Some of the glaciers that flow from the field have retreated dramatically, leaving behind new lakes and bare rock, and the long steadiness of the Perito Moreno, which made it a kind of exception, may not last. Reports cited in standard references also note that the glacier has thinned considerably since 2019.

What We Owe the Ice

There is no easy lesson in a landscape of this kind. In the long view of geology a few decades are nothing, but human beings live in short views, and what they see is a wall of ice that is not quite what it was. The most respectful way to meet it is with attention: to walk the trails without leaving them, to take no ice and no stones, and to remember that the thunder of the falling glacier is also, in its own way, a message about time.

Reflection

Few places remove the sense of one's own scale as effectively as Los Glaciares does. A cathedral may humble us by its ambition; a glacier humbles us by its indifference. It does not care that we are watching, and that is precisely the point. In an age when so much of what we see has been designed to attract our attention, here is a vast structure of ice that has been advancing and retreating, breathing in snow and exhaling water, long before there were people to give it a name, and that will go on doing so, in some form, long after the last of the walkways has been dismantled.

And yet the glacier is not simply a relic. It moves, it speaks, it breaks its own wall and heals it. That combination of permanence and change, of immense age and daily activity, is the particular gift of Patagonia. To travel to the end of the world and stand on the edge of a lake at sunset, watching a blue cliff of ice turn gold, and to hear, across half a mile of water, the slow crack of its next collapse, is to be reminded that the Earth is not finished, and that we are guests in a house that is still being built.

✦ Also Read

If the sight of living glaciers has caught your imagination, continue to Te Wahipounamu, South West New Zealand, where the Franz Josef and Fox glaciers descend from the Southern Alps almost to the edge of temperate rainforest, in a landscape that offers a striking Southern Hemisphere parallel to Patagonia. For a very different and equally dramatic expression of the planet's restless geology, turn to Tongariro National Park, New Zealand, where fire rather than ice shapes the mountains and where a sacred landscape of volcanoes is crossed on foot by one of the finest day walks in the world.

✦ Conclusion

Los Glaciares National Park is a place where the great processes of the Earth are performed at a human scale. One may reach out and almost touch the Perito Moreno, may sleep in a village at the foot of Fitz Roy, may sail among icebergs for a morning and be back in a warm bar by evening. That accessibility is the secret of its popularity and also its challenge: a wilderness that is easy to love is also a wilderness that is easy to harm. Its UNESCO inscription of 1981, under criteria (vii) and (viii), recognises this double character, the beauty that draws the crowds and the science that explains them.

The glaciers of Santa Cruz will continue to calve and the lakes to rise and fall, and for as long as they do so, they will offer one of the finest lessons the planet has to give. They tell us that the world is old and mobile, that beauty and violence are neighbours, and that the most enduring things are often the ones that are always changing. To visit Los Glaciares is to be reminded of this, in the plainest possible way, with the sound of falling ice carried over cold water on the Patagonian wind.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

1. When was Los Glaciares National Park inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List?

It was inscribed in 1981 as reference 145, under natural criteria (vii) and (viii). UNESCO lists its area as 726,927 hectares, although the property's brief synthesis speaks of roughly 600,000 hectares, and the park itself was established in 1937.

2. What is special about the Perito Moreno Glacier?

It is a glacier of about 250 square kilometres and some 30 kilometres in length, whose front, about 5 kilometres wide, advances across Lake Argentino and dams the Brazo Rico. When the water behind the ice rises high enough, it breaks through in a rupture, and the glacier is also notable for having remained close to equilibrium for decades while many glaciers elsewhere retreated. Its accessible walkways make it one of the few large calving glaciers that can be viewed at close range from land.

3. How often does the Perito Moreno rupture?

There is no fixed schedule. Documented ruptures took place in 1917, 1940, 1953, 1980 and 1988, and in this century in 2004, 2006, 2008, 2012, 2016 and 2018, with a smaller event in 2019, but the intervals have varied and scientists cannot predict the exact moment. Ruptures are most often associated with summer, although the one in 2008 was the first recorded in winter.

4. How do I reach the park, and where should I stay?

The Perito Moreno sector is reached from El Calafate, a town on the shore of Lake Argentino that is outside the park boundary and houses the park administration. The northern sector, with Fitz Roy and Cerro Torre, is based at El Chaltén, about 220 kilometres north of El Calafate. Most visitors base themselves in one or both of these towns.

5. What is the best time to visit Los Glaciares?

Most visitors travel during the austral spring and summer, roughly from October to April, when the days are longest and the trails and boat services are at their most accessible. The climate is changeable and cool even in summer, so layered clothing and windproof outerwear are essential, and conditions can alter within hours. Check opening hours and trail conditions with the park authorities before travelling.
