Virunga National Park — Where Gorillas, Lava and Glaciers Share a War-Torn Paradise

Africa's Oldest National Park, a Rift-Valley Wilderness of Active Volcanoes, Mountain Gorillas, Hippo-Crowded Rivers and Equatorial Glaciers, Guarded at Terrible Cost by Its Rangers and Inscribed on the List of World Heritage in Danger since 1994 | UNESCO World Heritage Site

Location:North Kivu Province, eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo, along the borders with Rwanda and Uganda in the Albertine Rift (nearest city: Goma)

Category:Natural
UNESCO Reference:63

Coordinates:0°55′S 29°10′E (approximate centre of the property)
Area:About 790,000 hectares (roughly 7,900 km²); UNESCO's property data lists 800,000 hectares

The Park at the Heart of Africa's Troubles

There are places on Earth whose beauty and whose suffering seem to have been born together, and Virunga is one of them. Lay a finger on a map of Central Africa at the point where the great Rift Valley splits the continent, where Lake Kivu lies like a long blue blade between the Congo and Rwanda, and you will be touching a country within a country: a wilderness of some 790,000 hectares in which a visitor, in a single week and in a single park, might in principle stand on the rim of a volcano whose crater holds a lake of molten rock, sit within a few paces of a mountain gorilla, watch hippopotamuses wallow in a river that runs towards the Nile, and look up at the glaciers of the Rwenzori, the legendary Mountains of the Moon, floating over the equator.

It is a diversity that no other protected area in Africa can match, and it is the reason that UNESCO inscribed Virunga National Park on the World Heritage List in 1979 among the earliest natural properties in Africa to be so honoured. The property is recognised under three criteria. Criterion (vii) acknowledges its superlative natural phenomena and scenic beauty, criterion (viii) its volcanic and rift-valley geology, and criterion (x) its extraordinary biodiversity, which includes the mountain gorilla, one of the most celebrated and most imperilled of all the great apes. UNESCO describes a park that ranges from swamps and steppes to the snows of the Rwenzori peaks, and that serves as an important wintering ground for migratory birds from the Palearctic.

Yet no honest portrait of Virunga can stop at its wonders, for the park is also one of the most dangerous protected landscapes in the world. It has been on the List of World Heritage in Danger since 1994, when the aftermath of the Rwandan genocide drove an exodus of refugees across the border and into its forests. Armed groups, poachers, charcoal traders and, in recent years, a resurgent rebel movement known as M23 have fought over it ever since, and the men and women who guard it, among the bravest conservationists alive, have paid for that duty with their lives. This post tells the story of the park as a place of wonder, and as a place of sorrow, in the belief that the one cannot be understood without the other.

A Park Born of a King's Conscience

Albert National Park

The story of Virunga as a protected area begins in the colonial era, which is to say that it begins in contradiction. In April 1925 the Belgian authorities, who then governed the Congo, created Albert National Park, named for their reigning king and the first national park to be established on the African continent. The idea owed much to Belgian naturalists, among them Victor van Straelen, Jean Massart and Jean-Marie Derscheid, who had persuaded the Crown that the high volcanic forests of the Virunga range, where the last mountain gorillas lived, deserved protection from hunters and from the plough. It was a conservationist gesture of real vision, and it was made, like so many such gestures in that age, over the heads of the people who lived on the land.

The first park was modest in extent and focused on the volcano chain, but it grew in the following decades to take in the plains and lakeshore to the north and the western flanks of the Rwenzori. Scientists from across the world worked there in the years before independence, and the Congolese name of the park changed with the country's fortunes, from Albert to Virunga after independence, a name taken from the volcanic range whose peaks form the southern skyline of the reserve.

A Landscape in Four Parts

To understand Virunga one must grasp its geography, for it is not one wilderness but several strung out along the Albertine Rift for hundreds of kilometres. In the south lies the volcano sector, with the active cones of Nyiragongo and Nyamuragira and the dormant Mikeno, which rises to roughly 4,400 metres, and with the mist-draped forests where the gorillas live. In the centre lies the great savannah of the Rwindi plains and the western and southern shores of Lake Edward, a country of papyrus, acacia and hippopotamus pools. In the north rises the Congolese flank of the Rwenzori range, with Mount Stanley reaching 5,109 metres and a remnant of equatorial ice. Between them run the rivers and forests that join the sectors into a single ecological whole, which is one of the reasons that the park's boundaries matter so much to its survival.

The Fire Mountains

Nyiragongo, the Lake of Fire

If Virunga has an emblem, it is Nyiragongo. The volcano rises to about 3,470 metres above the shore of Lake Kivu, a steep-sided stratovolcano whose summit is occupied by a caldera about 1.2 kilometres across, and in the floor of that caldera lies a lava lake that has fascinated volcanologists for generations. The lava of Nyiragongo is unusually fluid, so fluid that when its crater wall or flank has been breached the lava has run downhill at terrifying speed. For the visitors who have made the climb, a steep ascent through forest and then bare rock to a summit hut on the rim, the reward is a view of an inferno in the middle of the night: a surface of crusted black skin cracking into rivers of orange light, a thunder of degassing, a heat that strikes the face from hundreds of metres away.

The volcano is beautiful, and it is also a threat to the city that lies at its foot. Goma, home to a population in the hundreds of thousands, is built partly upon the lava of earlier eruptions. In 2002 Nyiragongo erupted and sent lava through the city, an event in which, according to Virunga's own history, 147 people were killed and some four hundred thousand were evacuated. On 22 May 2021 it erupted again, and again the lava reached the outskirts of Goma, destroying homes and leaving tens of thousands without shelter.

Nyamuragira and the Rift Beneath

Nyiragongo's neighbour, Nyamuragira, rises to roughly 3,000 metres to the north-west and is one of the most active volcanoes in Africa. It is a broad shield volcano, built less by explosion than by the repeated outpouring of lava, and its flows have crossed the forest and the lowlands for kilometres, creating a landscape of black rock in which pioneer plants gradually take hold. Together the two volcanoes are a window into the process that is slowly tearing East Africa apart. The Albertine Rift, the western arm of the great African Rift system, is a place where the continental crust is stretching and thinning, and where the heat of the mantle finds its way to the surface. It is this process that creates the deep lakes, the rift escarpments and the fertile volcanic soils that support the dense human population of the region, and the same process makes the ground tremble beneath the cities of Goma and Gisenyi.

The Mountains of the Moon

The northern sector of the park rises into one of the most improbable landscapes in Africa. The Rwenzori, which Ptolemy is said to have imagined as the source of the Nile and which later European explorers learned to call the Mountains of the Moon, are a fault-block range, not volcanic, upthrust out of the rift to heights above five thousand metres on the equator. Their upper slopes carry glaciers that are retreating with the warming of the climate, and below them lie belts of giant heather, giant groundsel and lobelia, which grow to a scale that seems to belong to a fairy tale, and which are sheathed in moss and dripping with mist.

Lake Edward, the Rwindi Plains and the Great Herds

Descending from the mountains one reaches the central sector, where the land flattens into savannah and the Rwindi and Ishasha rivers wander towards Lake Edward. This was once among the richest game country in Africa. The shores of the lake and the rivers that feed it hold one of the largest concentrations of hippopotamuses to be found anywhere, and the plains support buffalo, elephant, topi, waterbuck and the lions who prey upon them, as well as an astonishing variety of birds, from fish eagles to the migrants that arrive from the Palearctic each northern winter. The Ishasha flats, in the south of this sector, are a haunt of topi. The park's wildlife has suffered severely from poaching and armed conflict, and hippopotamus numbers in particular have fallen dramatically since the middle of the twentieth century.

The Gorillas of the Mist

A Species Brought Back from the Edge

The mountain gorilla, Gorilla beringei beringei, survives in only two places on Earth: the Virunga massif, which is shared among the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Rwanda and Uganda, and the forest of Bwindi in Uganda. The population was so small in the twentieth century that extinction seemed likely. A cross-border census in 2016 counted 604 gorillas in the Virunga massif, of which 286 lived on the Congolese side, and in 2018 the International Union for Conservation of Nature moved the mountain gorilla from Critically Endangered to Endangered, a rare and hard-won piece of good news in conservation. In 2024, according to reporting by Mongabay, the population of the Virunga region was boosted by ten births.

The recovery is the work of patience and courage, of rangers who follow the gorilla families from day to day, of vets who treat snared animals, and of communities who have come, in many places, to see the apes as an asset and not a threat. It is a story that is not guaranteed a happy ending. The gorillas still suffer from snares set for other animals, from respiratory diseases caught from humans, and from the destruction of their habitat at the park's margin, and in times of conflict some families have moved across the border to Rwanda or Uganda.

Orphans and Their Keepers

Virunga also became known for the care of orphaned gorillas, whose parents had been killed by poachers or in the fighting. The Senkwekwe Centre, established in 2009 and named after a silverback killed in the park, cared for such orphans, and the young animals and their human keepers were the subject of countless photographs. As of December 2023 the centre has been closed, for the simple and happy reason that it no longer had any orphans to look after.

The Price of Guarding Paradise

The Rangers

No account of Virunga can avoid the rangers. The park employs several hundred staff, among them armed rangers, many of whom are local men and women, and its leaders have a long record of public candour about the dangers. Reporting in 2025 put the number of rangers killed in the park's history at nearly two hundred, while a 2026 analysis in The Corridor put it at more than two hundred, the great majority in the last two decades. The deadliest single attack in recent memory came on 24 April 2020, when twelve rangers and a driver were killed in a militia ambush. In 2018 a ranger was killed during an attack in which two tourists were kidnapped, and the park, which had been recovering as a destination, was closed to visitors from June 2018 until February 2019.

The park's director, Emmanuel de Merode, a Belgian conservationist who has led it since 2008, was himself the target of an attack in April 2014, one of the most widely reported events in the history of the park. His survival, and his return to the work, became a symbol of the determination of those who protect Virunga. The film Virunga, directed by Orlando von Einsiedel and released in 2014, brought the park, its rangers and its orphaned gorillas to a wide international audience, and its depiction of the pressure placed upon the park by oil interests and armed groups changed how many people thought about conservation in the Congo.

Oil, Charcoal and Poverty

The enemies of the park are not only armed men in the forest. In 2011 a British company, Soco International, was given an oil exploration concession that overlapped the park, and it was withdrawn from the project after a campaign of international protest. Yet the pressure has not gone away, and the Congolese government has more recently offered oil blocks that overlap the protected area. Meanwhile a vast illegal trade in charcoal, made from the trees of the park, supplies the cooking fires of Goma, a trade controlled in part by armed groups and one which UN experts have estimated to be worth many millions of dollars a year. The people who burn the charcoal are, for the most part, poor and have few alternatives.

It was in answer to this that the park developed the Virunga Alliance in 2013, a programme that seeks to link conservation to development, peace and the creation of legitimate work. The centrepiece has been hydropower. Virunga Energies operated four hydroelectric plants and a small solar farm by the end of 2024, with a combined capacity of 45 megawatts, serving nearly 35,000 customers, and the idea behind them is as simple as it is ambitious: that people who have electricity, jobs and a stake in the forest are less likely to cut it down, and less likely to join the armed groups that profit from its destruction.

Between War and Hope

The List in Danger

Virunga has been on the List of World Heritage in Danger since 1994. UNESCO's decisions have repeatedly called for the withdrawal of armed groups, the protection of the rangers, the halting of illegal exploitation, and the creation of buffer zones around the park, which the property still lacks; the World Heritage Centre describes the establishment of such zones in all the sectors as indispensable and a matter of urgency. It has also asked, again and again, that no oil exploration or exploitation take place within the borders of the park. In the language of the World Heritage Convention, the danger listing is a call for help, and it is an acknowledgement that the property cannot be rescued without the cooperation of the Congolese state, its neighbours and the international community.

The M23 Years

The conflict has entered a new phase in recent years. M23, a movement that first rose in 2012, returned to arms in 2021 and 2022, and its fighters, who are accused by the United Nations and by many governments of enjoying the backing of Rwanda, have taken control of large areas of North Kivu. After the attack on the Bukima guard post in November 2021, the gorilla sector fell under M23 operational control, and the park's rangers withdrew from a number of posts in 2024. In January 2025 an M23 offensive took the city of Goma, the park's gateway, and later that year Bukavu. Reporting in 2025 recorded poaching intensifying in the areas the park could no longer patrol, and park officials spoke of the loss of a large share of the park's wildlife and of some thirteen percent of its surface to the political crisis. The park's headquarters at Rumangabo were reported to be working under M23 surveillance.

The consequences for the visitor are plain. Tourism in Virunga has not been consistently open for most of the last several years, and at the time of writing the park has been described by several sources, including its own tourism arm, as closed to tourism because of armed conflict. Other sources describe treks as having been intermittently suspended and resumed, and the situation changes frequently. No reader should assume that a trip to the Congolese side of the Virunga massif is possible, and no reader should attempt one without current, official advice. The people who have the courage to stay and work there deserve our admiration, and our prudence, which is another way of saying that we should not add to their burdens.

What Remains to Hope For

And yet there are reasons for hope, and they are real. The gorilla population has risen. The park's institutions have survived a war, the loss of colleagues and the collapse of tourism income, which was once a major source of funding. The Virunga Alliance has electrified a region in which no one expected electricity. The Rwandan and Ugandan sides of the Virunga massif, protected in parks of their own, shelter the same gorillas and are open to visitors, and the survival of the species depends on the whole massif being protected as one. Beneath all of this lies a long truth about the Congo: that the people who live around the park are not an obstacle to its protection, but its only lasting guarantee.

Reflection

Virunga asks a hard question of those who admire it: what is a World Heritage Site worth when the people sworn to protect it are dying in its defence? The answer offered by the rangers, the scientists and the communities who have remained is not an abstraction. They have decided that the gorillas, the volcanoes, the rivers and the glaciers are worth the risk of a life, and the weight of that decision falls on the rest of us to understand, even if we can do little more than understand.

It would be easy to reduce the park to a story of tragedy, and equally easy to reduce it to a postcard of gorillas and lava. The truth is larger and stranger. Virunga is a place where the oldest forces of the planet, the slow stretching of a continent and the heat of the mantle, meet the newest and most human ones, the pressure of population, the hunger for resources and the courage of ordinary people. If it can be saved, it will be saved by the same combination of patience and stubbornness that brought the mountain gorilla back from the edge, and if it cannot, we shall have lost something that cannot be replaced.

✦ Conclusion

Virunga National Park is, by any measure, one of the great natural treasures of the world. Its volcanoes, among the most active on the continent, light the sky over the Rift; its forests shelter a share of the world's remaining mountain gorillas; its rivers and plains hold hippopotamuses and birds in numbers that few other places can claim; and its mountains carry ice on the equator. UNESCO recognised all of this in 1979, and the recognition has never been more necessary than it is today.

It is also a park in danger, in the most literal sense, and one whose future depends on peace in the Great Lakes region, on the continued courage of its rangers, and on the willingness of the world to take its predicament seriously. A reader who cannot go there, as at present few can, can still help by learning about it, supporting responsible conservation organisations, and remembering that behind every statistic about gorillas and lava lies a ranger who went to work in the forest that morning.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

1. When was Virunga National Park inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List?

It was inscribed in 1979 as reference 63, under natural criteria (vii), (viii) and (x). It has been on the List of World Heritage in Danger since 1994.

2. Why is Virunga on the List in Danger?

The park has suffered from armed conflict, poaching, illegal charcoal production, encroachment and the pressure of oil exploration. Rangers have been killed in large numbers, and UNESCO has repeatedly asked for the withdrawal of armed groups and the creation of buffer zones.

3. Can I visit Virunga National Park now?

Access has been severely restricted. Since the M23 advance in 2024 and 2025 and the fall of Goma, tourism has been suspended or described as closed by several sources, including the park's own tourism pages, although reports of reopening vary. Check the current advice of your government and of the park authority before any travel, and do not rely on tour operators alone.

4. Where can I see mountain gorillas instead?

Mountain gorillas can be visited, with permits and guides, in Volcanoes National Park in Rwanda, in Mgahinga Gorilla National Park in Uganda, and in Bwindi Impenetrable National Park in Uganda. The Virunga massif is a single ecosystem shared by three countries.

5. How high is Nyiragongo, and what makes it famous?

Nyiragongo rises to about 3,470 metres and holds a persistent lava lake in a caldera about 1.2 kilometres wide. Its unusually fluid lava has caused deadly eruptions, in 2002 and in May 2021, close to the city of Goma.
