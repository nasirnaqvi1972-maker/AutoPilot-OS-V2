The Centre of the World

According to Greek myth, Zeus once released two eagles from opposite ends of the earth, and they met at Delphi, on the slopes of Mount Parnassus. At the point where they crossed, a stone was set up to mark the navel of the world, the omphalos, and the sanctuary that grew around it became the most famous religious centre of the Greek world. For more than a thousand years, kings, generals, philosophers and ordinary citizens came here to consult the oracle of Apollo, whose priestess, the Pythia, spoke in his name.

The setting is among the most dramatic in Greece. Delphi lies on a steep, terraced slope above the valley of the Pleistos River, beneath two towering cliffs called the Phaedriades, the Shining Ones, which glow with the changing light of the day. Below, a sea of olive trees runs down to the Gulf of Corinth, and above, the high ridges of Parnassus rise toward the sky. It is a landscape that seems made for the gods, and the Greeks believed that it was.

UNESCO inscribed the Archaeological Site of Delphi on the World Heritage List in 1987 under no fewer than five criteria: (i), (ii), (iii), (iv) and (vi). It describes the pan-Hellenic sanctuary as blending harmoniously with its superb landscape and charged with sacred meaning, and as the religious centre and symbol of unity of the ancient Greek world in the sixth century BC.

Apollo and the Serpent

The sanctuary's origins are lost in myth and prehistory. According to the Greek tradition, the site was originally sacred to the earth goddess Gaia, whose oracle was guarded by a monstrous serpent, Python, until the young god Apollo came from Delos and killed it. The sanctuary thereby passed from the old chthonic powers to the bright Olympian, god of light, music, prophecy and harmony, and the priestess took the title Pythia in memory of the serpent.

Archaeology confirms that the valley was settled from the Bronze Age, and that a cult existed here in Mycenaean times, but the sanctuary of Apollo seems to have been founded in the eighth century BC, when Delphi began to attract visitors from beyond the local region. By the sixth century, it was recognised across the Greek world as the most authoritative oracle, and the city-states sent embassies, treasuries and offerings.

The sanctuary was controlled not by a single city but by an association of neighbouring states, the Amphictyony, which protected the shrine and organised its festivals. This arrangement helped to make Delphi a neutral ground, a place where Greeks of every city could meet, and it explains why UNESCO emphasises its role as a symbol of Hellenic unity.

The Pythia and Her Oracle

Consulting the oracle was a solemn affair. On the seventh day of the month, in the months when the god was believed to be present, those who wished to put a question first purified themselves, paid a fee and offered a sacrifice. A goat was sprinkled with water, and if it trembled, the god was thought to be willing to speak. The consultant then entered the temple, and the Pythia, a woman of mature years chosen from the local people, took her seat on a tripod in the inner sanctum.

What she said, and how, remains debated. Ancient authors say that she sat over a chasm from which a vapour rose and that she spoke in a state of inspiration, and that her words were interpreted by priests, who delivered the answer, sometimes in verse. Modern geologists have identified fault lines beneath the temple and traces of light hydrocarbon gases in the local springs, and some have suggested that these may have produced a trance, though the theory is contested. Whatever the mechanism, the effect on the Greeks was profound.

The oracle's advice influenced decisions of great consequence, from the founding of colonies to the conduct of wars. The most famous story is that of Croesus, the rich king of Lydia, who asked whether he should attack Persia and was told that if he did, he would destroy a great empire. He attacked, and destroyed his own. The tale, told by Herodotus, shows both the prestige of the oracle and the ambiguity of its answers.

The Sacred Way and the Treasuries

Visitors to the sanctuary climb the Sacred Way, a winding paved road that rises through a corridor of monuments to the Temple of Apollo. On either side stood statues, altars and small buildings called treasuries, built by the city-states to house their offerings and to proclaim their wealth and piety. Each was a miniature temple, and together they formed an open-air exhibition of the Greek world.

The most beautiful to survive is the Treasury of the Athenians, a small Doric building of marble, built around 490 BC, probably to celebrate the victory at Marathon, and reconstructed by French archaeologists in the early twentieth century from its original fragments. Nearby stood the earlier Treasury of the Siphnians, a gift from the gold-rich island of Siphnos around 525 BC, whose superb sculptured frieze, depicting battles of gods and giants and the Trojan War, is now in the museum.

Other monuments marked victories and alliances. The Stoa of the Athenians, a long portico, sheltered the trophies of naval victories, and a bronze column of three intertwined serpents, dedicated after the Greek victory at Plataea in 479 BC, once stood in front of the temple. Its remains were later taken to Constantinople, where they can still be seen in the Hippodrome in Istanbul. The sanctuary was thus a visible record of the glory and rivalry of the Greek states.

The Temple of Apollo

At the top of the Sacred Way, on an artificial terrace supported by an immense polygonal wall, stood the Temple of Apollo. The present remains, a stepped platform with a few reconstructed columns, belong to a fourth-century BC rebuilding after the earlier temple was destroyed by an earthquake in 373 BC. It was a Doric temple of limestone, with a colonnade of six columns by fifteen, and it housed the sanctuary of the oracle in an inner chamber below the floor of the main hall.

The walls and the forecourt of the temple were inscribed with maxims attributed to the Seven Sages of Greece, among them the famous commands Know thyself and Nothing in excess. They expressed a philosophy of moderation and self-awareness that came to be associated with Apollo and with Greek wisdom as a whole, and they have retained their power to the present day.

The polygonal wall below the temple, built of closely fitted blocks with irregular joints, is covered in inscriptions recording the freeing of slaves, who were dedicated to Apollo, a reminder of the social role of the sanctuary. The temple was a focus not only of religion but of law, diplomacy and commerce.

The Theatre, the Stadium and the Pythian Games

Above the temple, the slope rises to the theatre, built in the fourth century BC and later restored, with its curved rows of stone seats looking out over the sacred valley. It held about five thousand spectators, and it was used for dramatic and musical performances during the festivals. The view from its upper rows, over the temple to the olive groves and the distant sea, is one of the best in Greece.

At the top of the sanctuary, reached by a steep path, lies the stadium, a long track of about 177 metres, with stone seating along the slope for several thousand spectators. It was here that the athletic contests of the Pythian Games took place, held every four years in honour of Apollo, from 582 BC, and ranking second only to the Olympic Games in importance. Unlike Olympia, the Games at Delphi also included competitions in music and poetry, and the main prize was a laurel wreath.

The Castalian Spring, a sacred fountain at the foot of the cliffs, was used by pilgrims for ritual washing before entering the sanctuary, and a visitor can still see the remains of two monumental fountains built around it. The ritual cleansing, the processions, the sacrifices and the games all formed part of a complex religious experience.

The Sanctuary of Athena Pronaia and the Charioteer

A short walk below the main sanctuary, on the road from Athens, lies the sanctuary of Athena Pronaia, Athena before the Temple, the first holy place a pilgrim encountered on arriving at Delphi. Known today as the Marmaria, it contains several temples, the most celebrated of which is the Tholos, a circular building of the early fourth century BC, with a ring of twenty Doric columns around a circular cella. Three of its columns have been re-erected, and the elegant shape, framed by the cliffs, has become the emblem of Delphi.

The Delphi Archaeological Museum, beside the site, houses the sanctuary's greatest treasures. The bronze Charioteer, a statue of about 470 BC, was part of a monument dedicated to a victory in the chariot race at the Pythian Games, and its calm face, with inlaid eyes of glass paste, and its finely pleated robe make it one of the masterpieces of early classical sculpture. Other highlights include the two kouroi known as Kleobis and Biton, statues of Argive youths of the early sixth century BC, the sculpted frieze of the Siphnian Treasury and the omphalos stone.

Together, the sanctuary and the museum offer an unusually full picture of Greek religious life. They show not only the architecture and art of the sanctuary, but also the people who built, served and visited it, and the generosity with which the Greeks honoured their god.

Decline, Rediscovery and Visiting

The oracle's prestige began to wane in the Hellenistic and Roman periods, as people turned to other sources of guidance, but the sanctuary remained active. It was plundered by the emperor Nero, who is said to have carried off some five hundred statues, and restored by Hadrian, and the historian and philosopher Plutarch served as a priest there in the first century AD. In 393, the emperor Theodosius I banned pagan cults, and the oracle fell silent.

Over the centuries, the ruins were buried by landslides, and a village called Kastri was built on top of them. In 1892, the French School at Athens, under Théophile Homolle, began the Great Excavation, which required the relocation of the village and brought the sanctuary back to light. The excavations, which continue, have produced a mass of information about the architecture, religion and history of the site.

Delphi lies about 180 kilometres north-west of Athens, a drive of about two and a half to three hours, and the site and the museum are open daily, with seasonal hours that visitors should check. The best times are spring and autumn, when the weather is mild, and early morning, before the heat and the crowds. Summers are hot and the site is exposed. Wear sturdy shoes, bring water and allow at least half a day. The modern village of Delphi, with its hotels and restaurants, and the nearby mountain town of Arachova make good bases.

Reflection

To walk the Sacred Way at Delphi in the early morning, as the mist lifts from the valley and the cliffs turn from grey to gold, is to feel the force of an idea: that a landscape can be a message. The Greeks believed that the god spoke here, and whether or not one shares that belief, it is hard to stand in this place without a sense of listening. The questions that people brought to Delphi, about marriage, war, honour and fate, were the questions that every generation asks. The answers have gone, but the silence of the mountain, and the inscription that told visitors to know themselves, remain.

✦ Conclusion

The Archaeological Site of Delphi is one of the supreme places of the ancient world, where religion, politics, art and landscape came together in a single sanctuary. Its temple, treasuries, theatre and stadium preserve the monuments of a pan-Hellenic centre, and its oracle shaped the history of Greece for centuries.

It is also a place of lasting beauty, where the mountains, the cliffs and the olive groves create a setting that no architect could have designed. To visit is to enter, however briefly, the imagination of the ancient Greeks, and to hear echoes of a question that has never gone away: what does it mean to know oneself?

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

1. Where is Delphi?

It is in Phocis, central Greece, on the slope of Mount Parnassus, about 180 kilometres north-west of Athens.

2. Why was Delphi important?

It was the seat of the oracle of Apollo, whose priestess, the Pythia, advised Greek cities and individuals, and it was the religious centre and symbol of unity of the ancient Greek world.

3. When was it inscribed on the World Heritage List?

It was inscribed in 1987 under criteria (i), (ii), (iii), (iv) and (vi).

4. What can I see at the site?

The Sacred Way, the Treasury of the Athenians, the Temple of Apollo, the theatre, the stadium and the Tholos of Athena Pronaia. The museum holds the Charioteer and many other treasures.

5. When is the best time to visit?

Spring and autumn are best. Go early in the day to avoid heat and crowds, and check opening hours before travelling.
