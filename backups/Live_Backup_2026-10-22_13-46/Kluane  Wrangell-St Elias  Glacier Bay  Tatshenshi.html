Kluane / Wrangell-St. Elias / Glacier Bay / Tatshenshini-Alsek — The Realm of Ice Between Two Nations

Four Contiguous Parks Across the Yukon, British Columbia and Alaska, Where the Largest Non-Polar Icefield on Earth, the Tallest Peaks of the Saint Elias Mountains and Glaciers That Descend to the Sea Form One of the Greatest Protected Wildernesses on the Planet | UNESCO World Heritage Site

The Mountains Where the Continent Meets the Sea

There are places on Earth that cannot be taken in at a single glance, and there are places that cannot be taken in at all. The northwestern corner of North America belongs to the second kind. Here, where the Yukon Territory, the province of British Columbia and the state of Alaska draw together at the edge of the Pacific, the land rises in a wall of rock and ice so tall, so wide and so little visited that the human mind, accustomed to scale in the form of cities and fields, simply declines to hold it. One flies over it for an hour and sees no road, no roof, no fence, no smoke; only white, and the blue shadows between white, and the black teeth of mountains that no one has ever named.

This is the country that UNESCO, with a certain bureaucratic weariness at the length of its own title, calls Kluane / Wrangell-St. Elias / Glacier Bay / Tatshenshini-Alsek. The name is a list of four protected areas stitched across an international boundary, and the stitching is the point. Together they cover 9,839,121 hectares, an expanse larger than many European nations, and they shelter what the World Heritage Committee describes as the largest non-polar icefield in the world, more than two hundred glaciers in the central ice-covered plateau, and some of the longest glaciers on the planet, several of which flow all the way down to the sea. Nowhere else does so much ice, so much altitude and so much wildlife share a single unfenced landscape.

To arrive here is to understand why the idea of a World Heritage property was invented. The Convention of 1972 imagined places of such outstanding value that their protection should concern all of humanity, not merely the governments that happen to hold the title deeds. A frozen wilderness that ignores a border, that sends its meltwater into the Yukon River's watershed on one flank and the Gulf of Alaska on another, and that is traversed by grizzly bears who have never read a map, is the clearest argument for that principle. This post follows the ice from its mountain cradle to its tidewater grave, and listens, along the way, to the people who have lived at its margins for far longer than any park has existed.

A Wall Raised by Colliding Plates

The Making of the Saint Elias Range

The mountains that form the spine of this property are young, restless and still rising. The Pacific tectonic plate, carrying a blanket of oceanic crust, slides north-westward along the edge of North America and is gradually driven beneath it, and at this corner of the continent the collision is so violent that a coastal range has been shoved upward to heights found elsewhere only in the Himalaya and the Andes. Mount Logan, in the Yukon, stands 5,959 metres above sea level and is the highest mountain in Canada. Mount Saint Elias, which gives the range its name and straddles the international boundary, reaches 5,489 metres and is the second-highest peak in both countries. Between them and their neighbours the land climbs from tide-line to the stratosphere in the space of a few dozen kilometres, one of the steepest continental gradients on Earth.

Such a gradient is a machine for generating weather. Storm after storm rolls in off the Gulf of Alaska, strikes the mountain wall, is forced upward, and surrenders its moisture as snow in quantities that defy the imagination. The snow does not melt; it accumulates, compresses, recrystallises, and begins slowly to flow. The upper basins of the range are therefore a great engine of ice, and it is this engine, together with the active geology that built the mountains, that earned the property its recognition under criterion (viii), for outstanding examples of ongoing geological processes, and under criterion (vii), for superlative natural phenomena and scenic grandeur.

Further north and west the geology changes character. In Wrangell-St. Elias the Wrangell Mountains are of volcanic origin, and Mount Bona is the highest volcano in the United States, while Mount Blackburn is the loftiest point of the Wrangell range. Thus the property holds, within a single World Heritage perimeter, two very different mountain-building stories: the crumpling and thrusting of a coastal collision zone, and the old fire of a volcanic arc now muffled in snow.

The Rivers of Ice

Bagley, Malaspina and the Longest Glaciers

If the mountains are the skeleton of this country, the glaciers are its circulation. The Bagley Icefield, which fills much of the interior of Wrangell-St. Elias, is the largest subpolar icefield in North America, a high plateau of ice so deep that mountain summits poke through it like islands in a frozen sea. From it, glaciers spill in every direction, some feeding the rivers of the Alaskan interior, some creeping toward the Pacific. To the south-east, the Malaspina Glacier fans out onto the coastal plain in a vast lobe, the largest piedmont glacier in North America, a sheet of ice that looks from above like a spill of cream across a dark table. The Nabesna Glacier, in the north of the park, is accounted among the longest valley glaciers in the world.

On the Canadian side, the Kaskawulsh Glacier is the great river of the Kluane ice, a ribbon of white and grey flowing down from the high plateau and splitting, at its terminus, into the waters that eventually drain in two opposite directions: one stream toward the Bering Sea by way of the Yukon River, the other toward the Gulf of Alaska by way of the Alsek. A glacier, in this landscape, is not a static object but a negotiation between snowfall and warmth that has been going on for thousands of years, and the property contains one of the finest living laboratories in which to watch that negotiation.

It is a laboratory that is now registering unmistakable change. Scientists who monitor the glaciers of the Saint Elias Mountains report thinning and retreat across much of the range, and the International Union for Conservation of Nature tracks the property's prospects in its World Heritage Outlook. The ice that gives this property its identity is, paradoxically, among the more fragile of its treasures, and a visitor who walks to the edge of a retreating tongue cannot avoid the sense of standing at a slow-motion vanishing. Readers who have seen the glaciers and fjords of Te Wahipounamu in New Zealand will recognise the same elegiac mood, though here the scale is continental rather than alpine.

The First Peoples of the Ice Country

Tutchone, Tlingit and the Long Human Presence

It is tempting, standing before so much emptiness, to imagine that this landscape was never inhabited. Nothing could be further from the truth. Kluane, the name by which the Canadian park is known, is commonly rendered from the Southern Tutchone language as a reference to abundant fish, and the lakes and rivers around the mountains have sustained human communities for thousands of years. Today the park lies within the traditional territories of the Champagne and Aishihik First Nations and the Kluane First Nation, whose Final Agreements with the Canadian government protect their rights to harvest and to participate in the management of the land. The history of the protected area is bound up with theirs: Kluane was set aside as a national park reserve in 1972, pending the settlement of land claims, and the eastern portion became a full national park in 1993 only after agreement had been reached with the Champagne and Aishihik First Nations.

Further south, at the edge of the Pacific, the Huna Tlingit knew Glacier Bay as a homeland long before it was a park. Tlingit oral history remembers a time when the great glacier advanced and the bay's ancestral villages were lost. The National Park Service has worked with Tlingit organisations in Hoonah and Yakutat since 1994, and the park today recognises two ancestral homelands of cultural and spiritual significance within its boundaries. A World Heritage property inscribed for natural values is also, quietly, a cultural landscape, because the peoples of the Yukon and the Alaskan panhandle shaped their lives to the rhythm of salmon runs, glacial rivers and mountain passes that the property protects.

An astonishing reminder of that human depth came in 1999, when hunters in the Tatshenshini-Alsek region came upon the remains of a man preserved in a melting glacier. The Champagne and Aishihik First Nations, consulted at once, agreed to scientific study, and the remains, named Kwäday Dän Ts'ìnchi, "Long Ago Person Found" in the local language, became one of the most important discoveries in the archaeology of the northern Pacific. DNA testing identified living relatives within the First Nations, a rare instance of an ancient traveller being restored to an unbroken line of descent. The ice, it turned out, was not merely a barrier but an archive.

Four Parks, One Idea

The Long Road to a Single Inscription

The story of how four parks became one World Heritage property is a story of diplomacy by increments. In 1978 the United States proclaimed Wrangell-St. Elias a national monument, and in 1979 UNESCO inscribed Kluane National Park in Canada together with Wrangell-St. Elias in Alaska, creating from the outset a transboundary property. The recognition came a little ahead of the American park's final legislative form: on 2 December 1980 the Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act elevated the monument to a national park and preserve and, in the same stroke, enlarged Glacier Bay into the national park and preserve we know today. In 1992 the World Heritage Committee extended the property to include Glacier Bay, and in 1994 it added Tatshenshini-Alsek Provincial Park in British Columbia, whose creation in 1993 had closed the last gap in the chain of protected land between the Yukon and the Pacific.

The result is a continuous block of protected wilderness on a scale that few other regions can claim. The property includes 242,700 hectares of marine waters and 1,900 kilometres of coastline, and it is complemented by other designations, among them the Glacier Bay-Admiralty Island Biosphere Reserve and its associated Ramsar wetland site. Four management agencies on two sides of an international border, differing in law and in tradition, now steward a single ecosystem. That they do so, with the cooperation of Indigenous nations, in an area in which the border is invisible on the ground, is a quiet triumph of international cooperation. For readers who know the first American national park, Yellowstone, the Alaskan units extend the same ideal of wilderness preservation into a region incomparably larger and wilder.

Copper, Gold and the Argument for Wilderness

Kennecott and the Windy Craggy Controversy

No account of this country would be honest without its encounters with industry. In the early twentieth century, prospectors discovered copper ore of extraordinary richness in the Wrangell Mountains, and a company town sprang up at Kennecott to extract it. From 1903 to 1938 the mine and its tall red mill buildings dominated a narrow valley in what is now Wrangell-St. Elias, shipping ore by railway to the coast. When the veins ran out, the town was simply abandoned, and today the weathered timber and iron of Kennecott form a National Historic Landmark district, an eerie museum of boom and bust standing beside the glacier that overlooks it. The nearby settlement of McCarthy, reached by a gravel road of some sixty miles, still carries the memory of that era in its saloon-front architecture and its stubborn small population.

A later episode determined the shape of the World Heritage property itself. In the 1980s, enormous copper deposits were found near Windy Craggy Mountain in the far north-west of British Columbia, and a company proposed to turn the peak into one of the largest open-pit copper mines in the world, in a watershed that drains into the Tatshenshini and Alsek rivers and, ultimately, into the Pacific fishery of Alaska. A coalition of conservation groups, rafting outfitters and Indigenous and international voices rose against the proposal, and in June 1993 the government of British Columbia decided to protect the area, creating Tatshenshini-Alsek Provincial Park and compensating the claim holders in a settlement of 103.8 million dollars. The following year the new park was added to the World Heritage property. A mine that would have bitten into the heart of the ecosystem became, by the opposite logic, the final piece of its protection.

Glacier Bay and the Retreating Ice

John Muir, Vancouver and a Landscape in Motion

Nowhere is the drama of ice more legible than at Glacier Bay. In 1794, when members of George Vancouver's expedition explored these waters, Icy Strait was choked with ice, and the bay that now bears the name of glaciers lay beneath or beside the ice. By 1879, when the naturalist John Muir arrived by canoe, the ice had retreated some forty-eight miles up the bay, leaving a long fjord that was extraordinary in its newness. By 1916 the Grand Pacific Glacier had pulled back some sixty-five miles to the head of Tarr Inlet, a rate of retreat that has been called the fastest documented for any glacier. The bay is, in effect, a century-long film of a glacier dying and a landscape being born.

Behind the retreating ice, life comes in waves. Bare rock gives way to mosses and lichens, then to dryas and alder, then to the cottonwood and spruce that now fringe the lower reaches of the bay. Biologists have turned Glacier Bay into a classic study in ecological succession, which is one reason the property qualifies under criterion (ix), for significant ongoing ecological processes. The establishment of the national monument in 1925, under the Antiquities Act, and the transformation to a national park in 1980 preserved this living experiment for science and for those who simply wish to watch a continent being remade.

Grizzlies, Sheep and the Wild Corridor

A Refuge for the Great Mammals of the North

Where there is ice there is also, at its margins, an astonishing richness of life, and the property's recognition under criterion (x), for significant natural habitats for biodiversity, honours that paradox. The park system is home to grizzly bears, caribou, Dall sheep, wolves and a long list of smaller creatures. Kluane alone has recorded about one hundred and twenty bird species, among them the rock ptarmigan, the golden eagle and the bald eagle, and a census in 2009 counted 181 northern mountain caribou in the Kluane herd. Along the coast, the same wilderness supports humpback whales, orcas and minke whales, sea otters, harbour seals and sea lions, so that the property joins in a single piece of conservation the alpine, the boreal and the marine.

The Wild Rivers

Tatshenshini, Alsek and the Journey to the Sea

The Tatshenshini and Alsek rivers are the veins of the property's southern half, carrying meltwater from glaciers through U-shaped valleys to the Pacific at Dry Bay. In the mid-1970s two companies began rafting them for the first time, and the trip has since acquired a near-mythic status among wilderness travellers: ten days or more of braided channels, grizzly-trodden gravel bars, calving glaciers and campsites beneath mountains that rise almost vertically from the water, with no road and no town for hundreds of kilometres. Navigating the Alsek's icy lakes in a raft, dodging icebergs the size of houses, is an experience that makes human beings feel appropriately small.

Reflection

I have come to think that the great value of this property lies less in any one of its superlatives than in its refusal to be domesticated. Its mountains are the second highest on the continent, its icefield the largest of its kind, its glaciers among the longest on Earth, and yet it is not these statistics that linger. What lingers is the silence of a valley in which the only sound is the grinding of ice, and the sudden, humbling realisation that the land has gone on being itself without any reference to us. We spend our lives surrounded by things that were built for our purposes. Here is a place that was never built, and it is a rare privilege to be allowed to stand in front of it.

It is a privilege that is under threat from a force no border can hold back. The warming of the northern latitudes is thinning these glaciers faster than the generation that proclaimed these parks could have imagined, and the retreat of the ice will reshape rivers, forests and fisheries across three jurisdictions. The achievement of the people who protected this land, from the Indigenous custodians who lived lightly upon it, to the conservationists who halted Windy Craggy, to the diplomats who drew four parks into one, was to hold back the bulldozer. The challenge of the present century is a harder one: to hold back the thermometer. A World Heritage property cannot do that alone, but it can remind us what is at stake.

Te Wahipounamu, South West New Zealand pairs glaciers that descend into temperate rainforest with deep fiords and the cultural memory of Māori pounamu seekers, and its story of ice, rock and ecological isolation echoes this post in a gentler register. ✦ Conclusion

The Kluane / Wrangell-St. Elias / Glacier Bay / Tatshenshini-Alsek property is, by any measure, one of the supreme natural monuments of the world: nearly ten million hectares of ice, rock, forest, river and sea, inscribed by UNESCO under all four natural criteria and shared between two nations that have chosen to protect it together. It shelters the largest non-polar icefield on Earth, the highest mountain in Canada, the second-highest peak of both countries, and the longest and most dramatic glaciers of the continent, and it does so in a landscape so wild that most of it has never seen a visitor.

Yet its value is not only physical. It is a story of cooperation across a border, of Indigenous nations whose presence is measured in millennia, and of ordinary citizens who defended a valley against a mine. Its future depends on the same blend of science, diplomacy and respect. To visit it, whether by raft, bush plane, bus or cruise ship, is to accept an invitation to witness the planet at its most elemental, and to carry that experience home as an argument for keeping such places whole.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

1. Why is this World Heritage Site listed under four names?

The property began in 1979 as Kluane National Park in Canada together with Wrangell-St. Elias in the United States. It was extended in 1992 to include Glacier Bay National Park and Preserve, and in 1994 to include Tatshenshini-Alsek Provincial Park in British Columbia. UNESCO therefore lists all four protected areas together as a single transboundary property under reference 72ter.

2. How large is the property?

UNESCO gives the area as 9,839,121 hectares, including 242,700 hectares of marine waters and 1,900 kilometres of coastline. That is roughly 98,000 square kilometres, making it one of the largest protected areas in the world.

3. Which criteria justify its inscription?

All four natural criteria apply: (vii) for superlative natural beauty, (viii) for outstanding examples of earth-shaping geological and glacial processes, (ix) for ongoing ecological processes such as post-glacial succession, and (x) for significant habitats for biodiversity including grizzly bears, caribou and Dall sheep.

4. What is the highest mountain in the property?

Mount Logan in Kluane National Park and Reserve, Yukon, at 5,959 metres, is the highest mountain in Canada. Mount Saint Elias, on the Alaska–Yukon boundary, reaches 5,489 metres and is the second-highest peak in both Canada and the United States.

5. When is the best time to visit?

Most services and roads operate from late spring to early autumn, with June to early September the main season. Services are limited, many areas are reachable only by air, boat or gravel road, and conditions change quickly, so check the current advice of Parks Canada, the National Park Service and BC Parks before travelling.
