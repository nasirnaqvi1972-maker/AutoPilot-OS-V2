The City of Four Names

Sucre has had four names in its history, each reflecting a different chapter in the story of the city and the continent it inhabits. It was founded as La Plata in 1538 — the city of silver, named for the mineral wealth of the surrounding region — by the Spanish conquistador Pedro de Anzures. It was later known as Charcas, after the indigenous people of the region. Then as Chuquisaca. And finally, at independence in 1825, it was renamed Sucre in honour of Antonio José de Sucre — the Venezuelan general who, fighting under Simón Bolívar, liberated Bolivia from Spanish rule and became the first president of the new republic.

The city that was founded in 1538 and renamed in 1825 is, in its physical fabric, essentially the same city: the white-washed buildings, the red-tiled roofs, the grid plan of the colonial urban layout, the plazas and churches and convents and university buildings of the colonial capital are still present, still largely intact, still defining the character of a city that has been, since the late sixteenth century, one of the most important centres of political, judicial, and intellectual life in the Andean world. Sucre was the seat of the Audiencia of Charcas — the highest court in Spanish South America, whose jurisdiction extended across what is now Bolivia, Argentina, Chile, and Peru — and the site of the signing of Bolivia's Declaration of Independence in 1825. It was, for the Spanish colonial empire, one of the most important cities in the Americas.

UNESCO inscribed the Historic City of Sucre on the World Heritage List in 1991, under criterion (iv), recognising it as an outstanding example of a type of architectural and urban ensemble that illustrates a significant period in human history — specifically, the period of Spanish colonial rule in South America and the extraordinary built legacy that rule left across the continent. The Architecture — White, Beautiful, and Complete

The architecture of Sucre is characterised, above all, by whiteness. A municipal ordinance requiring all buildings in the historic centre to be whitewashed — an ordinance still enforced today — gives the city its most distinctive visual quality: the brilliant white of the lime-washed facades against the deep blue of the Andean sky and the warm red of the terracotta tiles, a colour combination of extraordinary simplicity and extraordinary effectiveness. Sucre is not merely white because its buildings happen to be painted that way. It is white as a statement of urban identity, a collective decision to present itself to the world in a particular visual register that distinguishes it from every other colonial city in South America.

The architectural tradition of Sucre is the Andean baroque — a style that developed in the Spanish colonial cities of the Andes between the sixteenth and eighteenth centuries, synthesising Spanish baroque forms with indigenous Andean decorative motifs in a way that produced a distinctive regional architecture unlike anything in Spain or in the other colonial regions. The carved stone portals of Sucre's churches and public buildings — the intricate facades of the Cathedral, of the Church of San Francisco, of the Church of San Miguel — display the mestizo baroque style at its most accomplished: European structural forms combined with indigenous plant and animal motifs, the whole executed in the warm golden stone of the surrounding mountains by craftsmen whose training combined Spanish and Andean traditions.

The University of San Francisco Xavier — founded in 1624, one of the oldest universities in the Americas — is one of the intellectual and architectural centrepieces of the historic centre. Its colonial buildings, arranged around a central courtyard, house faculties that have been in continuous operation for four centuries, making it one of the most venerable educational institutions in the Western Hemisphere.

The Plaza 25 de Mayo — The Heart of Bolivia's History

The Plaza 25 de Mayo — named for the date in 1809 when the first cry for independence was raised in the city, a decade and a half before independence was actually achieved — is the social, political, and architectural heart of Sucre. Surrounded by the Cathedral, the Casa de la Libertad (where Bolivia's Declaration of Independence was signed in 1825), the Prefecture of Chuquisaca, and the arcade of the historic commercial buildings, the plaza is one of the finest colonial squares in South America: well proportioned, architecturally coherent, its white buildings and its central garden with palm trees and the equestrian statue of Antonio José de Sucre creating a composition of great dignity and beauty.

The Casa de la Libertad — the House of Liberty — is the most historically significant building on the plaza and one of the most important in Bolivia. Originally the assembly hall of the University of San Francisco Xavier, it was here that on August 6, 1825, the representatives of the new republic assembled to sign the Declaration of Independence of Bolivia — the document that created the nation named in honour of Simón Bolívar. The hall is preserved exactly as it was at that moment: the original table, the original chairs, the original inkwells, the original Declaration itself — its physical reality a direct connection to the founding moment of the Bolivian state.

The Churches — Baroque Splendour at Altitude

Sucre contains more than a dozen colonial churches within its historic centre, ranging from the Cathedral on the main plaza to smaller neighbourhood churches of great individual character. Together they constitute one of the finest concentrations of colonial religious architecture in the Americas — a built record of the extraordinary investment of the Spanish colonial church in the spiritual conversion and the architectural statement of its presence in the Andean world.

The Church of San Francisco — one of the oldest churches in Bolivia, its construction begun in 1539 — is the most historically important of the colonial churches outside the Cathedral. Its tower, which can be climbed, offers the finest views over the historic centre, the red-tiled roofs of the colonial city extending to every horizon, the mountains of Chuquisaca visible beyond. The Church of La Merced — built in the seventeenth century, its interior preserved with an exceptional collection of colonial religious art — is the most intimate of the Sucre churches, its proportions and its decoration creating a contemplative atmosphere of great beauty.

The Convent of Santa Teresa — a colonial convent still inhabited by an active community of Carmelite nuns — houses one of the most important collections of colonial religious art in Bolivia, its sacristy and cloister decorated with paintings, sculptures, and decorative objects of the highest quality produced in the workshops of colonial Sucre and the surrounding region. The collection is accessible to visitors through a museum established within the convent buildings.

Sucre and Potosí — The Two Cities of Silver

Sucre cannot be fully understood without its relationship to Potosí — the mining city 160 kilometres to the southwest, at an altitude of more than 4,000 metres, whose silver mines produced approximately half of all the silver mined in the world between 1545 and 1800 and whose wealth funded the construction of colonial Sucre. Potosí was the engine; Sucre was the administrative, judicial, and cultural capital that the engine's revenues built and sustained. The Audiencia of Charcas in Sucre had jurisdiction over the silver mining operations of Potosí; the Church of Sucre received the tithes and donations of the silver miners; the University of San Francisco Xavier educated the lawyers and administrators who managed the colonial silver economy.

The relationship between the two cities was one of the defining dynamics of colonial South American history. Potosí at its seventeenth-century height had a population of approximately 200,000 — one of the largest cities in the world at that time, larger than London or Paris — and the wealth it generated was on a scale sufficient to transform not merely Sucre and the Audiencia of Charcas but the economy of Spain and, through Spain, of the entire early modern world. Reflection — The City That Preserved Itself

Sucre is one of those rare historic cities that has preserved its colonial fabric not merely in isolated monuments or selected streets but comprehensively — in its entire historic centre, in the continuity of its street network, in the scale and character of its buildings, in the whiteness of its facades, and in the quality of the urban spaces that connect its monuments. This comprehensive preservation is the result not of deliberate conservation policy alone but of a combination of factors — the relative economic marginality of the city after the independence period, when political power moved to La Paz and commercial activity centred on other Bolivian cities, and the deep cultural pride of the Sucrenses in their city's colonial heritage — that have conspired to keep the historic centre essentially intact.

The result is a city that functions simultaneously as a living urban centre — with its university students, its markets, its cafes and restaurants, its daily life of a contemporary Bolivian city — and as one of the finest surviving examples of Spanish colonial urban culture in the Americas. UNESCO's inscription of 1991 recognised what the people of Sucre have always known: that their white city, at its altitude on the Bolivian altiplano, is something extraordinary — a fragment of the colonial world preserved with a completeness that the forces of development have not, at least not yet, been able to destroy.

✦ Conclusion

The Historic City of Sucre is one of the finest colonial cities in the Americas — a city whose comprehensive preservation of its colonial urban fabric, whose architectural quality, whose historical significance as the birthplace of Bolivian independence, and whose extraordinary Andean setting at 2,810 metres above sea level together constitute a heritage of outstanding value that the UNESCO inscription of 1991 fully recognised.

To walk the streets of Sucre — to move from the Cathedral across the Plaza 25 de Mayo to the Casa de la Libertad, to climb the tower of San Francisco and look out over the red-tiled roofscape to the mountains beyond, to sit in one of the colonial courtyards in the late afternoon light and understand that the buildings around you have been standing, essentially unchanged, for three or four centuries — is to have a genuine encounter with history in the most direct and most physical possible way. Sucre offers that encounter with a generosity and a completeness that very few colonial cities anywhere in the Americas can match.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

Q1: Why is Sucre called the White City? Sucre is called the White City because of the municipal ordinance requiring all buildings in the historic centre to be whitewashed. The brilliant white of the lime-washed facades against the Andean sky and the red terracotta tiles of the roofs gives the city its most distinctive visual character and distinguishes it from every other colonial city in South America.

Q2: Is Sucre the capital of Bolivia? Sucre is the constitutional capital of Bolivia — the seat of the judicial branch of government and the city where Bolivia's Declaration of Independence was signed in 1825. La Paz is the seat of the executive and legislative branches of government, and serves as the administrative capital. Bolivia is unique in having this divided capital arrangement.

Q3: What is the Casa de la Libertad? The Casa de la Libertad — the House of Liberty — is the building in Sucre where Bolivia's Declaration of Independence was signed on August 6, 1825. Originally the assembly hall of the University of San Francisco Xavier, it is now a national museum preserving the original assembly hall exactly as it was at the moment of independence. It is the most important historical site in Bolivia.

Q4: Is the Historic City of Sucre a UNESCO World Heritage Site? Yes. The Historic City of Sucre was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1991, recognised under criterion (iv) as an outstanding example of a type of architectural and urban ensemble illustrating a significant period in human history — specifically, the Spanish colonial period in South America.

Q5: How do I get to Sucre? Sucre has its own airport — Alcantarí Airport — with flights from La Paz (approximately 45 minutes), Santa Cruz (approximately 1 hour), and Cochabamba. Sucre is also accessible by long-distance bus from La Paz (approximately 12–14 hours), Santa Cruz (approximately 12 hours), and Potosí (approximately 3 hours). The journey from Potosí to Sucre by road, passing through the Andean landscape between the two historic cities, is particularly rewarding.

🧳 Visitor's Guide

Nearest Airport — Alcantarí Airport (SUC) — approximately 35 km from the city centre; flights from La Paz, Santa Cruz, and Cochabamba

Altitude — 2,810 metres above sea level — allow 1–2 days to acclimatise, especially if arriving from low altitude; altitude sickness (soroche) can affect visitors

Getting Around — Historic centre is compact and best explored on foot; taxis for longer distances

Best Season — April to October (dry season) — clear skies and mild temperatures; the wet season (November to March) brings afternoon rain but the city is less crowded

Time Needed — 2 full days to explore the historic centre comprehensively; add day trips to the Cretaceous dinosaur footprints at Cal Orcko (5 km from centre) — the largest known concentration of dinosaur tracks in the world

Must See — Plaza 25 de Mayo and Cathedral; Casa de la Libertad; Church of San Francisco and tower views; Convent of Santa Teresa and its art collection; Central Market (Mercado Central) for local food

Day Trip — Potosí (160 km, 3 hours) — the UNESCO-inscribed silver mining city whose wealth built colonial Sucre; strongly recommended combination

Currency — Bolivian Boliviano (BOB)

Language — Spanish (official); Quechua and Aymara also spoken in the region
