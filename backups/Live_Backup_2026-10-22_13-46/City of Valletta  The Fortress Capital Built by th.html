A City Built to Withstand the World

Approach Valletta by sea and the first thing you see is a wall. Great ramparts of honey-coloured limestone rise straight from the water of the Grand Harbour, tier upon tier, topped by bastions, cavaliers and the domes and spires of churches. It is a skyline that seems designed to say one thing: this place is not to be taken. That is exactly what its builders intended. Valletta was conceived as a fortress, and it is one of the most completely planned fortified cities ever built.

Yet behind the walls lies something unexpected. The streets are straight and orderly, running in a grid across a narrow peninsula, lined with tall palaces, churches and wooden balconies painted in bright colours. At the end of almost every street the view opens onto the glittering sea. In just 55 hectares there are, according to UNESCO, 320 monuments, which makes the city one of the most concentrated historic areas in the world.

Valletta is inextricably linked to the history of the Order of St John of Jerusalem, the military and charitable order of knights that founded it in 1566 and ruled Malta for more than two centuries. It is a city of soldiers and nurses, of baroque splendour and austere defence, of a painter on the run and a besieged island that held out against an empire. This post traces its story from the Great Siege to the present day.

The Great Siege of 1565

The story begins in 1522, when the Ottoman Empire expelled the Knights of St John from the island of Rhodes. After years of wandering, the knights were granted the island of Malta in 1530 by Emperor Charles V, and they made their base around the Grand Harbour, on the southern shore of which lay the fortified town of Birgu. The knights were a force that Mediterranean Christians relied on and Muslim powers resented, and in 1565 the Ottoman sultan Suleiman sent a vast fleet and army to remove them.

The siege lasted nearly four months. The knights, led by the Grand Master Jean de Valette, and the Maltese defenders held out against enormous odds, first at Fort St Elmo, which fell after a terrible bombardment, and then at the fortified towns of Birgu and Senglea. In September a relief force arrived from Sicily and the Ottoman army withdrew. It was one of the great defensive victories of the sixteenth century, and it convinced the Order that it needed a new, stronger and fully fortified capital.

A New City on Mount Sciberras

The Order chose to build its new city on Mount Sciberras, a bare rocky peninsula between the Grand Harbour and the neighbouring Marsamxett Harbour, with Fort St Elmo at its tip. The foundation stone was laid in March 1566 by de Valette himself, and the city took his name. The pope, Pius V, sent his military engineer Francesco Laparelli to design it, and King Philip II of Spain provided substantial funds.

Laparelli planned a city on a rectangular grid, a striking departure from the winding streets of medieval Maltese towns. After his death in 1570 the Maltese architect Girolamo Cassar, who had been his assistant, oversaw the completion of the work, designing many of the principal buildings. UNESCO notes that the Knights conceived and planned the city as a single, holistic creation of the late Renaissance, within a uniform grid plan and fortified walls.

Bastions, Streets and Sea Views

The fortifications were the heart of the plan. Massive bastions faced the sea on two sides, and across the landward neck of the peninsula a deep ditch was cut into the rock, so that the city was an island of stone. The walls were built to resist the heaviest artillery of the time, and their angled faces and projecting bastions covered every approach with crossfire.

Within the walls, the streets run at right angles, many of them climbing and falling steeply with the terrain, so that some become staircases. Their orientation is said to have been chosen to channel cooling sea breezes through the city and to give long vistas toward the harbours. The result is a city that is as practical as it is beautiful, in which defence, comfort and display were all taken into account.

The Auberges: Palaces of the Tongues

The Knights of St John were divided into groups according to their language or region of origin, called tongues, and each tongue had its own residence in Valletta, known as an auberge. These buildings, designed in a dignified late Renaissance and Baroque style, give the city much of its character. UNESCO lists among Valletta’s monuments the Auberge de Castille, the Auberge de Provence, the Auberge d’Italie and the Auberge d’Aragon, among others.

The Auberge de Castille, with its elaborate Baroque façade, is now the office of the Prime Minister of Malta, while the Auberge de Provence houses the National Museum of Archaeology. Together they show how a military order, made up of noblemen from across Catholic Europe, organised its life in a city that was part barracks, part monastery and part court.

St John’s Co-Cathedral and Caravaggio

The finest religious building is St John’s Co-Cathedral, built in the 1570s to designs by Cassar as the conventual church of the Order. Its exterior is austere, almost fortress-like, with two square towers and a plain façade, but the interior is one of the most sumptuous in Europe. In the seventeenth century the painter Mattia Preti covered the barrel-vaulted ceiling with scenes from the life of St John the Baptist, and the walls are carved with golden ornament. The floor is a mosaic of inlaid marble tombstones commemorating knights of the Order.

The cathedral also houses a masterpiece by Caravaggio. The great Italian painter fled to Malta in 1607 after a killing in Rome, was admitted into the Order and painted for the cathedral the monumental Beheading of Saint John the Baptist in 1608, his largest painting and the only one he signed. Soon afterwards he quarrelled with the knights, was imprisoned and escaped, but the painting remains in the oratory, one of the most powerful works of the Baroque.

The Palace and the Sacred Infirmary

The political centre of the Order was the Grand Master’s Palace, a long, imposing building around two courtyards that housed the ruler of the island and later the British governors. Its state rooms, hung with tapestries and frescoes and its armoury, still contain a vast collection of weapons and armour, and the palace remains the office of the President of Malta.

The Order was not only military but charitable, and its hospital, the Sacred Infirmary, was among the most advanced in Europe. It cared for sick people of all faiths and nations, served by knights who regarded their patients as their lords, and its very long ward was one of the marvels of its age. The hospitaller mission is central to UNESCO’s recognition of Valletta, which is linked to the history of the military and charitable Order of St John.

War and the George Cross

The twentieth century brought a new siege. During the Second World War, Malta was a vital British base in the Mediterranean and was subjected to relentless bombing by German and Italian aircraft, in some of the heaviest air raids of the war. Valletta suffered terribly, with its Royal Opera House, many churches and a large number of houses destroyed or damaged, and its people sheltering in tunnels cut into the rock.

In April 1942 King George VI awarded the George Cross to the people of Malta for their extraordinary heroism, and the cross now appears on the island’s flag. The city was rebuilt after the war. Among the most debated of recent additions are the new City Gate and Parliament building by the architect Renzo Piano, completed in 2015, and the open-air theatre created in the ruins of the bombed Opera House, which show how a historic city can absorb contemporary design.

What UNESCO Recognised: Criteria (i) and (vi)

Valletta was inscribed on the World Heritage List in 1980 under two criteria. Criterion (i) recognised the city as a masterpiece of human creative genius, a late Renaissance city planned and built as a single entity, with the fortifications, grid plan, auberges, churches and palaces forming a coherent whole. Criterion (vi) recognised its association with the history of the military and charitable Order of St John of Jerusalem.

The property covers 55.5 hectares and contains 320 monuments, all within the limits of the original fortifications, and UNESCO emphasises that the city has been kept largely free of later development within that area. The property data records no separate buffer zone, and protection relies on national planning and heritage law and on the Valletta Rehabilitation Projects Office, which manages the site.

A Living Capital

Valletta is a capital as well as a monument. It is the seat of Malta’s government and parliament, its President and its law courts, and fewer than eight thousand people live within its walls. In 2018 it served as a European Capital of Culture, an occasion that brought restoration of squares, palaces and churches and a new emphasis on cultural life.

The challenges of a living World Heritage city are familiar. Tourism, including large numbers of cruise passengers arriving at the Grand Harbour, brings income but also crowding. The cost of property pushes out residents, and traffic, new development and the pressure to adapt old buildings to modern uses all require careful management. The aim is to ensure that Valletta remains a place where people live and work rather than a stage set for visitors.

Visiting Valletta

Valletta is compact and best explored on foot, though the steep, stepped streets and strong sun can be tiring. Key sights include St John’s Co-Cathedral, the Grand Master’s Palace, the Upper Barrakka Gardens, from which there is a superb view of the Grand Harbour and where a saluting battery fires a daily salute, the Manoel Theatre, one of the oldest working theatres in Europe, and the underground Lascaris War Rooms, which tell the story of the Second World War.

A short ferry across the Grand Harbour leads to the Three Cities of Birgu, Senglea and Cospicua, with their own fortifications and a stronger sense of everyday life. The best times to visit are spring and autumn, when the weather is mild, while summer is hot and crowded. Visitors to churches should dress modestly, and opening hours and ticket arrangements should be checked in advance.

Reflection — A City Planned as a Single Idea

Few cities in Europe were conceived and built as a single act. Most grew by accretion, street by street and century by century. Valletta was drawn on a drawing board, built on bare rock and finished within a generation, and the result is a place of unusual coherence. Every street, bastion and façade belongs to the same idea: a city that is at once a fortress, a monastery and a theatre of power.

That coherence is Valletta’s gift to the visitor, but it is also a responsibility. A planned city is vulnerable to piecemeal change in a way that an organic one is not, because each intervention disturbs the whole. In this it resembles the fortified harbour town of Old Havana and its Fortification System, Cuba and the island abbey of Mont-Saint-Michel and its Bay, France, where defence and devotion shaped every stone.

✦ Conclusion

Valletta is a rare thing: a city that has remained recognisably the one its founders planned. Its bastions, its grid of streets, its palaces and its churches record a decisive moment in Mediterranean history, when a small island withstood a great empire and then built a capital to ensure it would never be taken. Its inscription in 1980 recognised both its architecture and its association with the Order of St John.

To walk its streets is to move through four and a half centuries of history in a single afternoon, from the silence of St John’s to the view from the Barrakka and the sound of the noon gun. Come with time, wear comfortable shoes and let the city show you how a fortress can also be a home.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

Why is Valletta a UNESCO World Heritage Site?

It was inscribed in 1980 under criteria (i) and (vi). Valletta is a late Renaissance city planned and built as a single entity by the Knights of St John after the Great Siege of 1565, with 320 monuments in 55 hectares, and it is inextricably linked to the history of the military and charitable Order of St John.

Who built Valletta?

The Knights of St John built it, starting in 1566 under Grand Master Jean de Valette. The Italian military engineer Francesco Laparelli designed the plan, and the Maltese architect Girolamo Cassar completed the work and designed many of its main buildings.

What is the Great Siege of Malta?

It was the Ottoman siege of Malta in 1565, which lasted nearly four months. The Knights and the Maltese withstood the attack, and the victory led to the decision to build Valletta as a new fortified capital.

What is Caravaggio’s painting in Valletta?

The Beheading of Saint John the Baptist, painted in 1608, hangs in the oratory of St John’s Co-Cathedral. It is Caravaggio’s largest painting and the only one he signed.

When is the best time to visit?

Spring and autumn offer mild weather and fewer crowds. Summer is hot and busy, especially when cruise ships are in port. Dress modestly for churches and check opening hours in advance.
