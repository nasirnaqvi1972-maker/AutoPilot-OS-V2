A Town of Stone on an Island of Spice

Zanzibar has the sound of a legend: a name that suggests spices, sultans, dhows and distant shores. The reality is no disappointment. On the west coast of Unguja, the main island of the Zanzibar archipelago, some forty kilometres off the coast of Tanzania, lies Stone Town, the historic heart of Zanzibar City. Known locally as Mji Mkongwe, the Old Town, it is a dense knot of narrow lanes, coral-stone houses and carved wooden doors, set beside a harbour where wooden dhows still ride at anchor.

The town is exceptional because it preserves, almost intact, the urban fabric and the way of life of a Swahili trading port of the nineteenth century. For many centuries, the monsoon winds carried ships between the Indian Ocean world and the East African coast, and Zanzibar became one of the great meeting places of that world. Africans, Arabs, Persians, Indians and Europeans left their mark on its architecture, its language and its customs, creating a culture that is both distinctly Swahili and truly cosmopolitan.

UNESCO inscribed the Stone Town of Zanzibar in 2000, under criteria (ii), (iii) and (vi), recognising its role in the exchange of cultural influences across the Indian Ocean, its testimony to Swahili civilisation and its association with the slave trade and its abolition. It is a place of beauty and of memory, where the pleasures of the present sit beside the pain of the past.

The Swahili Coast and Its Early Rulers

The story of Zanzibar begins long before the sultans. The islands were inhabited by Bantu-speaking peoples who engaged in fishing, farming and trade, and by the first millennium CE they were part of the Swahili coast, a chain of port towns stretching from Somalia to Mozambique. Swahili culture grew out of the blending of African traditions with Islam and with the commerce of the Indian Ocean, and its language, Kiswahili, is today one of the most widely spoken in Africa.

Merchants from Arabia, Persia and India came on the monsoon winds, bringing cloth, beads, ceramics and spices, and carrying away ivory, gold, timber and, tragically, enslaved people. Local rulers, among them the Shirazi dynasties of tradition, controlled the trade, and towns such as Kilwa, Mombasa and Zanzibar prospered. The ruins of Kilwa Kisiwani, further down the coast, give a sense of the wealth and sophistication of these medieval city-states.

In 1503 the Portuguese arrived, seeking control of the Indian Ocean trade, and for almost two centuries they dominated the coast from forts and trading posts. Their rule was resented, and in 1698 Omani Arabs, with local support, captured Zanzibar and established their authority. They built a fortress on the site of the Portuguese church, the Old Fort, which still stands beside the harbour as the oldest surviving monument of Stone Town.

Sultan Said and the Clove Empire

Zanzibar’s golden age began in the 1830s under the Omani ruler Sultan Said bin Sultan, who was so taken with the island that in 1840 he moved his capital from Muscat to Zanzibar. He introduced cloves, a crop from the Moluccas, and encouraged Arab landowners to establish plantations worked by enslaved labour. Within a few decades Zanzibar had become the world’s leading producer of cloves, and the island’s economy was transformed.

The clove boom created great wealth, and Stone Town grew accordingly. Merchants from Oman, India and Europe built houses, warehouses and mosques, and the harbour filled with ships. Indian traders, particularly from Gujarat and Kutch, played a vital role as financiers and merchants, and the Indian community left a rich architectural legacy of houses and temples. Zanzibar also became a base for expeditions into the interior of Africa, organised by traders who sought ivory and slaves.

This prosperity rested on a brutal foundation. Zanzibar’s economy was inseparable from slavery and the slave trade, and the splendour of Stone Town cannot be understood without recognising the suffering on which it was built. The town’s heritage therefore includes both its monuments of power and the places that bear witness to human exploitation.

Coral Stone, Carved Doors and the Swahili House

The architecture of Stone Town is its greatest glory. Most buildings are made of coral rag, a local limestone formed from fossilised coral, bonded with lime mortar and covered with plaster. The result is a warm, pale surface that ages gracefully, though it also demands constant maintenance in the humid tropical climate. The houses stand tall, often three or four storeys high, with flat roofs, deep verandahs and inner courtyards that provide shade and ventilation.

The Doors of Zanzibar

The most celebrated feature is the carved wooden door. Hundreds of them survive, many of them dating from the nineteenth century, and they came in several styles. Arab doors have rectangular frames and flat lintels, often carved with verses of the Qur’an, while Indian doors, associated with Gujarati merchants, have arched tops and brass spikes, originally intended to deter elephants in India but retained as a sign of status. A door was often the first thing made when a house was built, and its size and ornament proclaimed the wealth and origin of its owner. Beside many doors stand the baraza, stone benches where residents sit to chat and watch the street, an enduring element of Swahili social life.

Landmarks of the Sultans — Fort, Palace and Baths

Among the major monuments of Stone Town is the House of Wonders, or Beit al-Ajaib, built in the 1880s for Sultan Barghash as a ceremonial palace. Its name reputedly came from its modern marvels: it was the first building in East Africa with electricity and the first with an elevator. A great white block with verandas supported by slender cast-iron columns, it dominates the waterfront and has been the symbol of Zanzibar for generations, though it has been closed for restoration after a partial collapse in 2020.

Nearby stands the Old Fort, or Ngome Kongwe, built by the Omanis around 1700 as a defence against the Portuguese and other rivals, and later used as a prison and a venue for cultural events. A short distance away lies the Sultan’s Palace, or Beit al-Sahel, now a museum of the Omani dynasty, and the Hamamni Persian Baths, built in the 1880s as public baths with elaborate vaulted chambers, an example of Zanzibar’s borrowing of Middle Eastern forms.

The Forodhani Gardens, on the sea front, become each evening a lively open-air market where vendors grill fish, prawns and skewered meats, and families and visitors gather to watch the sunset. This mixture of old monuments and daily life is typical of Stone Town, where heritage is not isolated in museums but woven into the rhythms of the town.

The Shadow of the Slave Trade

In the nineteenth century, Zanzibar was the centre of the East African slave trade, one of the largest in the world. Tens of thousands of men, women and children were brought from the African mainland, many of them captured in the interior by raiders and traders and marched hundreds of kilometres to the coast. At Zanzibar they were sold in open markets, held in cramped, windowless chambers and shipped across the Indian Ocean or put to work on the island’s clove and coconut plantations.

The campaign against the trade was led by British abolitionists, including the explorer David Livingstone, whose descriptions of its horrors stirred public opinion. In 1873, under heavy British pressure, Sultan Barghash agreed to close the slave market, and the building of the Anglican Cathedral began soon afterwards on its site, completed in 1880. Its altar is said to stand on the spot where the whipping post once stood, and beside it a memorial of chained figures honours the victims.

Visitors today can see the remains of the underground slave chambers, small, dark and airless rooms where captives were kept before sale, and visit the cathedral and its museum. These places are painful but important, and they ensure that Zanzibar’s history is remembered in its full complexity, not only as a story of spices and sultans but also of suffering and eventual resistance.

Protectorate, Revolution and Union

In 1890 Zanzibar became a British protectorate, though it continued to be ruled by sultans. The most famous episode of this period was the Anglo-Zanzibar War of 27 August 1896, which began after the death of Sultan Hamad bin Thuwaini and the seizure of power by a rival, Khalid bin Barghash. A British naval squadron bombarded the Sultan’s palace, and the war ended in roughly forty minutes, a record usually described as the shortest in history.

Zanzibar remained under British protection until it achieved independence in December 1963, as a constitutional monarchy under the Sultan. A month later, in January 1964, a revolution overthrew the Sultan and his government, and an African-led revolutionary council took power. The upheaval was accompanied by violence against Arab and Indian residents, and caused many to flee, an episode whose legacy remains sensitive on the island.

In April 1964, Zanzibar joined with the mainland state of Tanganyika to form the United Republic of Tanzania, though it retains its own government and considerable autonomy. The political and social changes of this period transformed Stone Town, as many of its great houses were taken over, subdivided and, in some cases, left to decay, leaving the town with a legacy of dilapidation as well as beauty.

A Living Town — Culture, Music and Cuisine

What distinguishes Stone Town from many heritage sites is that it is genuinely lived in. Some sixteen thousand people live within its boundaries, and the old streets are filled with the sounds of the call to prayer, the chatter of the market and the songs of children. The Darajani Market, with its stalls of fish, vegetables, spices and fabrics, is a lively centre of everyday life, while small workshops, tailors and craftsmen continue traditional trades.

Music is central to Zanzibari culture. Taarab, a genre that blends Arab, Indian and African influences, grew up in the nineteenth century at the sultan’s court and became popular throughout East Africa, and its sweeping melodies can still be heard at weddings and festivals. The Sauti za Busara music festival and the Zanzibar International Film Festival attract artists and audiences from across the continent and beyond. Visitors may also learn that the singer Freddie Mercury of the band Queen was born in Stone Town in 1946, and a small museum commemorates him.

Zanzibari cuisine reflects the island’s history of trade, with dishes that combine coconut, rice and fish with cardamom, cloves, cinnamon and chilli. Spiced pilau, grilled seafood, samosas and sweet pastries are all part of the culinary repertoire, and a guided spice tour to the plantations outside town is one of the most popular excursions. For the traveller, food is one of the most delicious ways to understand the island’s past.

Reflection — The Beauty and the Burden of Memory

Stone Town is a place that asks to be understood on more than one level. At first glance, it enchants: the whitewashed walls, the carved doors, the light on the harbour, the scent of cloves on the evening air. But the longer one stays, the more one feels the weight of what lies beneath. The town was built on trade that connected continents and on a trade in human lives, and its grandeur cannot be separated from that history.

This is perhaps the greatest value of the place. It does not offer a comfortable story but a complex one, in which beauty and suffering, creativity and cruelty, exchange and domination are all intertwined. To walk its alleys with attention is to confront the full richness of the human past, and to recognise that preserving heritage means preserving not only what we admire but also what we need to remember.

✦ Conclusion

The Stone Town of Zanzibar is one of the most remarkable historic towns in Africa, a place where the Swahili, Arab, Indian and European worlds met and merged. Its coral-stone houses, carved doors, palaces and mosques record the prosperity of the Indian Ocean trade, while its slave market and cathedral recall the human cost of that prosperity.

Today it is a living town, rich in music, food and daily life, but also fragile, with many buildings in need of repair and pressures from tourism and development. Those who visit it with curiosity and respect will find a place of enchanting beauty and enduring significance.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

Why is Stone Town a UNESCO World Heritage Site?

UNESCO inscribed it in 2000 under criteria (ii), (iii) and (vi). It is an outstanding example of the cultural exchange of the Indian Ocean, preserves the architecture and urban fabric of a Swahili trading city, and is associated with the slave trade and its abolition.

What is special about the doors of Stone Town?

The carved wooden doors are a symbol of the town. Arab-style doors have rectangular frames and Qur’anic inscriptions, while Indian-style doors have arches and brass studs. A door was a mark of status and wealth, and hundreds of historic examples survive.

Can I visit the former slave market?

Yes. The Anglican Cathedral stands on the site of the old slave market, with a memorial and a small museum nearby. Visitors can also see the remains of underground slave chambers. Guided visits are recommended to understand the history.

What is the best time to visit Zanzibar?

The dry seasons, from about June to October and from December to February, are the most pleasant. The long rains from March to May bring heavy downpours and some businesses close. Check dates of Ramadan, which affects opening hours and dress expectations.

How do I get to Stone Town?

Visitors fly to Abeid Amani Karume International Airport, about ten minutes from Stone Town, or take a ferry from Dar es Salaam, which takes roughly two hours. Many travellers combine Zanzibar with a safari on the Tanzanian mainland.

🧳 Visitor's Guide

Getting There — Fly to Zanzibar airport or take a ferry from Dar es Salaam (about two hours); Stone Town is a short drive from the airport

Best Time to Visit — June to October and December to February for the best weather

Getting Around — Stone Town is compact and best explored on foot; taxis and small tuk-tuks are available

Dress and Etiquette — Zanzibar is predominantly Muslim; dress modestly in town, avoid public displays of affection and ask before photographing people

Must See — House of Wonders (exterior while closed), Old Fort, Forodhani Gardens, slave market memorial, Hamamni Baths and the carved doors

Spice Tours — Half-day tours to plantations outside town are popular; choose reputable guides

Safety — Take usual precautions against petty theft and use licensed guides and transport

Responsible Tourism — Buy from local craftspeople, respect residents’ privacy and avoid supporting exploitation
