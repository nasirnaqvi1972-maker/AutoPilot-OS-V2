A Palace Unlike Any Other

Of all the great royal residences of France, Fontainebleau is the one that history has lived in most continuously. Set in the middle of an enormous forest, about an hour south-east of Paris, the palace is not the result of a single plan but of eight centuries of additions, alterations and redecorations by successive kings and emperors. Its courtyards, wings and gardens form an architectural patchwork that reflects the taste of every period from the Middle Ages to the Second Empire.

Napoleon, who knew the palace well, called it the true home of kings, the house of the centuries, and the phrase captures its essence. Almost every French sovereign from the twelfth century to Napoleon III stayed, hunted, governed, celebrated or mourned here, and the rooms still carry the marks of their presence. With more than a thousand rooms and an area of several hectares, it is one of the largest châteaux in Europe.

UNESCO inscribed the Palace and Park of Fontainebleau in 1981, under criteria (ii) and (vi), recognising its role as the place where the Italian Renaissance was transmitted to France and its association with major events of French history. It is a monument not only to royal taste but to the exchange of artistic ideas across Europe.

From Hunting Lodge to Renaissance Court

The story begins in the forest. The area around Fontainebleau, with its game-filled woodland and a natural spring, drew the Capetian kings from the twelfth century, and a royal hunting lodge was established here, with a chapel consecrated in 1169 by Thomas Becket, the Archbishop of Canterbury, then in exile in France. Successive kings added to the buildings, and the place became a favourite retreat for the French monarchy.

The decisive transformation came under Francis I, who came to the throne in 1515 and who, from 1528, undertook a vast rebuilding programme. An admirer of Italian culture, he wanted a palace that would rival those of the Italian princes and showcase the glory of the French crown. He demolished much of the old château and built a new residence around a series of courtyards, retaining the medieval keep but wrapping it in a new architectural language.

Francis was a major collector. He acquired paintings by Leonardo da Vinci, including the Mona Lisa, which hung at Fontainebleau for a time, as well as works by Raphael, Titian and other masters. His court became a magnet for artists, scholars and craftsmen, and the palace was the setting for a remarkable moment of cultural exchange that transformed French art.

The First School of Fontainebleau and the Gallery of Francis I

To realise his vision, Francis summoned Italian artists, among them Rosso Fiorentino, who arrived in 1530, and Francesco Primaticcio, who followed in 1532. Together with local craftsmen they created a style of decoration that combined painted frescoes, sculpted stucco and carved woodwork in elaborate compositions. This artistic movement, known as the First School of Fontainebleau, gave France its distinctive version of Mannerism, with its elongated figures, learned allegories and playful ornament.

A Gallery of Allegory

The finest surviving work of the period is the Gallery of Francis I, a long corridor built between about 1533 and 1539 to connect the king’s apartments with the chapel. Its walls are covered with panelling of carved walnut, richly framed frescoes and stucco figures, depicting mythological and allegorical scenes that glorify the king as a wise and powerful ruler. The gallery was the first great example of this sort of interior in France, and it inspired later palace galleries across Europe, including the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles. The Italian-trained artists also created the Ballroom, completed under Henri II, with its coffered ceiling and frescoed walls.

Henri II, Henri IV and the Making of a Royal Landscape

Henri II, who succeeded Francis in 1547, continued the work, completing the Ballroom and adding the insignia of himself and his mistress Diane de Poitiers, whose crescent moons and initials appear throughout the decoration. After the religious wars that ravaged France, Henri IV returned to Fontainebleau and expanded it again, adding the Oval Court, the great Parterre and a tennis court, as well as the Chapel of the Trinity with its richly painted vault.

The Bourbon kings that followed made their own mark. Louis XIII, who was born at Fontainebleau in 1601, commissioned the horseshoe staircase in the Cour du Cheval Blanc, which was completed in the 1630s. Louis XIV, who loved the forest and the hunt, spent the autumn seasons here and commissioned the great landscape architect André Le Nôtre to redesign the gardens, giving them the formal geometry that still defines them today.

Fontainebleau was also a stage for the great events of the monarchy: royal weddings, diplomatic receptions and, in 1685, the signing of the Edict of Fontainebleau by Louis XIV, which revoked the Edict of Nantes and ended religious toleration for Protestants in France. It was a reminder that the palace was not only a place of pleasure but also a centre of power and, at times, of controversy.

Napoleon and the Farewell at the White Horse Court

During the French Revolution, Fontainebleau was stripped of its furnishings and its royal associations, and it fell into disrepair. Napoleon, who rose to power at the turn of the century, saw the palace’s value and restored it, refurnishing it as an imperial residence and installing his own Throne Room in the former royal bedchamber. He also used it as a place of confinement for Pope Pius VII, who was held here from 1812 to 1814 in a dispute with the emperor.

The palace’s most famous episode took place in the spring of 1814. After defeat by the Allied armies and the occupation of Paris, Napoleon signed his first abdication at Fontainebleau on 6 April, and on 20 April he said farewell to the soldiers of his Old Guard in the Cour du Cheval Blanc, descending the horseshoe staircase to embrace the standard-bearer before leaving for exile on the island of Elba. The courtyard has been known ever since as the Cour des Adieux, the Court of Farewells.

Napoleon’s association with the palace is palpable in the Imperial Apartments, where the Empire style, with its heavy mahogany furniture, eagles and military motifs, still dominates. Visitors can see his study, his bedroom, his bath and the small desk on which he is said to have signed the abdication. The rooms feel remarkably intimate and offer an unusual sense of encountering history at human scale.

Napoleon III, the Chinese Museum and the Theatre

Under Napoleon III and Empress Eugénie, Fontainebleau enjoyed a final flourish of imperial glamour. The court spent weeks here each summer, hosting lavish receptions, hunts and entertainments. A theatre was built in a former wing and opened in 1857, a jewel of Second Empire design with its gilded boxes, red velvet seats and painted ceiling, and it remains one of the most beautiful historic theatres in France.

The Empress also created the Chinese Museum, a series of rooms displaying objects, many of them taken from the Summer Palace in Beijing during the Anglo-French expedition of 1860. The collection, which includes porcelain, jade, enamels and Buddhist figures, reflects both the nineteenth-century fascination with the East and the difficult history of the acquisition of some of its contents, a question that continues to prompt discussion among historians and curators.

After the fall of the Second Empire in 1870, Fontainebleau became a museum and a national monument, and in the twentieth century it served several other purposes. From 1949 until the 1960s it housed military headquarters of the North Atlantic alliance, and today it is open to visitors and used for exhibitions, concerts and cultural events, with extensive conservation work continuing to preserve its decoration.

Gardens, Forest and the Painters of Barbizon

The park of Fontainebleau comprises several distinct gardens. The Grand Parterre, laid out by Le Nôtre in the 1660s, is one of the largest formal gardens in France, stretching along an axis flanked by clipped hedges and fountains. The English Garden, created in the early nineteenth century, offers a more romantic landscape of winding paths and ponds, while the Diana Garden and the Grand Canal, dug under Henri IV, add further variety. The Carp Pond, with its small island pavilion, is a favourite spot for walkers.

Beyond the palace grounds lies the great Forest of Fontainebleau, a woodland of about 25,000 hectares famous for its sandstone boulders, its ancient trees and its wildlife. In the nineteenth century the forest drew artists seeking to paint nature directly, and the nearby village of Barbizon gave its name to a school of landscape painters, including Jean-François Millet and Théodore Rousseau, who prepared the ground for Impressionism. Today the forest is a favourite destination for walkers, cyclists and rock climbers, who value its distinctive boulders.

The relationship between palace and forest is fundamental to the place. The palace was created for the hunt, and its gardens were conceived as an extension of the forest, so that to visit Fontainebleau is to experience both architecture and landscape as parts of a single royal domain.

Reflection — A Palace That Remembers Everything

What makes Fontainebleau remarkable is not any single room or reign but the sense of accumulation. Each ruler left a layer: a Gothic chapel, a Renaissance gallery, a Baroque staircase, an Empire throne, a Second Empire theatre. Unlike Versailles, which crystallises a single moment of absolute monarchy, Fontainebleau is a palimpsest, a place where history has been written and rewritten without ever being erased.

That layering gives the palace a human quality that more monumental residences often lack. One finds the traces of individual tastes, of rivalries and loves, of renovations and neglect. To walk its corridors is to feel that the past is not a single story but many overlapping ones, and that the drama of French history, from the Renaissance to the Empire, can be read in its walls.

✦ Conclusion

The Palace and Park of Fontainebleau is one of the great royal residences of Europe, a place where eight centuries of French history are layered in stone, paint and garden. It was here that the Italian Renaissance took root in France, that Louis XIV signed one of the most controversial edicts of his reign and that Napoleon said goodbye to his army.

It rewards visitors who come with time and curiosity. The Gallery of Francis I, the Ballroom, the Imperial Apartments and the gardens each tell a different story, and together they make one of the richest historic interiors in Europe. Its quiet forest setting, less crowded than that of Versailles, only adds to its charm.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

Why is Fontainebleau a UNESCO World Heritage Site?

UNESCO inscribed it in 1981 under criteria (ii) and (vi), recognising it as the place where Renaissance art and architecture were transmitted to France and where artists developed a distinctive style, and for its association with important events in French history.

What is the Gallery of Francis I?

It is a long gallery built in the 1530s and decorated by Rosso Fiorentino and others with frescoes, stucco and carved panelling. It is considered a masterpiece of Renaissance decoration and the starting point of the First School of Fontainebleau.

What happened at Fontainebleau in 1814?

Napoleon signed his first abdication in the palace on 6 April 1814 and on 20 April bade farewell to his Old Guard in the Cour du Cheval Blanc, afterwards called the Cour des Adieux, before leaving for exile on Elba.

How do I get to Fontainebleau from Paris?

Trains run from the Gare de Lyon to Fontainebleau-Avon in roughly 40 minutes, followed by a short bus ride or walk to the palace. Check current timetables and the palace’s opening days before travelling, as it is usually closed one day each week.

Is Fontainebleau worth visiting if I have seen Versailles?

Yes. Fontainebleau offers a different experience, with older Renaissance interiors, Napoleon’s apartments and a forest setting, and it is generally quieter. Many visitors find it more intimate and more varied than Versailles.
