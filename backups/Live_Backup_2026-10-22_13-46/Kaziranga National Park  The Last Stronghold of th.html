The Armoured Giants of the Floodplain

In the early morning, when mist hangs over the tall grass and the first sun colours the Brahmaputra floodplain in Assam, a dark shape detaches itself from the green. It is a great one-horned rhinoceros, an animal that looks as though it were assembled from plates of grey armour, with a single black horn and a skin folded into heavy panels. It grazes calmly, flicks its small ears and wades into a shallow pool, followed by a calf. It is hard to imagine, watching it, that its species came within a few animals of extinction a century ago.

Kaziranga National Park, in the north-eastern Indian state of Assam, is the reason this animal survives. The park holds about two-thirds of the world's Indian rhinoceroses, a population counted at more than 2,600 in the census of 2018, up from just over a thousand in the early 1980s. It also shelters tigers at some of the highest densities recorded anywhere, Asian elephants, wild water buffalo, swamp deer and hundreds of species of birds, all within a mosaic of grassland, wetland and woodland that is one of the last unmodified natural areas of the region.

UNESCO inscribed Kaziranga on the World Heritage List in 1985 under natural criteria (ix) and (x), recognising both the outstanding ecological processes of the Brahmaputra floodplain and its importance as a habitat for threatened species. The inscribed property covers 42,996 hectares, a strip of land about forty kilometres long and thirteen wide, running along the southern bank of the great river.

A Landscape Made by Water

Kaziranga is the creation of the Brahmaputra. The river, one of the great waterways of Asia, descends from the Himalaya through Tibet and arrives in Assam as a vast braided stream, depositing enormous quantities of silt on its floodplain. Over thousands of years, the river has built up a flat, fertile plain dotted with shallow lakes known as beels, channels and abandoned river courses, and every summer, during the monsoon, it spills over its banks and floods the land.

The flood and the fire shape the vegetation. Tall elephant grass, which can grow to more than three metres, covers more than two-thirds of the park, interspersed with patches of shorter grass, open woodland of cotton trees and Indian gooseberry, and wetlands full of water hyacinth and lilies. Each year, in the dry season, park managers burn some of the grassland to encourage fresh growth for the grazing animals, mimicking the natural cycle of fire and flood that has maintained the landscape for millennia.

The monsoon floods can cover most of the park for days, forcing the animals to flee to the highlands to the south, the Karbi Anglong hills, crossing the main highway in the process. The floods are essential for the health of the ecosystem, since they renew the soil, refill the wetlands and clear the vegetation, but they are also a danger, and in severe years large numbers of animals are lost.

A Duchess, a Tiger Hunt and a Park

The history of Kaziranga as a protected area begins in the early twentieth century. According to a story widely told in Assam, in 1904 Mary Curzon, the wife of the Viceroy of India, Lord Curzon, visited the area hoping to see the famous one-horned rhinoceros, but failed to find a single animal. Disturbed by this, she is said to have pressed her husband to take urgent measures to protect the dwindling species, and in 1905 the area was declared a proposed reserve forest of about 232 square kilometres.

Over the following decades, the protection of the area was gradually strengthened. It became a game reserve and then a wildlife sanctuary in 1950, and in 1954 the state of Assam passed a law imposing heavy penalties for killing rhinoceroses. In 1968 the state passed a National Park Act under which Kaziranga was designated a national park, a status confirmed by the central government in 1974, and further land was added to the park in later years.

The effort paid off. By the early twentieth century, the Indian rhinoceros had been hunted to near extinction across its range, with only a few hundred, perhaps far fewer, surviving in northern India and Nepal. Kaziranga, protected by forest guards and the remoteness of its swamps, became the species' principal refuge, and from there the rhinoceros population began to recover.

The Great One-Horned Rhinoceros

Rhinoceros unicornis, the Indian or greater one-horned rhinoceros, is the largest of the Asian rhinos, an animal that can weigh more than two tonnes and stand almost two metres at the shoulder. Its thick grey-brown hide falls in folds around the neck, shoulders and hips, giving it the appearance of a knight in armour, and its single horn, made of compressed keratin, may grow to half a metre in length. Despite its size, it can run at speed and swim well, and it spends much of the day wallowing in water or mud to keep cool.

Rhinos are mostly solitary, except for mothers with calves, and they feed on grass, aquatic plants, leaves and fruit. They mark their territories with dung heaps, called latrines, which are used by many animals over time, and the sight of an adult rhinoceros grazing in a pool, with egrets perched on its back, is among the most characteristic images of Kaziranga.

The greatest threat to the species has always been poaching for the horn, which is prized in some parts of Asia for use in traditional medicine and as a status symbol. Kaziranga has seen waves of poaching, and has responded with an intensive protection system, including armed forest guards, watchtowers, patrols on foot, boat and elephant, and the cooperation of local communities. The result is that the park remains the species' stronghold, though no one involved considers the battle over.

Tigers, Elephants and Buffalo

The rhinoceros is the flagship, but the park is home to a wider cast of animals, sometimes called its big five: the rhinoceros, the tiger, the Asian elephant, the wild water buffalo and the swamp deer. Kaziranga was declared a tiger reserve in 2006, and surveys have recorded some of the highest tiger densities in the world, although the cats are seldom seen in the tall grass, moving silently between cover and water.

Elephants move through the park in family herds, wandering between the grasslands and the forested hills, and they are an important part of the ecosystem, shaping the vegetation by browsing and trampling. The wild water buffalo, a massive and formidable animal with great crescent horns, is found here in the largest numbers of any protected area in the world, and its herds can often be seen wading through the shallow wetlands.

The swamp deer, or barasingha, a graceful animal with branched antlers, lives in the park's grasslands, along with hog deer, sambar, barking deer and wild boar. Leopards, sloth bears, jungle cats, hog badgers and a variety of smaller carnivores complete the community, and the rivers shelter otters, turtles and the Gangetic river dolphin. The abundance of life makes the park one of the richest wildlife areas in Asia.

Birds of the Wetlands

For birdwatchers, Kaziranga is a destination of the first rank. More than 300 species have been recorded, and the park is recognised by BirdLife International as an Important Bird Area. The wetlands attract great flocks of migratory waterfowl in winter, among them bar-headed geese, ruddy shelducks, northern pintails and gadwalls, which arrive from Central Asia and Siberia to escape the northern cold.

Resident species include several of great conservation importance. The greater adjutant stork, a huge, bald-headed scavenger and one of the rarest storks in the world, nests in the area, while the spot-billed pelican, the Pallas's fish eagle and the Bengal florican, a grassland bustard threatened across its range, are all present in the park. Kingfishers, bee-eaters, hornbills, parakeets and a host of smaller birds fill the woodlands.

The birds illustrate why the park's grassland and wetland mosaic is so valuable. Each species depends on a particular combination of conditions, and the maintenance of the floodplain's natural cycle of flood and fire, with its constantly shifting patchwork of habitats, is the key to preserving them all.

Floods, Roads and the Struggle Against Poaching

The park faces serious challenges. The annual floods, while essential, can be devastating, and exceptional years, such as those of 1988, 2012 and 2019, have killed hundreds of animals, including rhinos, as the water covered most of the park. The animals that escape to the hills face other dangers: they must cross a busy national highway that runs along the southern boundary, where vehicles injure and kill them, and authorities have introduced speed limits and protected crossing points to reduce the risk.

Poaching remains a persistent threat. Rhino horn commands a high price in illegal markets, and criminal networks have repeatedly targeted the park. The authorities have responded with strong measures: armed patrols, intelligence networks and legal protections that give forest guards wide powers. These have reduced losses significantly in recent years, though the approach has attracted criticism from human rights groups concerned about the treatment of local people.

Other pressures include the encroachment of invasive plants on the grassland, the erosion of the park's banks by the changing course of the river, and unregulated tourism development near the boundaries. The long-term survival of Kaziranga depends on vigilance, adaptability and the continued goodwill of its human neighbours.

Visiting Kaziranga

The park lies in the Golaghat, Nagaon and Sonitpur districts of Assam, about 190 kilometres east of Guwahati, a drive of around five hours, and some 100 kilometres from the nearest airport at Jorhat. The main tourist centre is the village of Kohora, near the park's Central Range, and there are other ranges at Bagori, Agoratoli and Burapahar, each offering a slightly different landscape.

The park is open from about November to April, and closed during the monsoon season when the floods cover the land. The best time to visit is from December to March, when the weather is cool and the grass has been cut or burned, making animals easier to see. The main way to see the wildlife is by open jeep safari along the park's tracks, accompanied by a guide and an armed forest guard, and early morning and late afternoon are the best times.

Visitors should book safaris and permits in advance, particularly in peak season, and follow the instructions of their guides at all times. Rhinos and elephants are wild animals and can be dangerous. Travellers should check current conditions, opening dates and any regional advisories before planning a visit.

Reflection

There is a patience in the way the rhinos of Kaziranga move that seems to belong to an earlier age of the earth. They graze, wallow and rest in the tall grass, indifferent to the jeeps and the cameras, much as their ancestors did before human beings had a name for them. That they are here at all is the work of a century of ordinary people: forest guards walking the swamps in the monsoon, officials writing laws, villagers deciding to tolerate the great animals in their fields. The park is a reminder that extinction is not inevitable, and that a landscape saved by many small decisions can still hold, on a misty morning, a living echo of the ice age.

✦ Conclusion

Kaziranga National Park is one of the greatest wildlife refuges in the world, and one of the clearest examples of what determined conservation can achieve. Its rhinoceros population, once reduced to a handful, now numbers in the thousands, and its tigers, elephants, buffalo and birds make it a living museum of the floodplain ecosystems of the Brahmaputra.

It is also a landscape under constant pressure from floods, poachers, roads and climate change. Its survival depends on continuing vigilance and on the support of the people of Assam, who have made it a symbol of their region. To watch a rhinoceros wade through a misty pool at dawn is to see what that effort has preserved.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

1. Where is Kaziranga National Park?

It is in the Indian state of Assam, in the districts of Golaghat, Nagaon and Sonitpur, on the southern bank of the Brahmaputra River, about 190 kilometres east of Guwahati.

2. Why is it a World Heritage Site?

It was inscribed in 1985 under natural criteria (ix) and (x) for its outstanding ecological processes and as the world's major stronghold of the Indian one-horned rhinoceros.

3. How many rhinos live in Kaziranga?

The 2018 census counted about 2,613, which is about two-thirds of the world's population of the species.

4. When is the park open?

Generally from November to April. It closes during the monsoon season from about May to October because of flooding.

5. How do I see the wildlife?

By jeep safari with a guide and armed forest guard on the park's tracks, early in the morning or late in the afternoon.
