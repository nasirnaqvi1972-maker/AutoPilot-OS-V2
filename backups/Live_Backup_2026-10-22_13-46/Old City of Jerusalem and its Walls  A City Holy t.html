A City That Belongs to the World’s Imagination

Few places on earth carry as much meaning as the Old City of Jerusalem. Within a walled area of about one square kilometre stand sites that are holy to billions of people: the Temple Mount and the Western Wall, the Church of the Holy Sepulchre, and the Dome of the Rock and Al-Aqsa Mosque on the Haram al-Sharif. The streets between them are narrow, stepped and ancient, filled with the sounds of bells, prayers and markets.

UNESCO inscribed the Old City of Jerusalem and its Walls on the World Heritage List in 1981, on the proposal of Jordan, and placed it on the List of World Heritage in Danger in 1982. It was recognised under criteria (ii), (iii) and (vi) for its outstanding spiritual and cultural importance and for the testimony it bears to successive civilisations. This post follows UNESCO’s designation and listing. The political status of Jerusalem is disputed and sensitive, and nothing here is intended as a position on sovereignty.

What is beyond dispute is the city’s extraordinary significance as a place of memory and devotion. For Jews it is the site of the biblical Temples and the focus of prayer and longing; for Christians it is the scene of the crucifixion and resurrection of Jesus; for Muslims it is the third holiest city, associated with the Prophet Muhammad’s Night Journey. This convergence of faiths in a tiny space is unique.

Jebus, David and the First Temple

The earliest settlement on the hill of Jerusalem lies south of the present Old City, on a narrow ridge above a spring, where traces of occupation go back to the Bronze Age. Egyptian texts of the second millennium BCE mention a city called Urushalim. According to the biblical account, King David captured the stronghold, then held by the Jebusites, around 1000 BCE and made it his capital, and his son Solomon built the First Temple on the hill to the north, now known as the Temple Mount.

The First Temple stood for several centuries, serving as the religious centre of the kingdom of Judah, until it was destroyed by the Babylonians in 586 BCE, who also deported much of the population. When the exiles returned, under Persian rule, a Second Temple was built, completed around 516 BCE. Jerusalem became a focus of Jewish identity and worship, a symbol that would remain central even in times of dispersion.

In the first century BCE, King Herod the Great transformed the city and the Temple Mount, constructing a vast platform and a magnificent rebuilt Temple, as well as palaces and fortifications. Much of his great enclosing wall, including the section known as the Western Wall, survives today, bearing witness to the scale of his ambition.

Rome, Aelia Capitolina and Christian Jerusalem

In 70 CE, following a Jewish revolt against Roman rule, the Romans besieged and destroyed Jerusalem and burned the Second Temple. After another revolt in 132 to 135 CE, the emperor Hadrian rebuilt the city as a Roman colony called Aelia Capitolina and barred Jews from entering. Its plan, with a north–south main street called the Cardo and a grid of streets, still shapes the layout of the Old City, and traces of its columns can be seen today.

Everything changed when the emperor Constantine adopted Christianity. In the early fourth century, he ordered the construction of a great church over the traditional site of the crucifixion and tomb of Jesus, and Jerusalem rapidly became a major destination for pilgrims. Churches, monasteries and hospices proliferated, and the city was filled with the sound of Christian liturgy in Greek, Armenian, Syriac and other languages.

The Byzantine period left a lasting mark. Mosaics, such as the famous sixth-century map of the Holy Land found at Madaba in Jordan, depict a city dominated by the Church of the Holy Sepulchre, and the traditions established in this era shaped Christian devotion for centuries to come.

Islamic Jerusalem — The Noble Sanctuary

In 637 or 638, Jerusalem was taken by Arab Muslim forces, and the city entered a new era. It became known as al-Quds, the Holy, and the Haram al-Sharif, the Noble Sanctuary, on the site of the former Temple, became its focus. At the end of the seventh century the Umayyad caliph Abd al-Malik built the Dome of the Rock over the exposed bedrock, which Muslim tradition associates with the Prophet’s ascent to heaven, and with the sacred rock that Jews and Christians also revere.

The Dome of the Rock is an extraordinary building: an octagonal shrine topped with a golden dome, covered inside with mosaics and outside with tiles, and encircled by inscriptions from the Qur’an. It is not a mosque but a shrine, and one of the oldest surviving Islamic monuments. Nearby stands the Al-Aqsa Mosque, whose present form dates largely from the eleventh century onward, and which Muslims regard as the third holiest mosque after those of Mecca and Medina.

Under successive Muslim dynasties, including the Abbasids, Fatimids, Ayyubids, Mamluks and Ottomans, the Old City was enriched with madrasas, hospices, fountains, markets and bathhouses. The Mamluk period in particular left a fine legacy of architecture in the Muslim Quarter, with elegant stone façades, arches and carved doorways that give the lanes their distinctive character.

The Holy Sepulchre, Crusaders and the Christian Quarter

The Church of the Holy Sepulchre is the most sacred place in Christianity. Constantine’s basilica, dedicated in 335, was damaged by Persian invaders and later destroyed in 1009 by the Fatimid caliph al-Hakim, and then rebuilt in the eleventh century. The Crusaders, who captured Jerusalem in 1099, created much of the church we see today, joining the sites of Calvary and the tomb under a single roof and consecrating the new building in 1149.

A Shared and Delicate Balance

The church is shared by several Christian communities, chiefly the Greek Orthodox, Armenian Apostolic and Roman Catholic churches, with smaller roles for the Coptic, Syriac and Ethiopian churches. Their rights and responsibilities are governed by a long-standing arrangement known as the Status Quo, which dates to the Ottoman period. A wooden ladder that stands on a ledge above the entrance, unmoved for generations, is a famous symbol of the delicate balance, and the keys to the church are held by Muslim families, a tradition dating back centuries. Around the church, the streets of the Christian Quarter, including the Via Dolorosa, with its stations marking Jesus’ path to crucifixion, draw pilgrims from around the world.

The Western Wall and the Jewish Quarter

For Jews, Jerusalem has been the centre of prayer and longing for nearly three thousand years. The Western Wall, a section of the retaining wall of Herod’s Temple Mount, is the holiest place where Jewish prayer is permitted today, and worshippers visit it at all hours, placing written prayers between the ancient stones. Nearby lies the Jewish Quarter, which has a long and complicated history, with a continuous Jewish presence in the Old City for much of the past millennium.

The Quarter’s synagogues, yeshivas and houses were heavily damaged in the conflicts of the twentieth century, and many were restored or rebuilt after 1967. Among them are the Hurva Synagogue, rebuilt in the twenty-first century on its nineteenth-century model, and the Sephardic synagogues, which preserve traditions of Jewish life in Spain and the Ottoman world. Archaeological excavations in the Quarter have also revealed remains of the Herodian and Byzantine city, including the Cardo.

The relationship of Jews to the Temple Mount is complex, because of religious and political sensitivities about access and worship. Rules and arrangements for visits to the Temple Mount have changed over time and remain contested, so visitors are advised to check current regulations carefully.

The Walls, the Gates and the Four Quarters

The walls that give the Old City its name were built between 1535 and 1542 by the Ottoman sultan Suleiman the Magnificent, on the foundations of earlier fortifications. They run for about four kilometres, with towers, battlements and eight gates, of which seven are open. Among them are the Jaffa Gate, the main entrance from the west, the elaborate Damascus Gate on the north and the Lions Gate on the east, while the sealed Golden Gate, on the Temple Mount side, is the subject of long-standing tradition and expectation.

Inside, the city is traditionally divided into four quarters: Muslim, Christian, Jewish and Armenian. The Armenian Quarter, the smallest, is centred on the Cathedral of Saint James and preserves a community that has lived in Jerusalem since the early centuries of Christianity. The Muslim Quarter, the largest and most densely populated, contains the main markets, with their sounds, colours and scents.

The Ottoman period also left its mark in the form of fountains, schools and public works, including the Sabil of Suleiman, and the Citadel at the Jaffa Gate, with its Tower of David, which incorporates stonework from many periods. Walking the ramparts, which can be done along portions of the walls, offers a view over the Old City’s varied rooftops, domes and minarets, and a good sense of how its parts fit together.

Reflection — Holiness and the Weight of Stone

It is impossible to stand in Jerusalem’s Old City and not feel the pressure of belief. In a few hundred metres, three great traditions have built their holiest places, each marked by walls, domes and stairways, each filled with people who consider this ground the centre of the world. The effect can be moving and unsettling, and it raises a question that no amount of history can answer: how do people share a place that matters so deeply to them all?

The Old City’s long history offers no simple lesson, but it does suggest something about the persistence of memory. Empires have risen and fallen, walls have been breached and rebuilt, and communities have come and gone, yet the stones continue to carry meaning. To walk its lanes with attention and respect, listening to the many voices that make up its past and present, is perhaps the most appropriate way to honour a city that belongs, in a sense, to everyone who has ever dreamed of it.

✦ Conclusion

The Old City of Jerusalem and its Walls is one of the most important historic places on earth: a compact city of stone in which the sacred geography of Judaism, Christianity and Islam is concentrated in a few hundred metres. Its monuments, from the Western Wall and the Dome of the Rock to the Church of the Holy Sepulchre and the Ottoman walls, record three thousand years of faith, conquest and creativity.

Its place on the List of World Heritage in Danger reflects the fragility of its heritage and the sensitivities that surround it. For visitors, it offers a profound experience, best approached with humility, curiosity and respect for all the communities for whom it is home and holy.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

Why is the Old City of Jerusalem a UNESCO World Heritage Site?

UNESCO inscribed it in 1981 under criteria (ii), (iii) and (vi), recognising its outstanding religious significance to Judaism, Christianity and Islam, and its exceptional testimony to successive civilisations. It was proposed by Jordan and has been on the List of World Heritage in Danger since 1982.

What does site proposed by Jordan mean?

The Old City was nominated to the World Heritage List by Jordan, which administered East Jerusalem at the time, and UNESCO continues to list it that way because of the city’s disputed status. This post follows UNESCO’s designation without taking a position on sovereignty.

What are the main holy sites in the Old City?

The principal sites are the Temple Mount or Haram al-Sharif, with the Dome of the Rock and Al-Aqsa Mosque; the Western Wall; and the Church of the Holy Sepulchre. They are sacred, respectively, to Muslims, Jews and Christians, though the Temple Mount is also holy to Jews.

What are the four quarters of the Old City?

The Old City is traditionally divided into the Muslim, Christian, Jewish and Armenian Quarters. These divisions reflect long-standing communities rather than strict boundaries, and the quarters differ in size and character.

What should visitors know before going?

Dress modestly, especially at religious sites, and respect prayer times and local customs. Access rules, particularly for the Temple Mount, can change, and security conditions may vary, so check current official travel advice and site regulations before and during your visit.

🧳 Visitor's Guide

Travel Advice First — Check your government’s current travel advisory and local conditions before and during your visit, as the security situation can change quickly

Getting There — Most visitors arrive via Tel Aviv’s Ben Gurion Airport and travel by road or train to Jerusalem; some come from Amman, Jordan, by land crossing

Best Time to Visit — Spring and autumn offer pleasant weather; summer is hot and winter can be cool and wet; major religious holidays bring large crowds

Dress and Etiquette — Wear modest clothing covering shoulders and knees; follow instructions at holy sites and respect prayer times

Temple Mount and Haram al-Sharif — Access for visitors is regulated and subject to change; check current times and rules, and be prepared for security checks

Church of the Holy Sepulchre — Open from early morning; expect queues at the Edicule, especially during holidays

Sabbath and Holidays — Many services and some sites are affected by Friday prayers, the Jewish Sabbath and religious holidays; plan accordingly

Walking the Ramparts — Sections of the walls can be walked for fine views; check opening times and tickets locally
