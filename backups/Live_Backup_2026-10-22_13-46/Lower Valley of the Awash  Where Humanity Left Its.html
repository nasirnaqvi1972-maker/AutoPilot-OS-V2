The Cradle in the Rift

There are places on the surface of the earth that matter because of what was built upon them, and there are places that matter because of what was left beneath them. The Lower Valley of the Awash belongs unequivocally to the second order. No cathedral spire rises here, no citadel wall climbs a ridge, no carved stair descends into a sacred pool. What the traveller meets instead is a vast, shimmering, mineral-coloured country of ochre cliffs, grey gravels and thorn scrub, a land so hot and so spare that it seems to have been stripped of everything except time. And yet it is in this unpromising terrain, in the lowlands of Ethiopia's Afar Region, that humanity has found some of the most eloquent documents of its own beginnings.

The Awash River, one of the great rivers of Ethiopia, rises on the high plateau west of the capital, tumbles down into the Rift Valley, and then runs north-eastward through a semi-arid basin, never reaching the sea. It ends instead in a chain of saline lakes near the border with Djibouti, swallowed by the desert that its own water briefly redeems. Along its course, over millions of years, the river and the lakes that preceded it deposited layer upon layer of silt, sand and volcanic ash. Those layers have since been tilted, broken and exposed by the restless tectonics of the rift, and within them lie the bones of creatures that walked here when the world was an utterly different place. UNESCO, when it inscribed the property in 1980, described the Awash valley as containing one of the most important groupings of palaeontological sites on the African continent.

To stand at the edge of one of these eroding gullies is to experience a peculiar vertigo. The slope under your boots is not simply earth; it is a calendar. The pale band at your knee may be three million years old, the dark horizon below it more than three and a half. A fragment of jaw glinting in the gravel might belong to an antelope, a pig, a monkey, or to a small upright ape who had not yet learned to chip a stone but who had already, in the most consequential gamble of her lineage, stood up on two legs. This is the landscape that gave the world Lucy, and with her a new and astonishing chapter in the story of what it means to be human.

A Valley Made by the Tearing of the Earth

The Afar Triple Junction

To understand why the bones are here, one must first understand why the valley is here at all. The Afar depression lies at one of the few places on the planet where the surface of the earth is visibly being torn apart. Three great fractures meet in this region, the Red Sea rift, the Gulf of Aden rift and the Main Ethiopian Rift, and the crust between them is stretching and thinning so steadily that geologists speak of the Afar as an ocean in the making. Over a span of many millions of years, as the African and Arabian plates drift apart, the floor of this basin has subsided far below the level of the surrounding highlands, creating a low, scalding bowl in which water, sediment and ash have gathered ever since.

This is not an incidental detail. The very forces that make the Afar one of the most inhospitable places on earth are the forces that made it one of the finest archives in the world. Volcanoes erupted repeatedly and covered the basin in layers of ash that can be dated with great precision, allowing scientists to fix the age of fossils sandwiched between them. Lakes and rivers meandered across the floor, drowning carcasses and burying them swiftly in fine sediment, which is precisely the condition under which bone survives. And finally the same faulting that deepened the basin later lifted and tilted these ancient deposits, so that erosion could slice through them and present the buried past to anyone who looked.

A River That Never Reaches the Sea

The Awash itself seems to know that it is a creature of paradox. Fed by the rains of the highlands, it carries life through a country that would otherwise be wholly arid, supporting riverine woodland, grassland and seasonal pasture. In the deep geological past the same valley held lakes and marshes, and the animals whose bones are now found there, early elephants, giraffe-like browsers, horses, crocodiles and swine, tell of a mosaic of woodland and open grassland very different from the bare expanse of today. Into that mosaic stepped our ancestors, and it was this mixture of trees and open ground, many researchers believe, that helped to shape the strange new way of moving that would eventually define our kind.

The Morning of 24 November 1974

Hadar and the Search for the Missing Ancestor

By the early 1970s the question of human origins was in a state of fertile uncertainty. The fossil record of the first four-footed, tree-dwelling apes and of the first undoubted members of our genus was reasonably well known, but the long interval between them was a nearly blank page. When an international team that included the American anthropologist Donald Johanson, the French geologist Maurice Taieb and the French palaeontologist Yves Coppens began working the dry badlands of Hadar, in the Afar, in the middle part of the Awash drainage, they were hunting for precisely that missing chapter. Systematic fieldwork at Hadar had opened in 1973, and the first season had already yielded a knee joint that seemed to belong to an upright walker.

On the morning of 24 November 1974, Johanson and a graduate student, Tom Gray, set out to survey a gully they had already visited. By Johanson's own account, they were about to turn back when a fragment of an arm bone caught his eye in the sunlight. Close beside it lay a piece of skull, a thigh bone, vertebrae, ribs and a lower jaw. Over the following weeks the team recovered fragments representing roughly forty per cent of a single skeleton, which UNESCO records as fifty-two pieces, an extraordinary degree of completeness for a creature of such antiquity. The skeleton, catalogued as A.L. 288-1, was that of a small adult female who had died roughly three million years ago. She stood about a metre tall and weighed perhaps thirty kilograms.

A Name from a Song

That evening, the camp celebrated, and a cassette of the Beatles was played again and again beneath the stars. The song was "Lucy in the Sky with Diamonds", and the skeleton had a name before the night was over. In Ethiopia she is known by a more resonant one, Dinknesh, an Amharic word often translated as "you are marvellous". The two names, one borrowed from a popular song of the twentieth century and the other from the language of the country that holds her, sit side by side like an emblem of the encounter between global science and local heritage that this landscape continues to embody.

What made Lucy so important was not merely her age, which has been estimated at about 3.2 million years, but the architecture of her body. The shape of her pelvis and the angle of her knee showed beyond reasonable doubt that she walked upright, while her long arms and curved fingers hinted that she was still at ease in the trees. Her small brain was no larger than a chimpanzee's. The discovery overturned a once-cherished assumption, that big brains had led the way to human-like walking, and replaced it with an altogether more surprising order of events: our ancestors stood up first, and thought about it afterwards. Lucy and her kin were later assigned to a new species, Australopithecus afarensis, named for the Afar itself.

Before Lucy and Beyond Her

Older Ancestors in the Middle Awash

Lucy is the celebrity of the valley, but she is far from alone in it. Upstream and nearby, in the Middle Awash study area, teams led by the American palaeoanthropologist Tim White and Ethiopian colleagues, among them Berhane Asfaw and Giday WoldeGabriel, have recovered the remains of a succession of hominids that stretch back far beyond her. Among them is the skeleton nicknamed Ardi, a female of the species Ardipithecus ramidus that lived about 4.4 million years ago. The first remains were found in the 1990s, but the detailed analysis of the skeleton was published only in 2009, after painstaking reconstruction of crushed and fragile bones. Ardi walked upright on the ground yet retained a grasping big toe for moving through branches, a combination that astonished researchers and suggested that the last common ancestor of humans and chimpanzees was a far less chimpanzee-like creature than had been supposed.

The Awash also preserves, in its broader basin, remains that tell of what came after. At Dikika, not far from Hadar, an international team led by the Ethiopian palaeoanthropologist Zeresenay Alemseged announced in 2006 the remarkably complete skeleton of a three-year-old Australopithecus afarensis child who lived about 3.3 million years ago. Popularly called Selam, a word meaning peace in several Ethiopian languages, she is one of the most intact fossils of an early hominid child ever recovered. At Gona, in the Afar, the archaeologist Sileshi Semaw and his colleagues reported in 1997 the discovery of stone tools about 2.5 million years old, among the earliest evidence anywhere for deliberate toolmaking. Taken together, the finds make the Awash one of the few places on earth where one can follow, within a single regional landscape, the long road from upright ape to tool-making forebear.

Reading the Rock

The method by which scholars have read this record is almost as remarkable as the record itself. Palaeoanthropologists, geologists, dating specialists and specialists in ancient plant and animal life work together, mapping each stratum, dating the volcanic ash within it, and cataloguing every fragment of fossil wood, pollen and tooth. From the bones of the pigs, antelope and monkeys found alongside Lucy and her kin, they reconstruct the climate and vegetation of each epoch. From the wear on teeth they deduce diet. From the shape of a pelvis or the curve of a toe bone they read the gait of a creature that has been dust for millennia. A fossil, in this discipline, is never an object alone. It is the centre of an argument about an entire world.

The Afar and the Land of Salt

No account of this valley is complete without the people who have lived in it for centuries. The Afar are a pastoralist people who have herded camels, goats, sheep and cattle across these lowlands for countless generations, moving with the seasons between the river and the dry plains. Their world is one of formidable endurance. They have long worked the salt flats of the Danakil to the north, cutting slabs of salt from the desert crust and carrying them by camel caravan to the highlands, a trade that has linked the Afar lowlands to the great plateau kingdoms of Ethiopia, including the realm of the rock-hewn churches described in our essay on Lalibela, Ethiopia.

It is the Afar who know this terrain best, and it is Afar guides who have long accompanied scientists across it, reading the ground with an intuition that no textbook can supply. The relationship between researchers and the community has not always been simple. Heritage in the Awash is not an abstraction; it lies in land that is used for grazing, that is crossed by migration routes, and that is claimed through kinship and custom. Any plan for the future of the valley must therefore begin with the recognition that it is a living landscape as well as an ancient one, and that the people who inhabit it are neither obstacles nor spectators but the true custodians of the ground.

What Inscription Recognised, and What It Left Undone

Three Criteria for an Ancient Landscape

When the World Heritage Committee inscribed the Lower Valley of the Awash in 1980, it did so under three of the cultural criteria. Under criterion (ii), the committee recognised that the hominid and animal fossils found in the valley bear witness to developments that have modified views of the history of humankind as a whole. Under criterion (iii), it noted that the palaeo-anthropological remains, dating back almost four million years, are of exceptional antiquity. Under criterion (iv), it observed that the human vestiges excavated there, more than three million years old, provide an exceptional record that contributes to our understanding of human development. The property is classed as cultural, a reminder that, for the Convention, the story of our species is itself a form of heritage.

It is a sobering irony that a site so important to the story of our origins remains among the more modestly resourced on the World Heritage List. UNESCO's own record notes that the boundaries of the property are not clearly defined, that no buffer zone has been provided, and that the fossils are vulnerable to collectors. Because bones in the Awash lie on the surface of the badlands, exposed by erosion and easily removed, a single unscrupulous visitor can erase what millions of years left behind. Without precise boundaries, a management plan and a staffed presence, enforcement is difficult, particularly in a region of great distances and few roads. Observers and heritage specialists have repeatedly urged the creation of a clear delimitation, a buffer zone and an agreed management framework developed together with local communities.

A Shared Responsibility

The challenge is partly one of scale. The Awash drainage covers a vast territory, and the fossil localities are scattered across it rather than gathered in a single compound. A nominal 150 square kilometres, the figure given by UNESCO, cannot encompass the full geography of the research that has made the valley famous, which ranges across several study areas. The question of what exactly is protected, and by whom, is thus both a legal and a philosophical one. In a place where the monument is a landscape, conservation means safeguarding not a wall but a relationship, between earth, water, people and the scientists who come to read what the earth has kept.

Lucy's Journeys, and the Ethics of Display

The fame of Lucy has carried her far beyond the Afar. In the late 2000s, her original bones were sent on a long exhibition tour of museums in the United States, an episode that stirred considerable debate among scientists and heritage specialists in Ethiopia and elsewhere. Supporters argued that the tour introduced millions of people to the story of human origins and brought international attention, and funds, to Ethiopian science. Critics warned that the fossil was fragile, irreplaceable and best conserved at home. The disagreement has not been forgotten, and it has left a lasting mark on how Ethiopia and its partners think about the movement of national heritage.

Today the original skeleton is kept in secure storage at the National Museum of Ethiopia in Addis Ababa, while a cast is displayed to the public. The choice reflects a broader principle that governs the care of the most precious fossils, that the original should be studied and preserved with great care while replicas carry the story to audiences. For the traveller, the practical consequence is that the first encounter with Lucy usually takes place in the capital, in the cool galleries of the museum, before any journey to the valley itself. There, among casts and reconstructions, one can begin to grasp what the scientists found, and prepare for the dust and heat of the rift.

Reflection

It is difficult to leave the Awash without a sense of humility. The monuments we usually visit on our travels are the work of kings and priests, of masons and architects, of people whose names, or at least whose dynasties, we can sometimes recite. Here, the principal characters have no names. They built nothing that survives and left no writing. Their legacy is a few bones in a dry gully, and yet that legacy is, in a real sense, the preface to every monument ever raised. The tombs of Egyptian pharaohs, the temples of Angkor and the stone enclosures of Great Zimbabwe all rest, ultimately, upon the fact that somewhere in Africa a small ape once stood upright and began a journey that would end in cathedrals and cities.

There is something quietly radical in the idea that the oldest heritage of all is shared by everyone. Whatever our nation, language or faith, the lineage that led to Lucy is a lineage that leads to each of us, and it began on this continent. The Lower Valley of the Awash is therefore not simply an Ethiopian treasure or an African one; it is the common inheritance of the species, held in trust by the Afar people and the Ethiopian state, and watched over, however imperfectly, by the international community. To visit it, in whatever form, whether in the dust of Hadar or in the hush of the museum in Addis, is to pay a visit to our own deepest family home.

Although its fame rests on the great wildebeest migration, the Serengeti lies close to Olduvai Gorge and the Laetoli footprints, where other chapters of the early human story were uncovered, and the essay explores a living African ecosystem that echoes the grassland mosaics in which Lucy once walked. ✦ Conclusion

The Lower Valley of the Awash asks us to look at heritage from the longest possible perspective. It is not a place of grand architecture or of obvious beauty, but of deep time, a landscape in which the earth itself acts as a witness. In the fossils of Hadar and the wider Awash basin lies a record of the moment when our lineage began to walk, to make tools and to move toward the human. The inscription of the valley in 1980 gave formal recognition to what palaeontologists had already understood, that this stretch of the Afar Rift is among the most important places in the world for understanding who we are.

Yet recognition is only the beginning of stewardship. The fragility of surface fossils, the absence of a defined buffer zone and the vastness of the terrain all remind us that the ancient past can vanish in an afternoon. Protecting it will require patient collaboration between Ethiopian institutions, international scientists and, above all, the Afar communities whose homeland this is. If that collaboration succeeds, the bones of the Awash will go on speaking, season after season, to every generation willing to listen to what the rift remembers.

✦ Frequently Asked Questions

1. Why is the Lower Valley of the Awash a UNESCO World Heritage Site?

It was inscribed in 1980 under criteria (ii), (iii) and (iv) because it holds one of the most important groupings of palaeontological sites in Africa. Its hominid fossils, the oldest of them dating back at least four million years, provide an exceptional record of human evolution, including the famous Lucy skeleton found in 1974.

2. Who was Lucy and where was she found?

Lucy, known in Amharic as Dinknesh, is a partial skeleton of an Australopithecus afarensis female who lived about 3.2 million years ago. She was found on 24 November 1974 at Hadar in Ethiopia's Afar Region by a team including Donald Johanson, and about forty per cent of her skeleton was recovered.

3. Can tourists visit the Lower Valley of the Awash?

Access is limited. The area is remote, has few facilities and lies in the hot Afar lowlands, and active research areas are restricted. Most visitors see Lucy and other Awash fossils at the National Museum of Ethiopia in Addis Ababa, where a replica is displayed. Anyone planning a field visit should arrange permissions and experienced local guides and check current travel advice for the Afar Region.

4. How big is the World Heritage property?

UNESCO states that the property covers an area of around 150 square kilometres. No buffer zone has been defined, and UNESCO has noted that the boundaries need clarification, which is one of the principal conservation concerns for the site.

5. What is the difference between the Lower Awash, the Middle Awash and Awash National Park?

The Awash River valley is traditionally divided into upper, middle and lower parts. The World Heritage property is the Lower Valley of the Awash, whereas many well-known hominid finds, such as Ardi, come from the Middle Awash research area, and Awash National Park is a separate protected area managed for wildlife. They lie within the same river system, and the scientific story links them closely, but they are distinct designations.
