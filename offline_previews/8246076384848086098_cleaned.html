The First Park on Earth

On the first day of March 1872, President Ulysses S. Grant signed into law the Yellowstone National Park Protection Act, setting aside approximately two million acres of the Wyoming, Montana, and Idaho territories as a public park and pleasuring ground for the benefit and enjoyment of the people. It was the first act of its kind in the history of the world — the first time any government had reserved a large tract of wild land not for military, agricultural, or commercial purposes but for the perpetual enjoyment of the public and the preservation of its natural wonders for future generations. The concept of the national park was born in Yellowstone, and it has since been adopted by virtually every nation on Earth.

The land that was set aside was remarkable in ways that justified the unprecedented act of preservation. Yellowstone sits atop one of the largest active volcanic systems on Earth — a supervolcano whose caldera, formed by a catastrophic eruption approximately 640,000 years ago, is so large that it was not recognised as a volcanic caldera until the development of aerial photography in the twentieth century. The heat of this volcanic system drives the most extraordinary concentration of geothermal features on Earth: more than 10,000 geothermal features, including approximately 500 geysers — roughly half of all the geysers in the world — along with hot springs, mud pots, fumaroles, and travertine terraces of extraordinary beauty and variety.

UNESCO inscribed Yellowstone on the World Heritage List in 1978 — in the very first year of the List's operation, among the first eight sites ever inscribed — under all four natural criteria, recognising it as a site of outstanding natural beauty, outstanding geological significance, outstanding ecological importance, and outstanding value for the conservation of biological diversity. It is one of the most comprehensively significant natural World Heritage Sites on the List. Readers who have explored our post on Tongariro National Park, New Zealand will recognise the combination of active volcanic landscape and outstanding natural beauty — but Yellowstone operates at a scale and a geological intensity that is in a category of its own.

The Supervolcano — Fire Beneath the Surface

The geological story of Yellowstone begins with a hotspot — a fixed point in the Earth's mantle where an unusually concentrated plume of heat rises toward the surface, melting the overlying rock into magma and driving volcanic activity of exceptional intensity. The North American tectonic plate has been moving slowly southwestward over this hotspot for approximately 17 million years, and the track of the hotspot's passage across the continent is marked by a chain of ancient caldera systems stretching across southern Idaho — the Snake River Plain — toward the current position of Yellowstone.

At Yellowstone itself, the hotspot has produced three catastrophic caldera-forming eruptions in the past 2.1 million years — eruptions of a scale so far beyond anything in recorded human history that they are almost impossible to conceptualise. The most recent, approximately 640,000 years ago, ejected approximately 1,000 cubic kilometres of material — enough to bury the entire state of Texas under a metre of ash — and collapsed the ground above the emptied magma chamber into the 45 by 85 kilometre caldera that now underlies much of the central portion of the park. The Yellowstone caldera is not dormant. It is restless: the ground surface within the caldera has risen and fallen by as much as a metre over the past century in response to the movement of magma and hydrothermal fluids beneath, and the seismic monitoring system records between 1,000 and 3,000 small earthquakes in the park each year.

The magma chamber that drives all this activity lies between 5 and 15 kilometres beneath the surface — close enough to heat the groundwater that percolates through the fractured rock above it to temperatures far above boiling, and to maintain the hydrothermal system that produces Yellowstone's extraordinary geothermal features.

The Geysers — Half the World's Supply

Yellowstone contains approximately 500 geysers — roughly half of all the geysers on Earth — concentrated in a series of geyser basins distributed across the central and western portions of the park. A geyser is a specific type of hot spring in which the geometry of the underground plumbing system creates the conditions for periodic eruption: water heated to boiling deep underground is prevented from boiling by the pressure of the water above it, until a slight reduction in pressure — caused by the natural drainage of the system — triggers a chain reaction in which the superheated water flashes to steam and erupts through the surface vent in a column of water and steam that can reach heights of tens or even hundreds of metres.

Old Faithful — erupting approximately every 90 minutes to a height of between 30 and 55 metres, its performance so regular that park rangers can predict its next eruption to within 10 minutes — is the most famous geyser in the world, and the centre of the Upper Geyser Basin, which contains the largest concentration of geysers anywhere on Earth. The Upper Geyser Basin alone contains approximately 150 geysers, including Castle, Grand, Riverside, and Beehive — each with its own eruption pattern, its own personality, and its own community of devoted observers who track its behaviour across seasons and years.

The Norris Geyser Basin is the hottest and most geologically dynamic geyser basin in the park — a landscape of hissing fumaroles, bubbling mud pots, and geysers whose behaviour changes from year to year in response to the seismic activity and hydrothermal dynamics of the system beneath them. Steamboat Geyser at Norris is the world's tallest active geyser, capable of erupting to a height of more than 90 metres in its major eruptions — events that occurred rarely for many decades but have become more frequent in recent years, for reasons that geologists are still working to understand.

The Wildlife — America's Serengeti

Yellowstone is the biological heart of the Greater Yellowstone Ecosystem — approximately 18 million hectares of protected and semi-protected land in Wyoming, Montana, and Idaho that together constitute the largest and most intact temperate ecosystem on Earth. Within and around the park, populations of large mammals that have been extirpated from most of their former range elsewhere in the contiguous United States survive in numbers and in ecological relationships that approximate the conditions of pre-European America.

The reintroduction of grey wolves to Yellowstone in 1995 — after an absence of more than seventy years, following their deliberate extermination from the region in the early twentieth century — has become one of the most studied and most celebrated experiments in ecological restoration in the history of conservation biology. The wolves' return produced effects that cascaded through the entire ecosystem in ways that biologists had predicted but whose full extent surprised even the most optimistic advocates of reintroduction. The wolves suppressed the elk populations that had been overgrazing the valley bottoms; the recovering vegetation stabilised the riverbanks; the stabilised rivers created new habitat for beaver, otter, and waterfowl; the whole system shifted toward a richer, more diverse, more resilient state. The Yellowstone wolf reintroduction is now the standard example used in ecology textbooks to explain the concept of the trophic cascade — the way in which the presence or absence of a top predator ripples through an entire food web.

Today Yellowstone supports populations of grey wolf, grizzly bear, American black bear, bison, elk, pronghorn antelope, mule deer, bighorn sheep, mountain goat, mountain lion, wolverine, lynx, river otter, and beaver, along with more than 300 species of birds. The bison herd — approximately 4,500 to 5,500 animals — is the largest wild bison population on public land in the United States, descended from the small remnant herd that survived the catastrophic commercial slaughter of the nineteenth century.

Readers who have explored our post on Serengeti National Park, Tanzania will recognise the parallel: both Yellowstone and the Serengeti are functioning ecosystems of a scale and integrity that has almost entirely disappeared from the rest of the world, both are anchored by their large predator and prey communities, and both demonstrate, in the most direct possible way, what the natural world looks like when it is allowed to function on its own terms.

The Colours of Yellowstone — Thermophiles and the Rainbow Landscape

One of the most visually extraordinary aspects of Yellowstone — and one of the least expected by first-time visitors — is its colour. The hot springs and geyser basins of Yellowstone are not the grey and white landscape of steam and bare rock that the word geothermal might suggest. They are landscapes of vivid, saturated colour: the deep blue of the superheated centre of the Grand Prismatic Spring, transitioning through turquoise and green to the brilliant orange and yellow of its outer margins; the blood-red and ochre of the bacterial mats along the outflow channels of the hot springs; the pale green and yellow of the Morning Glory Pool, whose colours have changed over the decades as visitors throwing coins into the spring have partially blocked its vent and lowered its temperature, allowing different communities of microorganisms to establish themselves.

The colours are produced by communities of extremophilic microorganisms — bacteria and archaea that have evolved to live in conditions of extreme heat, high acidity, or high alkalinity that would be instantly lethal to most other forms of life. Different species and communities of these organisms thrive at different temperatures, producing the distinctive colour zonation visible around the margins of many Yellowstone hot springs. The study of these organisms has practical as well as scientific significance: the heat-tolerant enzyme Taq polymerase, isolated from the Yellowstone bacterium Thermus aquaticus in the 1960s, is the basis of the polymerase chain reaction (PCR) technique used in molecular biology, forensic science, and medical diagnostics — making the hot springs of Yellowstone the direct origin of one of the most important biotechnological tools of the modern age.

The Mammoth Hot Springs — Travertine Terraces

At the northern end of Yellowstone, near the park's main administrative centre at Mammoth Hot Springs Village, the geothermal system produces a landscape of a completely different character from the geyser basins of the central park. The Mammoth Hot Springs terraces are formed not from silica-rich water erupting through volcanic rock, but from calcium carbonate-rich water rising through limestone — depositing travertine at a rate of approximately two tonnes per day and building the extraordinary stepped terrace formations that are among the most visually dramatic features in the park.

The Mammoth terraces are not static. The hot springs that feed them shift their positions constantly — a vent that was active and building a terrace last year may be dry and cooling this year, its travertine already beginning to grey and crumble, while a new vent a hundred metres away is beginning to build a new formation. The landscape of Mammoth Hot Springs is in continuous, visible flux — a geological process operating at a timescale accessible to direct human observation, the earth visibly making and unmaking itself before the visitor's eyes.

Reflection — The Idea That Changed the World

Yellowstone is, among other things, the origin of one of the most important ideas in the history of human civilisation's relationship with the natural world: the idea that wild land has value that is not reducible to its commercial or agricultural potential, and that this value is sufficient justification for protecting it in perpetuity from the forces of development and exploitation. This idea — embodied in the Yellowstone National Park Protection Act of 1872 — has since given rise to more than 6,500 national parks and protected areas in more than 100 countries, covering approximately 15 percent of the Earth's land surface.

The park itself — its geysers, its wildlife, its extraordinary geological landscape — is sufficient justification for the UNESCO inscription of 1978. But Yellowstone's deeper significance is the idea it represents: that some things are worth protecting not because they are useful but because they are beautiful, because they are irreplaceable, and because the experience of encountering them in their full wildness is something that every generation deserves the opportunity to have. Yellowstone gave the world that idea. The world has been acting on it — imperfectly, inadequately, but persistently — ever since.

Conclusion

Yellowstone is the beginning of an idea and the proof of its value simultaneously. It is where the world first decided that wild nature deserved protection for its own sake — and it is the most spectacular demonstration available anywhere on Earth of why that decision was right. Its geysers, its supervolcano, its ancient forests, its extraordinary wildlife, and the ecological restoration made possible by the return of the wolf together constitute a natural heritage of such variety and such significance that the UNESCO inscription under all four natural criteria seems, if anything, an understatement.

To visit Yellowstone — to stand beside Old Faithful as it erupts, to watch the morning light on the Grand Prismatic Spring, to see a wolf pack moving across the Lamar Valley in the early morning, to hear the bugling of elk in the autumn rut echoing across the caldera — is to encounter the natural world at its most generous, most dramatic, and most instructive. It is to understand, in the most direct possible way, what the world looked like before human beings began to remake it in their own image, and what it could look like again if enough people decide that it should.
