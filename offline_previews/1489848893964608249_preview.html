Situated in the western reaches of the Indian Ocean, the Aldabra Atoll stands as one of the world's premier natural laboratories for the study of island biogeography and evolutionary ecology. Comprising four principal coral limestone islands that encircle a vast, shallow lagoon and an outer fringing reef, Aldabra's physical layout is the product of dynamic geomorphological processes operating across deep time (UNESCO/CPE; Criterion vii). For historians and historical ecologists examining the maritime networks of the Indian Ocean, Aldabra represents an exceptional case study: an isolated insular space that largely escaped the intensive anthropogenic transformations that reshaped other regional landmasses.

Geological evidence indicates that the surface topography of Aldabra is primarily composed of ancient coral reef formations dating back approximately 125,000 years, which have been repeatedly forced above sea level by tectonic activity (Criterion ix). This historical emergence created a rugged, karstic landscape marked by jagged coral limestone. Scholarly interpretations of the atoll's strata emphasize that its morphological complexity—characterized by narrow passes, islets, and marine mudflats—directly enabled the development of discrete ecological niches. These physical barriers and opportunities drove high rates of speciation and endemism among the colonizing flora and fauna.

One of the most striking historical and ecological anomalies of Aldabra is its terrestrial food chain, which is uniquely dominated by an herbivore: the Aldabra giant tortoise (Aldabrachelys gigantea). Scholarly consensus identifies the atoll as the last remaining natural refuge for Indian Ocean giant tortoises, a life form once prevalent across neighboring island groups before historical human exploitation drove them to extinction elsewhere (Criterion x). Today, population estimates place the atoll's tortoise inhabitants at upwards of 100,000 to 152,000 individuals (UNESCO/CPE; Criterion x). Evolutionary biologists note that the native vegetation—including specific grasses and shrubs—has co-evolved alongside the grazing pressures of the tortoises, creating an interdependent landscape forged entirely without significant early human agricultural intervention (Criterion ix).

Beyond its chelonian populations, Aldabra functions as a crucial archive of Indian Ocean biodiversity. The atoll supports over 400 endemic species and subspecies across vertebrate, invertebrate, and plant classifications (Criterion x). Among its avian inhabitants is the White-throated Rail, recognized as the last surviving flightless bird species in the Western Indian Ocean, alongside localized taxa such as the Aldabra Brush Warbler and the Aldabra Drongo (Criterion x). Furthermore, the surrounding marine environment and pristine reefs maintain globally significant breeding rookeries for endangered green turtles and critically endangered hawksbill turtles, alongside massive oceanic seabird colonies (Criterion x).

While Aldabra's extreme geographical remoteness historically served as a barrier against widespread human interference, contemporary management relies on active institutional intervention. The property is legally protected and administered by the Seychelles Islands Foundation, utilizing structured management plans to balance scientific research with rigorous limitations on tourism (Protection and management requirements). Nevertheless, archaeological and ecological assessments stress that the atoll's pristine integrity faces modern vulnerabilities, notably the compounding pressures of global climate change, invasive alien species, and potential regional maritime hazards such as oil spills (Protection and management requirements).
