Geographic and Chronological Scope: The historic centres of Berat and Gjirokastra are located in central and southern Albania (the Drinos river valley), respectively. They represent a continuum of settlement stretching from antiquity (with Berat’s origins dating to the 4th century BC) through the Byzantine era and into the Ottoman period, which formally began in the region in 1417.

Architectural Typologies:

  • Berat: Characterized by a fortified citadel (the Kala, largely 13th-century), surrounding Byzantine churches (mostly 13th-century with wall paintings and icons), Ottoman-era mosques, Sufi dervish lodges (tekkes) from the 18th century, and tiered, light-filled merchant and artisan houses predominantly from the late 18th and 19th centuries.
  • Gjirokastra: Structured around a 13th-century citadel and built primarily by major landowners tied to central Ottoman authority. It features distinctive tower-houses (kullë) developed from the 17th century onward, with sophisticated examples from the early 19th century. It also includes an 18th-century bazaar, a mosque, and two churches.

Socio-Religious Coexistence: Both sites demonstrate a long-standing pattern of multi-ethnic and multi-religious coexistence. Berat, in particular, exhibits a historical framework where Orthodox Christian traditions continued their spiritual development alongside Islamic traditions influenced by the Ottoman period.

UNESCO Criteria: Inscribed under Criterion (iii) for bearing testimony to diverse, vanishing Balkan urban societies and varying socio-political structures (landowner-dominated Gjirokastra versus merchant/craftsman-driven Berat), and Criterion (iv) for exemplary vernacular architecture of the Classical Ottoman period built upon medieval foundations with religious pluralism.

The provided source texts lack granular archaeological stratigraphic data for the pre-Ottoman phases of both cities, particularly regarding the transition periods from late antiquity through the early medieval Slavic and Byzantine phases. Furthermore, while the texts mention illegal construction impacts in the late 1990s, they do not provide detailed documentation of specific archaeological losses or structural compromises sustained during those periods. The precise internal chronologies of individual vernacular housing transformations outside of broad stylistic dating (17th to 19th centuries) are also left unspecified.

Title: Echoes of the Balkans: Unlocking the Ottoman and Medieval Landscapes of Berat and Gjirokastra
I. Introduction: Geographic placement in Albania, dual inscription as UNESCO World Heritage sites, and overarching significance as living Ottoman-Balkan urban landscapes.
II. Deep Time and Stratigraphy: Examining the ancient and medieval roots—Berat's 4th-century BC origins and 13th-century Kala.
III. Divergent Urban Models: Contrasting the merchant-driven, open civic layout of Berat with the elite, landowner-centric, fortified citadel framework of Gjirokastra.
IV. Architectural Vernacular: The evolution of the kullë in Gjirokastra versus the terraced, light-capturing merchant homes of Berat.
V. Religious and Cultural Syncretism: The coexistence of Byzantine heritage, Ottoman mosques, and Sufi tekkes.
VI. Conclusion: Preservation challenges, integrity, and the ongoing historical value of these continuous settlements.

For scholars of global history and regional Balkan studies, the historic centres of Berat and Gjirokastra offer a remarkably intact window into centuries of urban evolution, cross-cultural exchange, and vernacular architecture. Designated collectively as UNESCO World Heritage sites, these two Albanian towns transcend simple architectural preservation; they serve as complex historical documents inscribed in stone, timber, and terraced earth.

While frequently celebrated for their Ottoman-era characteristics, neither Berat nor Gjirokastra is a monolith of a single historical epoch. Berat, situated in central Albania, features a monumental fortification locally known as the Kala. Archaeological and structural evidence indicates that while the primary fabric of the citadel was constructed in the 13th century, its foundational origins reach back to the 4th century BC. Within this defensive enclosure lies a dense concentration of Byzantine architecture, most notably 13th-century churches that preserve remnants of early wall paintings and liturgical art. Similarly, Gjirokastra—anchored in the Drinos river valley of southern Albania—centres around a 13th-century citadel that anchors the surrounding urban topography.

A critical analytical contribution of these sites to regional history is how their urban planning reflects distinct socio-economic structures. According to scholarly evaluations codified under UNESCO Criterion (iii), Gjirokastra and Berat represent two divergent paths of Balkan urban society:

Gjirokastra developed under the patronage of major landowners whose socio-political interests were directly aligned with the central Ottoman administration. The resulting urban morphology is tightly knit around the citadel, featuring authoritative, defensive architecture designed to project elite power.

Berat, by contrast, bears the physical imprint of a more independent civic life driven by craftsmen and merchants. Its open yet fortified urban layout reflects a decentralized economic vitality where trade and artisan guilds shaped the spatial distribution of neighbourhoods.

The architectural vocabulary of both towns highlights sophisticated vernacular engineering adapted to regional topographies and climate. In Gjirokastra, the dominant residential typology is the kullë (tower-house), a fortified dwelling style that emerged in the 17th century and reached peak complexity in the early 19th century. A classic kullë typically features an elevated ground floor, a heavily insulated winter living level, and an open, highly decorated summer level designed to capture natural light and airflow, often adorned with floral wall motifs for guest quarters.

In Berat, the domestic architecture responds differently to the sloping terrain. Houses are tiered along the hillsides in predominantly horizontal layouts designed to maximize interior daylight. Constructed largely during the late 18th and 19th centuries, Berat’s homes emphasize expansive window rows facing the valley, creating the iconic hillside aesthetic of "a city of a thousand windows."

Beyond commerce and elite governance, both towns provide tangible evidence of long-term religious and cultural coexistence. Berat stands out as a landscape where Orthodox Christian traditions maintained their spiritual and cultural continuity—evidenced by numerous Byzantine-era churches—alongside the introduction of Islamic institutions following the Ottoman conquest in 1417. This religious landscape also accommodated mystical traditions; historical records and physical structures note the presence of houses dedicated to religious brotherhoods, including 18th-century Sufi tekkes.

Ultimately, Berat and Gjirokastra remain globally significant not merely as relics of the Classical Ottoman period, but as continuous human habitats where ancient, medieval, and Islamic influences successfully converged and endured.
